## Commentary

**Self-Referential Simulation as a Candidate Causal Specification**

Seth (2025) frames the central question precisely: what causal architecture must a system instantiate for consciousness? His argument that current AI systems are not conscious is well-taken — not because they lack biological matter, but because they lack the right kind of causal organisation. Seth goes further than crude substrate chauvinism: he allows non-biological consciousness in principle if the right causal powers are instantiated, while arguing that current approaches fail to specify what those powers are. The Four-Model Theory (FMT; Gruber, 2015, 2026) proposes a candidate specification.

FMT identifies consciousness with a self-referential simulation generated by two kinds of models running in parallel — world-directed and self-directed — each with an implicit (learned, non-phenomenal) and an explicit (simulated, phenomenal) mode. The critical architectural property is self-referential closure: the system's model of the world includes a model of itself modelling the world, collapsing the inside/outside distinction that the Hard Problem presupposes. FMT does not claim to solve the Hard Problem — it proposes self-referential closure as constitutive of phenomenal character, a foundational commitment analogous to those every theory of consciousness requires (Seth & Bayne, 2022). The question is whether this commitment generates better predictions than the alternatives.

Architecture alone is insufficient. FMT imposes a dual threshold: the substrate must operate at dynamical criticality (Wolfram Class 4), AND must instantiate the four-model self-referential architecture. Criticality is necessary for the computation — it enables the substrate to sustain coherent simulation — but it does not by itself produce consciousness. An insect nervous system may operate near criticality without generating the rich self-model that human consciousness requires. The level of consciousness depends on the architecture; criticality is the substrate condition that makes the architecture computationally viable. This was derived in 2015 from computational universality requirements, and recent empirical work confirms that criticality is load-bearing: Algom and Shriki (2026) review 140+ datasets, Tucker, Luu and Friston (2025) ground it in excitatory–inhibitory balance, Xu et al. (2024) show sleep restores it. These findings confirm a necessary condition shared across criticality-based frameworks, not FMT uniquely — but FMT specifies what criticality is necessary *for*.

A specification earns its keep by generating testable consequences that competing frameworks do not. FMT's architecture does this. Because the explicit self-model is a running simulation with variable permeability to implicit processes, disruptions to that permeability produce specific, directional effects. For instance, the architecture predicts that global permeability increases (as occur under psychedelics) should partially compensate for local self-model blockages such as anosognosia — a consequence that follows from the inter-model information flow structure rather than from predictive processing per se. Similarly, because the explicit self-model requires continuous input to maintain coherent content, destabilising that input (via psychedelics, sensory deprivation, or meditation) should make identity content a function of whatever input dominates — ego dissolution becomes controllable rather than random. These consequences illustrate the kind of empirical specificity the architecture provides; a fuller catalogue, including predictions for dissociative identity disorder and lucid dream onset, appears in Gruber (2026, Sections 8.2–8.5). The point for the present commentary is not the individual predictions but the architectural property they share: each follows from the self-referential structure and the permeability mechanism, not from domain-specific stipulation.

The implicit/explicit distinction also generates confirmed consequences. Katlowitz et al. (2026) recorded hippocampal neurons during propofol anesthesia and found active semantic parsing, syntactic category discrimination, and representational plasticity — with zero conscious recall. FMT predicts exactly this: propofol suppresses explicit models (the simulation) while leaving implicit models (substrate-level knowledge) structurally intact. The hippocampus continues doing implicit-world-model work in the dark. IIT and GNW, which tie sophisticated processing to consciousness thresholds, have difficulty accommodating this finding.

This specification also clarifies the status of current AI systems — Seth's central concern. Large language models fail not because they lack biological matter but because they lack the architecture: no persistent implicit models, no self-referential closure, and no substrate dynamics near criticality. FMT can say precisely *what would need to change*: persistent world- and self-models, self-referential closure between them, and a substrate operating at the Class 4 boundary. This is not "any system computing the right function" — it is a named architecture with named dynamical constraints.

The active inference tradition itself trends in this direction. Laukkonen, Friston and Chandaria's Beautiful Loop (2025) derives self-modelling requirements from active inference — a structurally compatible result, though grounded in uncertainty reduction rather than simulation dynamics. That Friston's own framework generates architectural self-modelling constraints suggests the explanatory work is increasingly done by causal structure, not substrate chemistry. Seth's causal-powers framework and FMT's architectural specification may be closer than they initially appear: if "biological causal powers" ultimately reduce to "the causal powers of a self-referential architecture at criticality," the positions converge. Seth might contest this reduction — but the empirical predictions that would distinguish the two readings are precisely the kind of specification both frameworks call for.

Seth is right that the specification problem is the bottleneck. The productive disagreement is not whether specification matters but what form it should take. FMT offers one candidate — substrate-neutral, architecturally specific, empirically constrained by four untested predictions. Whether it is the right candidate is an empirical question, and the predictions are on the table.
