**Subject:** Pitch: After COGITATE — Why consciousness research needs a computational taxonomy, not another theory war

**Dear Nautilus editors,**

In April 2025, you published an excellent piece on the COGITATE adversarial collaboration — the decade-long, multi-million-dollar test that pitted Integrated Information Theory against Global Neuronal Workspace Theory and found neither fully confirmed. The result left the field in an awkward position: after the most expensive consciousness experiment in history, the two dominant frameworks produced an ambiguous verdict.

I'd like to propose a 3,000-word feature arguing that COGITATE's ambiguity wasn't a failure — it was diagnostic. Both theories were asking the wrong question, and the result tells us exactly *why*.

The problem is architectural. Both IIT and GWT (and most consciousness theories) ask "which neural process is conscious?" — hunting for the magic ingredient. But the brain doesn't work that way. It maintains four distinct types of model: a learned world model, a learned self model, an actively simulated world model, and an actively simulated self model. The first two are stored in synaptic structure (invisible to consciousness). The second two are generated in real time — that's your experience. The hard problem dissolves once you see that experience lives in the simulation layer, not the neural hardware layer. Looking for consciousness in neurons is like looking for the plot of a movie in the projector's circuits.

This isn't abstract philosophy. The framework — the Four-Model Theory of consciousness — makes nine specific, testable predictions that distinguish it from IIT, GWT, and predictive processing. Three examples:

**1. Psychedelic visuals follow the brain's processing hierarchy.** Under increasing doses, visual content should progress from V1-level patterns (phosphenes, geometric forms) through intermediate stages (faces, figures) to full narrative hallucinations — in order, dose-dependent, mappable to the cortical hierarchy. This is because psychedelics increase the permeability of the boundary between learned models and simulated models, and lower-level processing becomes visible first.

**2. Ego dissolution content is environmentally controllable.** During psychedelic ego dissolution, the identity content ("I became the ocean," "I was the wall") should track the dominant sensory environment. Change the environment, change what the person becomes. No other consciousness theory predicts this.

**3. Every anesthetic that erases consciousness disrupts criticality.** Despite working through completely different receptor mechanisms (propofol hits GABA, ketamine blocks NMDA, opioids do their own thing), all consciousness-abolishing agents should converge on a single dynamical signature: pushing the brain below the critical threshold. Testable with existing complexity measures (PCI, Lempel-Ziv).

The essay would follow a clear structure: what COGITATE actually showed (and why it's not a failure), what the four-model architecture is and why it reframes the question, three predictions that make it falsifiable (distinguishing it from philosophical speculation), and the engineering provocation — if this architecture is right, conscious machines become an engineering specification, not a philosophical fantasy.

**Why Nautilus:** This is a natural successor to your COGITATE coverage. Your readers already know the landscape and the frustration. This piece gives them a way forward — not another theory to root for, but a computational taxonomy that explains why the theory wars have been unproductive and what a resolution actually looks like.
