# Is psychology's core pseudoscience, and what can FMT ground? — S312 discussion

**MG opened this 2026-08-26 while the RIM fold-in was in flight.** His position, in his words: psychology
produces useful statistics on *arbitrary* concepts — intelligence, motivation, knowledge, intuition,
curiosity — held "in total disregard of physical reality in the brain", never nailed down, no visible
progress. FMT should be able to ground some of them. Which, and how. Plus three specific doubts: his own
definition of intelligence went **hollow on the motivation axis** while writing RIM, he now doubts
**knowledge** too, and he doubts that **Gf is fixed** — including the possibility that **age correction is
itself the error** and people do get dumber.

Every empirical claim below was verified against a primary or a publisher record this session. Where I could
not verify, it says so.

---

## 1. The strong claim is wrong, and it matters that he not walk into it

"Psychology is pseudoscience" fails on the demarcation it invokes. Pseudoscience is characterised by
*immunity to disconfirmation*; psychology's problem is the opposite — it disconfirms constantly and cannot
accumulate. A field that ran a hundred replications and published that 36% held is doing something a
pseudoscience never does.

**But the useful half of his claim is not the demarcation claim. It is that the constructs do not refer.**
And that version is not heterodox — it is the field's own leading critique, which makes it far more usable.

---

## 2. Where he is right, with the field's own witnesses

**Meehl (1978)** is the founding statement and it is nearly verbatim MG's complaint: theories in soft
psychology "come and go with little cumulative progress" — not refuted, merely abandoned. Meehl's *crud
factor* is the sharp end: in soft psych everything correlates with everything at roughly .2–.3, so
statistical significance carries almost no information, and a "confirmed prediction" is close to free.

**Eronen & Bringmann (2021)**, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 16(4), 779–788, update it and their
diagnosis is structurally identical to MG's. They name three obstacles: **the paucity of robust phenomena
that could constrain theories**, **validity problems with psychological constructs**, and **obstacles to
establishing causal relations between psychological variables**. Their explicit framing is that Meehl's 1978
assessment still holds. ⇒ MG's "never nailed down to anything, leaving everyone guessing" is the
construct-validity problem as the field states it about itself.

**Borsboom, Mellenbergh & van Heerden (2004)** supply the standard that makes the complaint precise: a test
is valid iff the attribute *exists* and *causes* variation in the measurement outcome. That is an
ontological requirement, not a correlational one — and it is the requirement MG is saying the constructs
fail. Using the field's own validity criterion against it is much stronger than importing a demarcation
argument from outside.

**And the sharpest ally is inside psychometrics itself: `g` may be a network artifact.**
**van der Maas, Dolan, Grasman, Wicherts, Huizenga & Raijmakers (2006)**, *Psychological Review* 113(4),
842–861 — the mutualism model. The positive manifold, which is the entire empirical basis for inferring `g`,
**emerges purely from mutually beneficial interactions between cognitive processes during development, and a
single underlying `g` factor plays no role in the model.** Kovacs & Conway's process-overlap theory reaches
the same place by another route. ⇒ The most replicated construct in the field may be a statistical shadow of
a network, which is precisely "an arbitrary concept that was never nailed down". RIM already cites van der
Maas at `:163`.

### ⚠ The counter he has to answer, and it is real

**The statistics are not arbitrary even where the concepts are.** `g`'s predictive validity for education,
occupation and mortality is among the most robust findings in social science. A construct can fail to refer
and still index something. Any FMT grounding programme has to explain *why the bad construct works*, not
merely that it is bad. Mutualism does exactly that — a network property is real, it is just not a thing —
and that is the template FMT should copy rather than compete with.

## 3. "Is Gf really fixed?" — he is right to doubt it, and the evidence is unusually strong

**Ritchie & Tucker-Drob (2018)**, *Psychological Science* — **142 effect sizes, 42 datasets, >600,000
participants**: an additional year of education is worth **1–5 IQ points**, the effect **persists across the
lifespan**, and it appears on **all broad categories of cognitive ability studied**, established through
quasi-experimental designs. Their own summary: education is "the most consistent, robust, and durable method
yet to be identified for raising intelligence."

Against that: **working-memory training does not transfer to Gf** (Melby-Lervåg & Hulme; Melby-Lervåg, Redick
& Hulme) — a null the field finds embarrassing.

**⇒ The two results are not in tension, and their joint reading is a result RIM can own.** Gf is not fixed;
it is not movable by *drilling the proxy*. Education moves it because education builds knowledge and
operational knowledge. WM training fails because it drills **P**. That is RIM's own K/P/operational-knowledge
decomposition predicting the field's most notorious null — **an existing failure explained, not a new claim
asserted**, which is the cheapest kind of evidence a theory can buy.

The Flynn effect points the same way (≈3 points/decade on Raven's, the most Gf-loaded instrument there is —
impossible genetically on that timescale), and the reversal in **Bratsberg & Rogeberg (2018)** is *within
families*, which rules out compositional explanations. RIM already cites it at `:141`.

## 4. "What if age correction is wrong and people do get dumber?" — his sharpest question

**This one is not a suspicion. It is close to established, and the reason it is invisible is a measurement
convention — which is his own thesis in miniature.**

IQ is **age-normed by construction**: the population mean is fixed at 100 at every age. So "do people get
dumber as they age?" is *unanswerable inside the metric*. The norming does not measure the answer; it
defines the question away. **If MG wants one example of psychology encoding an arbitrary decision so deeply
that it becomes invisible, this is the best one available.**

On raw, non-normed scores: **Salthouse (2009)**, *Neurobiology of Aging* 30, 507–514, concludes that some
aspects of age-related cognitive decline **begin in healthy educated adults in their 20s and 30s**, and
argues the reason longitudinal studies disagree is that **practice effects from prior test exposure mask the
decline**.

⚠ **State the dispute, because it is live and a reviewer will know it.** Schaie's group published a direct
reply — *"Salthouse again reifies the 'cross-sectional fallacy'"* — arguing the cross-sectional gradient
confounds cohort with age. The honest position is that the field does not agree on when decline starts, and
**that disagreement is itself the finding**: a century of intelligence measurement cannot say whether adults
get dumber, because its principal instrument is normed in a way that cannot address it.

The cohort version of his question is separately documented: the Flynn reversal in Norway, Denmark, Finland
and France. **So on both readings — individual and population — "people get dumber" has real support.**

## 5. What FMT can actually ground, and what it cannot

The move that does the work is not "reduce the concept to neurons". It is that **FMT has a physical
criterion for the implicit/explicit boundary — the death test**, in MG's own words: the implicit model is
everything still readable from a dead brain; the explicit modelling is the process it performs when alive.
That is a structure/process distinction with a physical boundary, not a construct.

| construct | FMT grounding | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Knowledge** | Factual knowledge = implicit-model content, inscribed in connectivity — survives the death test. Operational knowledge = the explicit layer's redeployment repertoire — also inscribed, but expressed only while running. | **Grounds cleanly.** RIM's factual/operational split *is* FMT's structure/process axis, which makes it physical rather than stipulated. |
| **Intelligence** | Capacity to build a rich model and re-point it cheaply (patterns #20/#26). Not a scalar. | **Grounds as a capacity, not a quantity** — and this predicts that `g` behaves like a network property, which is what mutualism finds. |
| **Intuition** | A readout from the implicit models whose *derivation is not itself explicitly modelled*, hence unreportable. | **Grounds, and yields a test**: the person can produce the answer and not the derivation. The intuition literature describes exactly this without being able to say why. |
| **Motivation** | See below — the answer is that it is not a component at all. | **The hollowness is real and diagnostic.** |
| **Curiosity** | The allocation policy pointed at the non-present and the novel. | Same object as motivation, different target. **Not a separate thing.** |

### The motivation answer, which is the sharpest thing in this discussion

**MG's axis went hollow because RIM types motivation as a component of the loop, alongside K and P — and in
FMT there is no motivational faculty to be a component.** What FMT has is pattern #35 (the quale delivers an
integrated value the brain reads; cognition supplies the credit assignment) and pattern #36 / *dreaming*
(offline replay-and-recombine over stored experiential material, terminating in a plan).

⇒ **Motivation is not a component of the loop. It is the allocation policy over the loop** — what gets
re-simulated, how often, for how long, and pointed at what. A schedule, not a substance.

Three things fall out immediately, and they are why this is worth taking seriously rather than merely
elegant:

1. **It dissolves the hollowness by explaining it.** You cannot nail down a faculty that is not there. An
   allocation policy has no trait-like essence to find, which is exactly what a century of motivation
   measurement reports.
2. **It predicts the measurement pathology Wittmann spent a career on.** An allocation policy is
   state-dependent and situation-bound, so single-occasion narrow measures *must* attenuate against broad
   aggregated criteria. Brunswik symmetry stops being an excuse for weak correlations and becomes a
   *derivation* from what motivation is.
3. **It converts RIM's Prediction 8 from stipulation to consequence.** Wittmann's Singapore finding is that
   motivation predicts performance **variability**, not level. A trait predicts level. **An allocation policy
   predicts consistency.** The datum RIM currently reports is the datum the reframe derives.

⚠ **The positioning risk, and it is the same shape as every other one in this project:** "motivation as
resource allocation" is adjacent to expected-value-of-control (Shenhav, Botvinick & Cohen) and to
opportunity-cost accounts of effort (Kurzban). **Verify before claiming novelty.** FMT's separable
contribution is that the allocated resource is *simulation time in an explicit self-model*, which those
accounts do not posit — but that difference has to be written, or the whole thing reads as EVC relabelled.

## 6. What this implies for a Platinum Edition

**The current RIM says: the field forgot a component.** That is a modest claim and it invites the modest
objection that PPIK already has interests and personality in its formal structure — which the review already
caught the paper conceding at `:157` while denying at `:35`.

**The stronger paper says: the field mis-typed the components.** P is the only one that is a capacity in the
psychometric sense. K splits along a physical boundary (structure vs process). M is not a component at all
but the scheduler over the other two. That is a claim about *why the constructs behave badly* — it explains
the attenuation, the training-transfer null, the polythetic structure, and the trait-measurement failure with
one move, and it is a much bigger paper than "add motivation".

⚠ **Framing warning, and it is not negotiable if this is going to a journal.** "Psychology is pseudoscience"
gets a desk rejection before the abstract ends. The identical content — routed through Meehl, Borsboom,
Eronen & Bringmann and van der Maas — is the field's own critique, is publishable, and lands harder precisely
because it cannot be dismissed as an outsider's complaint. **Attack the construct validity, never the
discipline.**
