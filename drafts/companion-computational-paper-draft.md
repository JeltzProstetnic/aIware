# In-Silico Tests of the Four-Model Architecture: Self-Referential Closure, Self-Modelling and Criticality in Model Systems

**Matthias Gruber**\
ORCID: 0009-0005-9697-1665\
Correspondence: research@matthiasgruber.com

---

## Abstract

The Four-Model Theory of Consciousness (FMT; Gruber, 2015, 2026) specifies consciousness as ongoing self-simulation across two kinds of models — implicit (learned, substrate-level) and explicit (generated, phenomenal in the self-model's closed loop) — organized by scope (world vs. self) and mode (implicit vs. explicit), and it requires a substrate capable of open-ended (Wolfram Class 4) computation. Because the theory is an architectural specification, its functional commitments can be tested in silico. This paper reports the in-silico results the master paper points to, sorted by evidential kind. Two distinctions are needed to read them, both forced by model-system results: a built control closure (Closure-1) versus an emergent predictive closure (Closure-2); and a capability-first reading of criticality on which avalanche (σ ≈ 1) and edge-of-chaos (λ ≈ 0) statistics are candidate instruments for one underlying capability rather than competing definitions, and neither certifies it alone. The banked results are mechanism demonstrations at toy, reservoir, and gridworld scale: a self-model-gated planner scales with problem depth where a bracket of reactive policies fails (0.00–0.33); prospective self-modelling confers a survival advantage (0.29–0.33 vs. 0.17–0.25); a self/other model carries a transfer capability that removing its generating projection abolishes (0.98 vs. 0.78, Cohen's *d* = 2.44), reported as feasibility and pre-conceded as not unique to FMT. On a rate reservoir, a demand-gated accuracy peak replicates on thirty fresh reservoirs but lies in the ordered regime, where a memory-capacity account predicts it, not at the measured edge of chaos. One result is partial — a maintained-decodable representation is not, at spiking scale, computable-upon — and one is null (re-entry on a homogeneous reservoir adds nothing, FMT-consistent and diagnostic). Three further results price the architecture rather than demonstrate a capability: opening the global loop of a model connectome costs 14.0% of its synapses where making it acyclic costs 74.0%, so the cheap edit relocates closure and does not abolish it; closure's advantage is conditional on the driven subsystem carrying change the rest of the system cannot derive for itself, and falls but does not vanish when it can; and an argmin over 72 enumerated architectures selects a bottleneck exactly where content is low-dimensional, spans otherwise-separate subsystems, must be delivered near the achievable ceiling, and reads back over a short path — four conditions a self-model satisfies, with the word "self" nowhere in the derivation. These are structural and wiring-cost results: optimality, never necessity. The scaled, closure-driven test of the theory's central dependency is not attempted here.

**Keywords:** consciousness; self-referential closure; criticality; edge of chaos; Wolfram Class 4; reservoir computing; world models; causal transfer; Four-Model Theory

## 1. Introduction

The Four-Model Theory (FMT) is an architectural claim: consciousness is what a particular kind of self-simulating system *does*, not what a particular kind of matter *is* (Gruber, 2015, 2026). Such a claim is buildable. If a theory says a system with features X and Y, running in regime Z, exhibits capability C, one can construct systems that instantiate X and Y in Z, matched systems that lack one of them, and measure C. An in-silico program for FMT tests the theory's functional commitments — task performance, transfer, and how a capability depends on substrate regime. It does not test phenomenality, which the other-minds problem forecloses for artefacts as for biological others, and which FMT's own Hard-Problem account rates only partial (Gruber, 2026, §3.4.3, §7.1 Table 5 note ‡, §8.10).

The results are reported by evidential kind. All banked results are mechanism demonstrations at toy, reservoir, or gridworld scale; they show the architecture's components behaving as the theory requires, and they are not the scaled test of the theory's central dependency, which this paper does not attempt. Where a result is also produced by other frameworks it is marked as such — the self/other transfer result of §4.3 is the case in point.

Section 2 restates the architecture in in-silico terms. Section 3 develops two framing moves the results forced. Section 4 reports the banked capability results; Section 5, the three architectural-cost results; Section 6, a null result on a homogeneous reservoir; Section 7 discusses; Section 8 states limitations.

## 2. The four-model architecture, in silico

The full theory — its philosophical commitments and neuroscientific grounding — is in the master paper (Gruber, 2026); this section restates only what the experiments require.

FMT distinguishes two kinds of models along two axes — a computational taxonomy, a 2×2 of model *kinds*, not a claim that the brain contains four discrete modules or clean anatomical boxes. The mode axis contrasts *implicit* models (learned, stored in connectivity, non-conscious, operating in the dark) with *explicit* models (generated, transient, phenomenal in the self-model's closed loop). The scope axis contrasts *world* with *self*, where the self-model's content is a proper part of the world-model's content. Crossing them gives the four canonical kinds: the Implicit World Model (IWM), the Implicit Self Model (ISM), the Explicit World Model (EWM), and the Explicit Self Model (ESM). The explicit models are generated from the implicit ones plus current input — the EWM from the IWM's stored world-knowledge, the ESM from the ISM's stored self-knowledge (Gruber, 2026, §3.2). Four is a minimum, not a maximum: the biological substrate implements an effectively uncountable ecology of overlapping models around these two axes, and the canonical four are the minimum set of model kinds the architecture forces and the ecology's extremal points.

Two commitments of the architecture matter for what we build. The first is self-referential closure: a system whose world-model includes a model of *itself modelling the world*, so that the model and the modelled are no longer separable (Gruber, 2026, §3.4.3). FMT treats closure — not mere self-monitoring, which is ubiquitous in engineering and produces no inside perspective — as the feature that distinguishes conscious from non-conscious computation. For an in-silico program, closure is not one thing (§3), and what an experiment toggles when it manipulates a feedback path need not be the closure the theory means.

The second is free modelling: the system must be capable of open-ended computation — the capability Wolfram (2002) and Langton (1990) located at Class 4, the edge of chaos — and must recruit it for its own modelling (Gruber, 2026, §3.7). This is a computational prerequisite, not a definition of consciousness. Near-criticality is the signature free modelling usually leaves in cortex, not a condition of it. The capability enables the computation; the architecture, the richness of the self-model, determines the level of consciousness. Free modelling and the four-model architecture are separately necessary and not jointly sufficient; FMT's sufficient condition is the conjunction of the four model kinds under closure, free compute, embodiment and at least one pain-pleasure axis (Gruber, 2026, §3.7.3).

A synthetic instantiation of the architecture therefore needs, minimally: a substrate capable of Class 4 dynamics; a stored implicit world-model that a generated explicit world-model is read out from; a self-model nested inside the world-model; and, for the strongest tests, the capacity to turn a closure loop on and off.

**Structural priors.** In animals, intelligence and consciousness emerge from an entangled history of innate structure and experience, and replicating that history in silico, years of embodied and socially scaffolded learning, is beyond a minimal program. The program therefore installs selected products of development as structural priors and tests the mechanism that operates on them. One such prior is a mirror-topographic correspondence between the coordinates of observed movement and the system's own. With it, an untrained network with the closure loop on rehearses an observed behaviour into its own motor channel: cosine similarity to the demonstrator template is 0.92, against 0.19 with the loop off (three seeds, smallest gap 0.72). Consolidating that rehearsal into a reproducible skill through reward-free play has not been achieved; the directed reproduction score stayed at chance in every closure condition. Each prior of this kind stipulates what development would otherwise have to discover, so a result obtained with it shows what the mechanism does given the prior and says nothing about whether an open-ended self acquires the prior unaided.

## 3. Two framing moves the results forced

Two conceptual moves are prerequisites for interpreting any experiment in this program. Both were forced by model-system results — the two closures by the re-entry null (§6), capability-first by the criticality scans (§3.2).

### 3.1 Two closures: control (built) and predictive (emergent)

FMT's constitutive notion (Gruber, 2026, §3.4.3) is a loop in which the system's model of the world *includes* the system's model of itself modelling, so that no internal description of the system's processing sits outside what the system models. Building such a thing surfaces two very different loops.

Closure-1, the built control ring, is the world-to-self *control* closure: the output of the explicit models flows downward to modulate the system's autonomous subsystems — body, actuators, sensor gating. Toggling it changes whether the high-level model is allowed to steer its own low-level machinery. It is a control-theoretic loop, not the constitutive self-referential loop the theory posits.

Closure-2, the emergent predictive loop, is the self-in-world *predictive* closure: an ESM embedded inside the EWM that, by predicting, shapes its own future input. It is not wired in; it emerges once a self-model nested in a world-model is trained to predict, because a nested predictor that acts on the world it predicts feeds its predictions back into its own input distribution. Closure-2 is the in-silico correlate of what FMT means by self-referential closure: the loop that closes between a differentiated self-model and world-model, not around an undifferentiated pool. Toggling its components separately shows it is not monolithic — it resolves into at least three distinct loops with separable effects (§4.6).

Every experiment in the program therefore carries a checklist question: which closure is being toggled, and is there a differentiated self/world structure for it to close between? Adding a feedback path to a homogeneous reservoir toggles neither Closure-1 nor Closure-2 in the sense the theory needs; it adds re-entry to a blob, with no distinct self and world models between which a loop could close. Section 6 reports a case where this happened.

### 3.2 Capability-first criticality: two instruments, one capability band

The second move concerns which criticality the program is committed to. The consciousness-criticality literature offers two operational signatures often treated as rival definitions: avalanche criticality, a branching ratio σ ≈ 1 with power-law-distributed cascade sizes (Beggs & Plenz, 2003; Priesemann et al., 2014), and edge-of-chaos criticality, a maximal Lyapunov exponent λ ≈ 0 separating ordered from chaotic dynamics (Langton, 1990; Bertschinger & Natschläger, 2004; Boedecker et al., 2012). One could treat a single signature as the definition of criticality and detune against it.

FMT's grounding does not require choosing. Its requirement derives from Wolfram's computational-universality framework: what the substrate must have is the *capability* to support open-ended computation (Gruber, 2026, §3.7.3), and criticality statistics are signatures of that capability, not the requirement. σ ≈ 1 and λ ≈ 0 are then two candidate instruments for that capability at different substrate levels — avalanche statistics natural at the spiking level, Lyapunov and spectral measures at the rate level — not two things a system must simultaneously be. Neither certifies the capability by itself. A subsampled branching ratio tracks firing rate: in our spiking pool, unconnected units driven to a matched rate pass the same σ test. A σ from that instrument therefore establishes a firing rate unless it is reported beside its rate-matched surrogate, and the instrument has to be chosen for the level that carries the self-model. They are not expected to co-occur: Kanders et al. (2017) show that avalanche and edge-of-chaos criticality can be distinct transitions in the same class of network, present at different parameter settings. Calling a system non-critical because it fails one instrument while passing the other confuses instrument with capability.

A model-system scan forced the point. In a leaky echo-state network the nominal spectral-radius setpoint $\rho_{\mathrm{sr}}$ = 1 was not the network's edge of chaos (the true edge lay near $\rho_{\mathrm{sr}}$ ≈ 2.6), and memory capacity and computational capability co-peaked in the ordered regime and co-collapsed at the edge, showing no clean memory/computation dissociation at the conventional statistic (§4.6). Reading the requirement off any single statistic would have mislabelled the substrate. The requirement is universal-computation capability (Class 4), certified by whichever instrument suits the substrate, not proximity to a particular σ or λ. We call this stance capability-first. It makes the reservoir/rate route (spectral radius, Lyapunov) and the spiking/avalanche route (branching ratio) complementary rather than contradictory: the same capability at two substrate levels.

## 4. Banked results: what the architecture has been shown to do in-model

*Evidential kind: mechanism demonstrations, confirmed in-model and surviving adversarial replication. Scale: toy / reservoir / gridworld.*

Each result below survived hostile re-analysis and, where relevant, pre-registered gate checks. The positive results show self-models and Class 4 capability affecting task performance, under the standing boundary that all of them are small-scale mechanism demonstrations. None is the scaled test of the theory's central dependency.

### 4.1 A self-model-gated planner scales with problem depth where reactive policies fail

Closure should pay off where reactive computation runs out *at a fixed budget* — on problems whose depth exceeds what a stimulus-response policy absorbs within the samples it is given. Nothing here claims a reactive architecture could not reach the same behaviour with more; the comparison is what each buys per sample. A gridworld experiment tests this and passes a full pre-registered gate battery (6/6 gates; author-ratified). A fitted self-model coupled to a planner is compared against a bracket of competent reactive baselines — tabular Q-learning, action-conditioned fitted-Q iteration with ensembles, a behavioural clone, and a wall-follower. On the depth-scaling task the entire reactive bracket scores 0.00–0.33 while the self-model-gated planner succeeds. The advantage is world-conditional (present where a model is required) and capacity-relative (it appears as problem depth grows past what the reactive bracket reaches on this budget).

**Methods.** The task is a numpy gridworld with a patrolling hazard; depth is operationalized as the planner's horizon, *h* ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32}, evaluated on a *frozen* MIXED set of *n* = 40 arenas. The score is the fraction of arenas solved in more than five steps, with within-arena bootstrap (10,000 resamples) for confidence. The self-model-gated arm couples a fitted self-consequence model to a finite-horizon planner and tracks a perfect-model ORACLE across the whole depth range (*f* = 0.07 at *h* = 1 → 0.99 at *h* = 32). Zero-shot scores for the reactive bracket are: scripted-compass 0.07, tabular Q-learning 0.00, ensemble fitted-Q iteration 0.00, behavioural clone 0.00, wall-follower 0.33; adversarial refits on the same episodes leave ≥ 0.55 headroom to the self-model arm. All six pre-registered gates passed.

### 4.2 Prospective self-modelling confers a behavioural survival advantage

An agent that simulates its own future can avoid outcomes a purely reactive agent walks into. In a toy survival task, an agent that acts on its self-model's own survival prediction outlives a matched reactive control — survival 0.29–0.33 for the prospective agent versus 0.17–0.25 for the reactive one, across runs of 5, 10, and 20 seeds. The magnitude is small and the task is a toy; it is an existence result for closure-on-performance, not a large effect. A predictive-perception-mask variant of the same manipulation showed no robust benefit, feeding the perception-mask dissociation of §4.6.

**Methods.** The task is a closed 3 × 3 room in which a predator sweeps in a boustrophedon pattern and inaction is fatal; survival is the fraction of the 40-step episode the agent survives. The prospective agent is an ungated self-preservation controller that devalues actions its own self-model predicts will not survive. Across seed budgets of 5, 10, and 20 the prospective agent scores 0.290, 0.332, and 0.292 respectively; the matched reactive control scores 0.170, 0.190, and 0.247. The perception-mask variant of the same manipulation shows no robust benefit. The effect is small and deterministic; we report it as an existence result, not a large effect.

### 4.3 A self/other model carries a capability that removing its projection abolishes — feasibility, not uniqueness

The architecture requires that an explicit self/other model be able to *carry* a capability, so that removing what generates it takes something behaviourally real away with it. The manipulation available is structural: the explicit models are generated activity, not stored structure, so what an experiment can cut is the projection that generates them, never the model itself. A model system shows this with a large effect. In an observational-to-outcome transfer task the model-bearing system reaches 0.98 while a control with that projection removed reaches 0.78, the chance level of the probe (Cohen's *d* = 2.44), and the transfer criterion passes at 1.0 in every model-bearing arm and 0.0 in every cut arm — a categorical, not graded, dissociation.

This result is not unique to FMT. That a network can learn a causal, transferable model and reuse it, and that removing the structure carrying the model removes the transfer, is shared with a large body of work on causal representation learning and model-based transfer (Schölkopf et al., 2021). It is not evidence favouring FMT over IIT, GNW, predictive processing, or any other account. Its role is prior and narrower: it establishes that the modelling step the architecture requires — building an explicit, reusable model and deploying it where it is needed — is well-defined and realizable in the substrate class used elsewhere in the program. The task on which it was shown is categorical and shallow; scaling it is separate work.

**Methods.** The task is observational-to-outcome transfer in a 9 × 7 gridworld. Training is 5 × 40 episodes on one hazard family and mechanism; a scripted victim dies at tick 1 and the death event is broadcast to the observing agent. Transfer is scored on avoidance of a *novel* Cliff or Thermal hazard sharing the trained mechanism, using 20 forced-choice probes. In each probe the agent is placed on a neighbour of the novel hazard and makes one move; stepping onto the hazard is one of its five actions, so avoidance by chance is 0.8. The model-bearing agent is an echo-state-network agent (N = 100 units, spectral radius 0.95, view radius 2, ε = 0.1); the ablated arm is identical apart from the removal of the explicit self/other model projection. The result depends on representing each hazard by its causal mechanism, with aversion accumulated through the self/other projection. Across 40 seeds, the model-bearing arm scores 0.9800 ± 0.0273 versus 0.7762 ± 0.1149 for the ablated arm, which sits at chance (Cohen's *d* = 2.44; independent-samples *t*-test *p* = 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁷); the transfer criterion itself is categorical (1.0 in every model-bearing arm, 0.0 in every ablated arm).

### 4.4 A demand-gated peak in a rate reservoir, replicated — in the ordered regime, not at the edge

*Evidential kind: replicated on thirty fresh seeds with the edge measured; the demand-gated shape replicates and the edge location does not. A dissociation supporting §3.2.*

Capability-first (§3.2) says the substrate must be able to support open-ended computation; whether sitting at the edge *buys* computational power is a separate question. In a rate echo-state network with an input-bias channel, an accuracy peak appears for the medium parity task alone: easy tasks are solved everywhere, and the hard task fails at small size and lifts with size. The demand-gated shape holds on 30 of 30 fresh reservoirs. Measured by the driven largest Lyapunov exponent on the task's own input, the peak lies in the ordered regime: λ ≈ −0.68 nats/step [−0.70, −0.67], the edge at spectral radius 2.29 against a peak at 1.05, accuracy 0.85 at the edge against a ceiling of 1.00, and 0 of 30 seeds within ±0.05 of λ = 0, robust to readout regularisation, state noise and size; the hard task's peak at *N* = 2000 lies at λ ≈ −0.38. The peak coincides with the peak of linear recall of the task's older operand and moves with it, so the gating is a memory-horizon effect. The input-bias channel, with input scaling 2, saturates about half the units and moves the driven edge to about 2.2 times its nominal value, inside the driven-stable regime in which Schuecker, Goedeke and Helias (2018) and Haruna and Nakajima (2019) place the optimum of driven random networks. Together with §4.6 this supports capability-first; it is not evidence that the edge pays. Five further toy-scale scans found no in-band benefit, and a spiking pool run under the same protocol could not test location, because its lag memory is carried by the membrane time constant and does not change at zero recurrent gain.

**Methods.** The task family is temporal parity, target = *u*[*t*−*k*₁] · *u*[*t*−*k*₂], with difficulty set by the lag gap (easy 1, 2 / medium 2, 6 / hard 4, 11) and chance level 0.5; a ridge readout is fit over a rate echo-state network. The apparent "compute floor" of earlier scans was traced to a readout-symmetry artefact — a bias-free $\tanh$ state is odd, which makes parity unrepresentable at the readout — and was removed by adding a constant input-bias channel. In the original run criticality was instrumented by the nominal spectral radius $\rho_{\mathrm{sr}}$ and the edge was not measured; the replication (*N* = 300, leak 1.0, input scaling 2, input-bias channel, 41-point $\rho_{\mathrm{sr}}$ grid 0.25–6.0, ridge 1e-2 with refits at 1e-6 to 1, state-noise arms 1e-3 and 1e-2, *N* ∈ {1000, 2000}, leak 0.3; seeds 100–129 for the primary and noise arms, 100–119 for leak 0.3, 100–109 for the scale and no-bias arms, pre-registered) measures the largest Lyapunov exponent of the input-driven reservoir by tangent propagation through the exact Jacobian along the task's own input stream, validated against a two-trajectory Benettin estimate, and takes the edge as the $\rho_{\mathrm{sr}}$ where λ enters λ > 0; a peak counts as at the edge if |λ_peak| ≤ 0.05 nats/step in at least 80% of seeds with the bootstrap interval of the mean inside that band. An accuracy peak appears only for the medium task and is *demand-gated*: easy tasks are solved everywhere, hard tasks nowhere at small size. Increasing reservoir size *N* ∈ {300, 1000, 2000} (ten seeds each) lifts the hard task off chance (median maximum 0.56 → 0.63 → 0.72) and its peak sits at λ ≈ −0.38 in the measured coordinate. Five further scans found no edge-peak: in two rate reservoirs capability was best on the ordered side; a third rate reservoir and a spiking pool returned nulls, the spiking pool's band having been assigned by a whole-pool branching ratio with no rate-matched surrogate, a reading that tracks firing rate; and a fourth rate reservoir returned a null inside the band.

### 4.5 A partial result: maintained-decodable is not yet computable-upon

Not every gate passed. At spiking scale, closure maintenance is content-specific: a re-entrant loop holds operands decodable across distractor delays D = 20–45 (decode 0.61–0.76 against chance 0.50) while a scrambled generic-drive control stays at chance (gate G2 passes). But the downstream test failed — on the same substrate the self-model did not beat a passive linear delay-line on a task that required *computing upon* the maintained content (gate G9 fails). Maintained-decodable content is not the same as computable-upon content. A loop can keep a representation decodable without that representation yet being usable for a downstream computation at a given scale.

**Methods.** The substrate is a two-pool spiking network; the world pool is an excitatory/inhibitory leaky integrate-and-fire population ($n_{\mathrm{world}}$ = 256), gain = 2.0, branching-σ measured from the raster in the range 0.44–1.03. No rate-matched surrogate was run, so these values fix a firing rate and not a critical regime; the result below does not depend on them. The delayed-parity task requires two operands held through a distractor delay *D*, with operands zeroed during the delay and a probe decode at the end. With re-entry scaling of 3.0, the loop keeps both operands decodable through *D* = 45 (decode 0.76, 0.64 and 0.61 at *D* = 20, 30 and 45; means over seeds 0–7 with the membrane voltage bounded below by a biophysical floor, pending independent re-analysis), while a matched scrambled-drive control and the loop-off arm stay at chance (0.48–0.52) — gate G2 passes. Gate G9, the downstream test, is a held-out ridge decode of the XOR of the two operands against a matched *linear leaky delay-line* control. G9 **fails**: the spiking self-model does not beat the delay line at this scale. Maintained-decodable is therefore not equivalent to computable-upon on this substrate.

### 4.6 Supporting dissociations

Two further model-system results underwrite the framing above rather than standing as headline claims.

A three-way closure dissociation. Toggling the components of Closure-2 separately shows that closure resolves into at least three distinct loops with separable effects: the transfer capability of §4.3 is carried specifically by mechanism binding (transfer 1.0 with it, 0.0 ablated); a projection re-entry loop reshapes the self-model itself (selfΔ ≈ 0.99); and a perception mask lowers the fidelity of the system's self-knowledge (the self-knowledge correlation $R_{\mathrm{ISM}}$ falls from 0.987 to 0.90). Closure is therefore a small family of loops, not a single switch — which is why the two-closure distinction (§3.1) treats "which closure?" as a mandatory experimental question.

**Methods.** The dissociation experiment toggles two default-off closure components against a fixed in-band substrate (ESN, spectral radius 0.95). Five arms are compared — *both* / *perception* / *projection* / *off* / *ablated* — across 5 seeds. Three readouts are measured. The transfer readout is carried by the ESM (1.0 for the full agent versus 0.0 for the arm with the projection removed) and is invariant across toggles. The selfΔ readout is the drop in predicted survival after the self-model witnesses and refits (≈ 0.99), which is moved only by the projection arm. The self-knowledge readout $R_{\mathrm{ISM}}$ is a Pearson correlation between the ISM's predicted self-state and the actual self-state, a correlation proxy for self-knowledge fidelity; the perception mask lowers it from 0.987 to 0.90.

Criticality is not read off a single statistic. The leaky-ESN scan ($\rho_{\mathrm{sr}}$ = 1 not the edge; true edge $\rho_{\mathrm{sr}}$ ≈ 2.6; memory and computation co-peaking in the ordered regime) is the empirical basis for capability-first (§3.2) rather than a headline result in its own right. It is what turned "operate at criticality" into "have and use Class 4 capability."

**Methods.** The model system is a leaky echo-state network, *N* = 1024, driven by a unipolar ±1 input (scale ≈ 3 breaks tanh odd-symmetry so XOR is representable), scanned over spectral radius $\rho_{\mathrm{sr}}$ = 0.90–3.00 with per-$\rho_{\mathrm{sr}}$ ridge cross-validation. The driven Lyapunov exponent (Benettin) puts the edge (λ = 0) near $\rho_{\mathrm{sr}}$ ≈ 2.6–2.7; the nominal $\rho_{\mathrm{sr}}$ = 1 setpoint is ordered (λ ≈ −0.12). Memory capacity $\mathrm{MC}(\rho_{\mathrm{sr}}) = \sum_{k=1}^{15} R_{\mathrm{recall}}(k)$ and computation R on XOR(*b*(*t*−2), *b*(*t*−3)) both peak together at $\rho_{\mathrm{sr}}$ ≈ 1.7–1.9 (λ ≈ −0.05) and co-collapse at the true edge — no memory/computation dissociation at the conventional $\rho_{\mathrm{sr}}$ = 1 setpoint.

## 5. Architectural cost, benefit and selection of closure

*Evidential kind: structural and wiring-cost results, exact within their stated scope. Substrate: one synthetic connectome family, at numpy and spiking scale.*

The results of Section 4 ask what the architecture can be shown to *do*. Three further results ask what it *costs*, and they are reported here as the master paper's fifth, sixth and seventh banked results (Gruber, 2026, §8). They share a substrate and a form of argument: each prices an architectural feature by enumerating the alternatives and measuring what the cheapest one buys. None of them is a capability claim, and none is evidence that closure is necessary for anything — a necessity claim this program has falsified five times.

One realization fact governs how every arm below is to be read. Every loop arm in the results below was simulated as the **materialised dense product** of its factors, not as a relayed bottleneck: the rank-*k* operator was formed as the product of its two factors and applied densely. The factored bill is the right quantity for a *storage* or *wiring-cost* claim, which is what these three results make; it is the wrong quantity for any dynamical or metabolic sentence, because what ran was a dense matrix. A physically wired bottleneck is a separate object; its one run on the spiking substrate is reported in Section 8.

### 5.1 Closure is cheap to relocate and expensive to abolish

Preventing closure is two operations, and conflating them is what makes "you cannot prevent it" sound like rhetoric instead of a measurement. One can *open the global loop* — take the self region out of the returning component it shares with the rest — or one can make the network *acyclic*, so that nothing returns anywhere. The two differ by a factor of five to six.

An exhaustive minimum-synapse block search over the model connectome finds that opening the global loop costs 14.0% of the connectome in its dense configuration and 12.0% in its modular one, and leaves two and three smaller closed self-consistent systems respectively still standing. Buying acyclicity costs 74.0% and 71.5% — a 5.3× and 6.0× multiplier over the cheap operation. The cheap edit therefore buys a rearrangement of where closure sits, not its abolition; abolishing closure costs the recurrence itself, which is a phase change rather than a position on an axis.

The result is exact in the direction the claim needs. A neuron-level cycle projects to a region-level closed walk, so region-level acyclicity implies neuron-level non-return, and these block-level figures are therefore an exact *upper bound* on the effort required — the conservative direction for a claim that closure is hard to prevent.

Two scope statements travel with it. This is a claim about graph structure and not about dynamics: it says nothing about whether the surviving closed components do anything the theory cares about, and dynamical survival of the residue is unmeasured. And it prices the *removal* of closure, not what closure buys; it is not a defence of closure necessity for capability, and it is scoped to one connectome family at region granularity.

The same search separates the program's closure manipulation from a second cut nobody had distinguished. Efferent disconnection of the self region — the manipulation the program actually uses — costs 14.03% of synapses and leaves two closed systems, the self region among them. Cutting the self region's own recurrence costs 6.23% and leaves one. The cheaper cut is the second, and the two leave opposite residues, so which cut an experiment performs is not a detail of implementation.

**Methods.** Cost is counted in **synapses** rather than blocks, because a designer preventing closure pays for wiring removed and a block count would let one fat block hide behind two thin ones. Dense rest configuration: opening the global loop takes 3 blocks / 2,160,000 synapses (14.0%), leaving CE+EWM+ISM and ESM; acyclicity takes 10 blocks / 11,400,000 synapses (74.0%), leaving nothing. Modular configuration: 2 blocks / 1,680,000 synapses (12.0%), leaving CE+EWM, ESM and ISM; acyclicity 7 blocks / 10,040,000 synapses (71.5%). The realized 440-neuron connectome is a single strongly connected component over all 440 nodes. The 14.0% figure was cross-checked by an independent method: a separate partition of the same architecture puts the re-entry block at 2,158,030 of 15,391,490 synapses. *Acyclic* is used as the standard term; the program's local name for the same property is *return-free*, kept only because *return* has a separate technical meaning elsewhere in it.

### 5.2 Closure earns its keep exactly where the driven subsystem carries change the bottleneck cannot derive

The conditional form of the closure claim can be measured directly by holding the architecture fixed and varying only what the system could in principle work out for itself. In a spiking architecture whose rank-*k* write operator writes to a subsystem and never reads it, with no return path at any point, holding connectome, carriers and per-unit injected energy fixed, and flipping *only* whether the carrier waveform carries information about the disturbance driving that subsystem, downstream *R*² moves from −0.001 to 0.640.

The claim rests on the negative result inside it. In the hidden condition every rival readout ties to four decimal places at every seed — matched-*τ*, plant-inversion, passive buffer, and the bottleneck-only arm — so no readout at any capacity extracts what is not there. The advantage is therefore not a property of the readout the loop happens to use; it is a property of what the signal carries.

Measured against the *closed* arm at matched condition, the advantage is 0.398 when the disturbance is absent, 0.209 when it is present but underivable, and 0.089 once an intervention has made it derivable: reduced, but never zero. This is a *conditional* claim and not a necessity claim. It is also worth saying why the third condition is not an escape route available to the system: the intervention that makes the disturbance derivable is not an architecture the system could adopt for itself, because observing the disturbance would require reading the driven subsystem, and reading the driven subsystem *is* the closure.

**Methods.** The write operator is realized as the materialised dense product of its factors, not as a relayed bottleneck; every arm in this comparison, the bottleneck-only arm included, was realized the same way. The reported effect is a mean paired reduction of 0.403 in a per-seed normalized-error measure (s.e.m. 0.027, *t* = 14.7, five seeds); the error normalization differs from 1 − *R*², so the reduction is **not** the difference implied by the two *R*² endpoints quoted above, and the two must not be subtracted into each other. The four-decimal tie is a within-run comparison: one rollout is stored per seed and all four readouts are computed from the same stored trajectory, so the substrate's floating-point nondeterminism is common to the arms and cannot produce the agreement. The absolute error values themselves reproduce across rebuilds only to about 0.00026, which is the envelope any figure from this substrate must be read within. Scope: one connectome family, five seeds.

### 5.3 A bottleneck architecture is selected under four conditions, and a self-model satisfies all four

A bottleneck is selected exactly where the content is low-dimensional relative to the substrate carrying it, spans otherwise-separate subsystems, must be delivered near the ceiling of what the architecture can achieve, and reads back over a short path. Each condition is measured by its own failure, which is what makes this a specificity result rather than the general observation that bottlenecks are cheap: a universal claim would predict bottleneck architecture in systems with no self-model at all, and would support the theory *less*, not more.

An argmin over 72 architectures — the seven possible spans of three subsystems crossed with a ten-value rank grid, plus two non-bottleneck rivals, enumerated in code before any arm ran — finds, first, that where a bottleneck is selected its cost-optimal span is *exactly* the regions the content occupies: 8 of 8 seeds, all four content conditions, at every performance bar from 0.80 to 0.99, with fewer regions unreachable at any capacity and more regions identical in function at proportionally higher cost.

Second, the selection is confined to low-dimensional content, and there is no threshold to state. At four stored patterns the bottleneck wins 100% of seeds at every substrate size from 42 units per subsystem upward, its advantage growing from 241 to 6288 synapses; at sixteen patterns on a small substrate it is robustly *more* expensive (−2428 to −7324 synapses); in between, the two cost curves have different shapes and cross twice, so which is cheaper alternates.

Third, the advantage grows steeply with the number of subsystems the loop spans — 195, 2159 and 6048 synapses for one, two and three subsystems at 84 units each, an elevenfold step and then a further factor of under three — monotonically at every substrate size tested, and becoming unbounded at 126 units, where plain wiring never reaches the criterion at any budget in 48 of 48 cells.

Fourth, and measured on a separate axis where the implemented operation is held bitwise identical across arms rather than matched statistically: the bottleneck must also read back over a short path. Its cost advantage over the bottleneck-free rival survives until the cost model prices the loop's synapses at roughly 1732 times the rival's when the path back is a single stage, and at only 210 times when it is eight — so a *distant* self-loop is not selected either.

The consequence is the reason this result is in the paper. For content spanning both a world model and a body, the cheapest bottleneck reads the body's own representation — and a loop reading the body's representation is a loop over the system's own state. **Self-inclusion falls out of wiring cost, with the word "self" nowhere in the derivation.** This is a wiring-cost result on a numpy substrate and claims nothing about consciousness, report or subjecthood; it is optimality and never necessity.

**Methods.** The span-equals-support finding was measured in four content conditions — content spanning world and body, world only, body only, and all three subsystems — at every performance bar from 0.80 to 0.99. The low-dimensionality boundary was mapped in one content condition, world and body, over substrate sizes of 21–126 units per subsystem and ranks of 4, 8 and 16; the second cost-curve crossing sits at the edge of that range, so nothing here claims there are exactly two crossings. One cost model was used throughout. The subsystem-scaling steps are 11.072× and then 2.801× and must not be summarized as an order of magnitude per subsystem. The near-ceiling condition is itself measured rather than assumed: at a bar of 90% of the achievable ceiling, plain wiring beat every bottleneck in all seeds and conditions — the failure that locates the third condition. The two bars are not the same measurement: the near-ceiling bar is relative, at 90% of the achievable ceiling, whereas the criterion held fixed across the synaptic-filter sweep below is absolute. The scope statement approved for the loop arms and the filter sweep on 12 August 2026, two days before the wired run reported in Section 8, is imported verbatim from the source record:

> *The loop arms are span-routed rank-k bottlenecks realized in folded form; the cost law is a
> factored-storage/wiring result, and no relayed implementation has run on the spiking substrate.
> The result reproduces unchanged under a synaptic time constant of $\tau_{\mathrm{syn}}$ = 2 — the substrate's decided
> value — and again at $\tau_{\mathrm{syn}}$ = 5, including the rival cost margins to four significant figures in two
> of three content conditions. At $\tau_{\mathrm{syn}}$ = 10 the achievable ceiling falls below the fixed criterion in
> two of three conditions, so the comparison is not adjudicable there. **The boundary therefore lies in
> the interval (5, 10].** One condition's rival margin rises fourfold at $\tau_{\mathrm{syn}}$ = 5 with no change of
> arm; that is recorded as an open diagnostic and no claim rests on it.*

## 6. A null result: re-entry on a homogeneous reservoir

*Evidential kind: reported null, FMT-consistent and diagnostic.*

One experiment toggled a self-referential re-entry loop (ON/OFF) on a homogeneous spiking reservoir and asked whether the loop improved retention of a cue across a delay of τ = 15 steps. The result was a clean null: the passive reservoir already maintained the cue near-perfectly out to roughly 75 steps (native memory horizon $H_{\mathrm{native}}$ ≈ 100 far exceeds τ = 15), and closure ON never beat OFF at any setting tested — neutral at small re-entry gains (re ≤ 0.5), destructive at large ones (re ≥ 3).

The null is FMT-consistent for two reasons the framing of Section 3 makes precise. First, the substrate was a single undifferentiated reservoir — a blob with no distinct self- and world-models between which a loop could close. The re-entry it toggled was neither Closure-1 nor Closure-2 in the theory's sense (§3.1); it was feedback on a blob. Second, the substrate was not memory-limited for this task ($H_{\mathrm{native}}$ ≈ 100 ≫ τ = 15), so the passive reservoir could already solve it cheaply and there was no modelling step left for closure to make cheaper. The theory holds that closure does nothing on its own: what it buys is a *sample-efficiency advantage*, and only inside a window where the task is hard enough for the substrate that the loop's overhead is repaid. A finite closed loop unrolled over a finite horizon is feedforward plus memory, so nothing here can be a capability closure alone confers — the claim is always about price. Outside that window the loop is overhead on work the substrate already does, so adding it should be neutral-to-harmful, which is what was observed.

That experiment tested closure as if it were the effect — as though switching a loop on should by itself produce a gain — rather than as a conditional efficiency advantage. Testing a conditional advantage as if it were a direct cause is a category error, and a diagnostic one: it signals that the design lacks the two things closure needs in order to be worth its cost, a differentiated self/world structure for the loop to close between and a task the passive substrate cannot already solve cheaply.

**Methods.** The substrate is a single homogeneous spiking reservoir of leaky integrate-and-fire neurons, *N* = 512 for the primary sweep and *N* = 1024 for the strongest re-entry condition. Gain = 1.0 gives a branching ratio σ ≈ 1. That instrument tracks firing rate, since unconnected units at a matched rate also pass it, so the setting fixes a rate and not a critical regime. A one-hot Left / Right cue is presented at *t* = 0, input is silenced for τ steps, and readout is a ridge one-vs-rest 5-fold decode (chance 0.5). With input silenced, passive decode remains near-perfect out to about 75 steps and falls to chance by roughly 150 steps, giving a native memory horizon $H_{\mathrm{native}}$ far above the tested τ = 15. Re-entry scale is grid-swept over {0.5, 1, 3, 6}, using a 64-dimensional projection of the self-state as α = 0.6 positive-feedback current (roughly a 2.3× gain). Closure ON never exceeds OFF: neutral for re ≤ 0.5, degrading at re = 1.0, and destructive for re ≥ 3 (the projection washes out the cue). Seed count for this specific null was not tabulated in the digest and can be recovered from run logs if a canonical $N_{\mathrm{seeds}}$ is wanted.

## 7. Discussion

The results place closure as a conditional efficiency advantage rather than a direct cause, and never as a capability it alone confers. The positive results cluster where reactive computation runs out — planning depth (§4.1) and prospective survival (§4.2) — and the neutral or harmful results fall where the passive substrate already solves the task: the re-entry null (§6), the perception-mask variant (§4.2), and the delay-line match at spiking scale (§4.5). A self-referential loop is overhead on work the substrate can do directly, and improves performance only where reaction to input is not enough and the system must act on a model. This is the pattern FMT predicts if its explicit, self-referential layer is a generated simulation running on a faster reactive substrate, and it is why a null on an already-solvable task is consistent with the theory rather than a strike against it.

Criticality has not shown the complementary conditionality: the one rate-reservoir peak with a demand-gated shape replicates in shape but sits in the ordered regime, where a memory-capacity account predicts the same gating (§4.4). Whether operating at the edge pays remains untested, for want of a task deep enough to exercise the explicit-modelling mechanism. Closure pays only where the task is demanding enough to repay what it costs. The two framing moves of Section 3 exist to keep that conditionality in view. **No result here licenses "closure enables what feedforward cannot."** A finite closed loop unrolled over a finite horizon is feedforward plus memory, so any input–output capability is feedforward-approximable and a necessity claim is refutable by construction. What is measurable, and what is measured here, is sample efficiency at a fixed budget, and price at a fixed criterion.

The cost results of Section 5 say something the capability results cannot, and it runs in the same direction. Closure is not an expensive design decision a system makes: on this connectome family it is what recurrence already gives you, cheap to move and prohibitive to remove (§5.1). What is conditional is not its presence but its payoff — the advantage is large where the driven subsystem carries change the rest of the system cannot work out, small where it can, and never zero (§5.2). And the shape closure takes is selected rather than stipulated: minimising wiring cost under four independently-measured conditions returns a loop that reads the system's own state, without the derivation ever mentioning a self (§5.3). With the conditionality above, the picture is of an architecture that is cheap to arrive at, conditional in what it buys, and nowhere necessary — which is the claim shape the theory has committed to, and the shape a necessity result would have had to break.

## 8. Limitations and scope

It does not test phenomenality. Every dependent variable here is functional. The results speak to whether an architectural feature changes a measurable capability's cost, not to whether any system experiences anything. The other-minds problem applies to artefacts with full force, and FMT's own Hard-Problem account is explicitly partial (Gruber, 2026, §3.4.3, §7.1 Table 5 note ‡, §8.10).

The banked results are toy-scale mechanism demonstrations. They survived adversarial replication and pre-registered gates, but they run in rate reservoirs, spiking reservoirs, and small gridworlds. They establish that the components behave as the theory requires; they do not establish the scaled dependency, and we do not claim they do.

The scaled test of the central dependency is not attempted here, and stating it correctly matters as much as running it. It is tempting to state the open test as whether a scaled, closure-driven modeller *loses its modelling capability* when the loop is cut. That test cannot come out positive, and not for want of trying: a finite closed loop unrolled over a finite horizon is feedforward plus memory, so on a deterministic, stationary task a generic recurrent network can reconstruct a fixed input–output table without genuine self-re-entry — which a model-system result confirms. A capability-loss binary is therefore refutable by construction, and a programme that ran it would be scoring its own strongest claim against a standard the mathematics forbids. **The claim is instead about cost at matched function on an open-ended workload:** how much more it costs a cut system to reach the same behaviour, and whether that gap grows with the openness of the task. It will be reported separately on its own terms. Nothing here should be read as having settled it.

The strongest single-effect result is not evidence for FMT over rivals. The self/other transfer dissociation (§4.3) is shared with much of machine learning; it is feasibility scaffolding, pre-conceded as non-unique.

The cost results are structural, and one realization fact bounds what they may be read as saying. Section 5's figures are graph-structural or wiring-cost quantities on one synthetic connectome family, at region granularity; dynamical survival of what remains after a cut is unmeasured, and the fifth result prices the removal of closure without saying anything about what closure buys. Every loop arm in this program was simulated as the materialised dense product of its factors rather than as a wired bottleneck, so the factored bill supports storage and wiring claims and no dynamical or metabolic claim whatever. A physically wired bottleneck, with each relay built as a pair of cells because a matrix entry can be negative and a firing rate cannot, has since run on the spiking substrate: it reaches the same performance bar on all eight individuals with 39.5 times fewer connections than the rival without a bottleneck (575,488 against 22,744,284), at a lower ceiling (about 0.954 against 0.995). Its energy readings were taken on a task every arm already solves at ceiling and are withdrawn, so this run supports a wiring claim and no metabolic one. The seventh result is an optimality result and not a necessity one: it says where a bottleneck is cheapest, not that anything requires one.

Capability-first is a commitment, not a proof. Treating σ ≈ 1 and λ ≈ 0 as candidate instruments of one capability band (§3.2) is grounded in Kanders et al. (2017) and in the leaky-ESN scan. Whether either certifies that capability in our substrates is still under test, and the subsampled branching ratio has already failed as a stand-alone certificate: rate-matched unconnected units pass it.

FMT offers an architectural specification rather than an analogy (Gruber, 2026, §10.1); this program tests that specification.
