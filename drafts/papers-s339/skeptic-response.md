In September, *Skeptic* ran a debate on the hard problem of consciousness. Gary Whittenberger argued that it is soluble. Michael Shermer argued that it is "a known unknowable." By the end of the exchange the two had arrived at the same place from opposite directions. Asked how brain activity can give rise to feeling, Whittenberger answers: "It just does! It is a brute fact." Shermer closes with Steven Pinker: that brain circuitry feels like something "may have to be stipulated as a fact about reality where explanation stops." One calls that wall a solution and the other calls it a limit, and both are standing at it.

They end up there, I think, because both use the wrong relation between brain and experience: "produces" for Whittenberger, at one point an equals sign for Shermer. A third relation, familiar to anyone who has used a computer, removes part of the wall. The part that remains is small, and it can be located.

## The sum in the spreadsheet

A spreadsheet cell shows the sum of column B. The sum is real: change a number and the invoice goes out with a new total. Yet no transistor holds the sum, and no description in the vocabulary of voltages and logic gates contains it, however complete. The sum exists at the level of the running computation, which the chips generate and sustain. Nothing nonphysical is involved, and the sum is still not identical to any pattern of voltages.

The Four-Model Theory, which I have been developing, holds that experience relates to the brain in the same way. (The name comes from two kinds of models, one of the world and one of the self, each present in an implicit form stored in the brain's wiring and an explicit form that runs as a live simulation.) The brain runs a simulation of the world with a model of the organism inside it, and experience belongs to that running simulation. Neurons do not feel, any more than transistors add.

On this view each side of the debate is half right. Shermer is right that there is "no ghost in the machine": there is one physical system, and a complete physical description of it fixes every fact about experience. Whittenberger is right that "M is M, and B is B, but M is not B," in the sense that the vocabulary of minds does not appear anywhere in the vocabulary of neurons. That gap is what every computation has with respect to its hardware. It does not make the mental a second kind of process, and it does not make its arising a brute fact. For the same reason Shermer's "neural activity = qualia" is too strong, in the way that "voltage = sum" would be.

Whittenberger also writes that "there is a sense in which we are the machine rather than being inside the machine." On the theory, the machine does not feel. The simulation it runs does, and the "I" is the self-model within that simulation. So we are inside after all: inside the simulation the brain runs, with no homunculus in the skull.

What separates this from a weather simulation that nobody experiences? The theory's answer is closure: the self-model and the world-model each feed into the other's updating, so the system models itself as the thing doing the modeling. That is the step the theory asserts, and I return to it at the end.

## What pain is for

Whittenberger holds that "the mental has no causal influence on the physical; the mental is merely epiphenomenal." That position undermines itself. If his pain caused nothing physical, it played no part in his typing an article about pain, which would then read the same in a world where nobody had ever felt anything. David Chalmers discussed this in 1996 as the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and it applies here in full.

His own description of a burned finger points to the answer: "You remember the experience, learn from it, and devise ways to avoid it in the future." He may well be right that the finger moves before the pain arrives; that is a spinal reflex. Pain does its work afterwards, in what he lists as learning. On the Four-Model Theory, the simulation is the brain's way of evaluating consequences without having to pay them in full: replaying the stove, imagining the next encounter, learning from what happened to somebody else. An animal that can learn only by surviving the trial learns nothing from a lethal mushroom. An animal that can watch another eat one, and model the result, learns without taking the trial itself. Unconscious processing could in principle reach the same competence; the simulation makes it cheap. That is the theory's answer to Whittenberger's third question, why consciousness exists. The felt pain does causal work in the way the spreadsheet's sum does, as part of a physical process, described at its own level.

## Shermer's dolphin

Shermer is right that the full experience of being a dolphin requires being one, and right that "understanding how the brain machinery works is different from experiencing the brain machinery itself." The theory explains why. Some physical states can be had only by being in them, never by reading a description of them. A menu can describe a meal completely and feed nobody, and the menu and the meal are both physical.

That fact concerns the problem of other minds: whether I can have your experience. The hard problem asks something else, namely why there is experience at all and what it takes to have it. Jerry Coyne made this point in 2018, when Shermer ran the same argument with a bat: we may never know what it is like to be a bat, and we can still find out what it takes to be conscious. "A is A" settles the first question and leaves the second untouched.

The claim that inner states are "private and subjective … by definition" can also be tested in part. The theory predicts a split. The structure of experience, such as which colors look similar to which, should be shareable across brains. The absolute encoding, the state that is *my* red, should not. The first half already has support: Kawakita and colleagues (2025) aligned color-similarity judgments between groups of people without any shared color labels, and the structures matched. The prediction is that the same alignment works between individuals, while a decoder trained on one brain's red fails on another's. Strict privacy predicts that neither transfers. If the decoder transfers as well as the structure does, the theory is wrong on this point.

## Natural-born dualists

Both authors accept Paul Bloom's observation that we are "natural-born dualists." Whittenberger takes the intuition at face value: "we think that way because we are that way! The dualism is real!" The theory offers a different explanation. The self-model can represent that it is having an experience, but it cannot represent the machinery that generates the experience, because that machinery is not part of the simulation. A self-model built this way will conclude that something is missing from any physical account of it. The dualist intuition is what such an architecture predicts, which makes it evidence about how self-models are built and none at all for a second kind of stuff. Michael Graziano's attention schema theory makes a closely related move.

## What remains

One step in the theory is not derived. It is the step from "a system whose self-model and world-model are closed on each other" to "there is something it is like to be that system." I argue for it as the best available explanation, and I cannot prove it. Every theory of consciousness contains a bridge of this kind. The Four-Model Theory places its bridge at one architectural feature and rates its own treatment of the hard problem as partial.

The treatment covers why experience is not found in neurons, what it is for, why it is private in one respect and shareable in another, and why it seems mysterious from the inside. It leaves open why closure is lived rather than merely describable, a step testable in principle but not with current methods.

Whittenberger and Shermer agree that explanation stops somewhere. It stops somewhere in every theory. What differs is where. If it stops at "brains produce minds," the whole of experience is a brute fact. If it stops at closure, most of experience is explained, and the unexplained remainder is small enough to point at.
