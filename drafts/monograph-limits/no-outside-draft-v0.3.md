# No Outside

## The Limits of Mathematics and Logic From Any First-Person Perspective

Matthias Gruber

## Preface

The question behind this book began small. Mathematics is a language: do we know that? If it is one, do we know that a mathematical model describes what its author believes he is talking about? A model written in equations can expose logic that intuition would never find on its own, and that is the reason the formalism is worth having. A model also imports error, and I think it imports two different kinds. The first is the ordinary error that comes with any simplification. The second is quieter. Whenever the mathematics gets stuck, someone adds a new object to make it run again, and the object stays. After a few generations nobody can say which parts of the formalism describe the world and which parts were added to keep the formalism working.

Followed to its end, the question reaches much further than mathematics. Mathematics and logic are languages in which observers who model themselves serialize the regularities of their world-models. Every such observer is a proper part of what it describes, runs on fewer resources than the whole, and cannot represent, as a whole, the apparatus that produces its own representations. From those three facts the familiar limits follow: incompleteness and undecidability, the uncomputable continuum, the impossibility of certifying randomness or independence from inside an experiment, the inability to sort surplus structure from world structure, and the failure of first-person evidence to decide between hypotheses about one's own origin. One principle underlies all of them: the generator never appears completely in what it generates. It can appear in part — a formal system can encode its own proofs as numbers — but a complete appearance would cost either consistency or a regress, because a frame that appears whole as its own content needs a further frame to hold it. At the end of the argument even the distinction between a description and an instance turns out to depend on the side of a generating relation from which it is drawn.

"First-person perspective" here means any embedded perspective: a human, an animal, a machine, or a creature inside a structure laid out statically on a disk. None of the limits is a human weakness that better engineering could remove. Each holds for every perspective there can be, because no perspective is outside.

Negative results travel between perspectives where positive ones do not: an inconsistency between a system and its own admitted input can be shown locally, and every successor system that keeps the input inherits it. Relational structure is shareable where absolute encoding is not, and mathematics is the most shareable part of a world-model because it keeps structure and drops encoding. A regularity that holds within a scope is compressed data about that scope, and discarding it leaves a deeper theory with nothing to reproduce. And every theory, my own included, rests on cuts it cannot fully sustain — implicit against explicit, time against space, boundary against projection — which an embedded mind has to make in order to understand anything at all. Using such a cut is legitimate. Mistaking it for the world is the same error as the patched formalism, committed by a theorist about his own tools.

Two earlier works of mine run through the argument: the Four-Model Theory of consciousness (Gruber, 2026a), which grew out of a German monograph (Gruber, 2015), and a cellular-automaton cosmology (Gruber, 2026b). Both carry the principle already, the first at the scale of the organism and the second at the scale of the cosmos. Neither applies it to mathematics and logic.

# Part I — Mathematics as a Language of Models

# Chapter 1 — Is Mathematics a Language?

## A language with a proof checker

In 2015 I wrote, in a book about consciousness, that mathematics too is at bottom a language, "if a very strictly regulated one" (*"auch die Mathematik ist im Grunde eine Sprache, wenn auch eine sehr streng geregelte"*; Gruber, 2015, p. 18). The example given there was deliberately trivial. Whether 1 + 1 equals 2, 0 or 10 depends on the system in which the symbols are read: 2 in ordinary arithmetic, 0 in arithmetic modulo 2, 10 in binary notation. The marks on the page are the same in all three cases. What differs is the set of rules the reader brings to them, and those rules belong to the language, not to the marks.

Calling mathematics a language does not make it arbitrary. What sets it apart is the strictness of its grammar. In a natural language one can say almost anything, including something that contradicts the previous sentence, and the language raises no objection. Mathematics comes with a built-in proof checker: a statement is admitted only if it can be derived from what was already admitted, by rules that everyone using the language has agreed to in advance. The result is a language in which some things cannot be said at all. That restriction is the source of its power. A derivation that passes the checker can be trusted by anyone who accepts the rules, without trusting the person who wrote it, and it can carry a line of reasoning far beyond the point where intuition would lose track.

The power is easy to underrate because its products become familiar. When Maxwell wrote the laws of electricity and magnetism in a single system of equations, the equations admitted wave solutions travelling at a speed fixed by two constants measured on laboratory benches, and that speed matched the measured speed of light. Nobody had intuited that light was an electromagnetic wave. The formalism said so, by derivation, and experiment agreed. This is what a language with a checker can do that a language without one cannot: it lets the consequences of a model be computed by people who could not have imagined them.

Sometimes the people who could not imagine them include the author. Einstein's field equations of 1915, applied to the universe as a whole, did not admit a static universe without an added term, and Friedmann showed in 1922 that without one they make the cosmos expand or contract. Einstein believed it was static, and in 1917 he added a term, the cosmological constant, whose only job was to hold it still. When the expansion of the universe was observed a decade later, the term lost its reason and was dropped. The equations had exposed something their author's intuition refused, and the author had patched them to agree with his intuition. Some seven decades after it was dropped, the term came back, this time to fit the measured acceleration of the expansion. The same symbol has been a patch and then a fitted parameter, and its formal role never changed.

The same restriction fixes which questions can be asked. Every language makes some distinctions easy and others awkward, and a formal language does so with more force than a natural one, because what cannot be written in it cannot be derived in it either. Whoever's toolkit is differential equations sees dynamical systems everywhere; whoever's toolkit is graph theory sees networks. Neither sees wrongly. Each sees what its instrument can register, and what the instrument cannot register leaves no visible gap. A field that formalizes early therefore tends to defend its equations at the moment it should be revisiting its concepts, because the equations are what it knows how to check and the concepts are not.

## What a mathematical statement means

To say what a statement in such a language means, a definition of meaning is needed. The one I use comes from the 2015 book, where it was built as a chain of four steps so that each term could be stated without the next. Data are physical differences that a reading device, an organism or a machine, can register. Information is data to which a coding can be assigned, a coding being a reversible transformation. Knowledge is information that has been assigned to a reference system. The meaning of a piece of information is its relation to a chosen reference system (Gruber, 2015, pp. 12–27). The same book then equates the reference system with a model: for the purposes of the theory, *Bezugssystem* and *Modell* can be treated as one (Gruber, 2015, p. 21).

Two consequences follow at once. A mathematical statement has meaning only relative to a model. "1 + 1 = 10" is false in one reference system and true in another, and nothing in the string decides which. And the formalism is a serialization of model structure. Communication on this account is serialization: a sender holding a model linearizes part of it into a sequence of symbols, and the receiver uses the sequence to reconstruct a model of its own. Natural language does this loosely, so the reconstruction varies from reader to reader. Mathematics does it under discipline. Its symbols and rules are chosen so that the reconstruction comes out the same for every competent reader, and the proof checker is what enforces the sameness.

"Competent" carries a condition. A receiver can reconstruct a model from a sequence of symbols only if it already holds a model base the sequence can be assigned to. A page of tensor calculus means nothing to a reader without years of training, however clearly it is written, because the page is a set of instructions for rebuilding structure, and the instructions presuppose structure the reader must bring. Mathematical communication succeeds as well as it does because those who practise it have been trained into closely similar reference systems, so that their reconstructions agree. The agreement is real and it is valuable. It is also an agreement between models, and it says nothing by itself about whether the models agree with anything else.

So a mathematical model describes what its author is talking about only to the extent that the reader's reconstruction matches the author's model, and the formal discipline guarantees that match only for the structure the formalism actually encodes. It guarantees nothing about the rest. Two readers can agree on every derivation in a theory and still disagree about what the theory is about, because the formalism carries relations between its objects and leaves open what the objects are. Mathematical logic has a precise version of this: formal theories of arithmetic have models that are not the natural numbers, structures that satisfy every axiom and contain elements no counting ever reaches. The axioms were written with the natural numbers in mind, and the axioms cannot say so.

The same account explains why a formal text carries more meaning than its direct interpretation, and why the surplus varies so much from case to case. The number 1.2 has many meanings across contexts: a price, a ratio, a length in any unit, a version of a document. The speed of light, taken as the physical constant, has almost none beyond itself; wherever it is used as that constant it refers to the same thing. How much a mathematical object means depends on how many models it is used in and how differently they are built. And a corpus of mathematics written by model-bearing systems is a record of their model structure. Each formula serializes part of a model, so a large enough body of formulas preserves the structure that produced them, including structure that no single author set out to write down.

## Two kinds of error

If a formalism serializes a model, it can be wrong in the ways a model can be wrong, and there are two.

The first is ordinary modeling error. Every model simplifies; that is what makes it a model and not a copy. The Four-Model Theory says this of itself: it is a model, and every model has modeling error (Gruber, 2026a, §10.3). Ordinary modeling error is visible in principle. One can say what was left out, estimate how much it matters, and refine the model when the residue grows large enough to measure.

The second kind I call patch error. A formalism meets a case it cannot handle, and instead of revising the concepts underneath, someone adds an object that makes the case come out right. The textbook example is the epicycle. Circular orbits did not fit the observed motions of the planets, so circles were set on circles until they did. The scheme survived for centuries because it could not easily fail: enough circles stacked on circles can approximate any closed path, so every new discrepancy could be absorbed by one more circle. Its success was a property of the method, and it said nothing about the sky.

The two kinds of error differ in what happens to the residue. Ordinary modeling error leaves one: the gap between prediction and measurement stays visible, and it points at what the model left out. A patch consumes the residue. The discrepancy that would have exposed the inadequate concept is absorbed by the added object, and the signal that something is wrong disappears along with the discrepancy. That makes patch error the more dangerous of the two. A model with ordinary error tells its users where it fails. A patched model has been adjusted so that it no longer can.

Renormalization in quantum field theory is the modern case most often named. Infinities appear in the calculations and are removed by a procedure that then yields predictions of extraordinary precision. Dirac never accepted it, and Feynman, who helped to build it, called it a "dippy process". Whether renormalization is a patch or a discovery about how descriptions at different scales relate to each other is still argued. Two facts are not in dispute: it was introduced to rescue a formalism, and the formalism alone could not say which of the two it was.

Philosophy has names for the pattern. Lakatos (1976) traced how mathematicians defended Euler's formula for polyhedra — vertices minus edges plus faces equals two — against counterexamples such as a solid with a tunnel through it. One response was monster-barring: narrow the definition of "polyhedron" until the counterexample no longer counts, and the theorem survives at the cost of its content. The Duhem–Quine thesis states the general form. A hypothesis is never tested alone, so a failed prediction can always be absorbed by changing one of the auxiliary assumptions that accompanied it. Redhead (1975) named the residue that such moves leave behind: surplus structure, mathematical structure in a physical theory that corresponds to nothing physical. The standard example is gauge freedom. The electromagnetic potential can be changed by adding the gradient of any function at all, and the measurable fields do not change. The formalism carries an infinity of distinct potentials for one physical situation, and only the fields are measured.

All of this is well known in philosophy and rarely taught to the people who use the formalisms. Recognising a patch does not help anyone solve the next problem, and for a working scientist that settles the matter.

## Where the damage happens

It is tempting to say that patches corrupt the mathematics. They mostly do not. Complex numbers entered algebra as a device for solving cubic equations and were regarded with suspicion for a long time. As a mathematical structure they are pairs of real numbers with a particular rule of multiplication, and they are consistent if the real numbers are consistent. They are also a conservative extension: nothing that can be proved about the real numbers alone changes when the complex numbers are added. The same holds for most patches. An object introduced to rescue a calculation is usually a perfectly good mathematical object, and the extended theory is as consistent as the one it extends.

The damage happens in the mapping from formalism to world. Before a patch, a formalism had a reading in which each of its structures stood for something. After it, the formalism contains structure that was added for its own sake, and nothing inside it marks which structure that is. Some physicists take the wavefunction literally and others treat it as bookkeeping. A potential turns out to be a gauge choice. An infinity is subtracted, and the subtraction is then described as a physical effect. In each case the mathematics is in order, and the question of what it is about has become harder to answer than it was before the patch went in.

An added object is not always a patch, and nothing about the act of adding it tells which it is. In the nineteenth century the orbit of Uranus deviated from what Newtonian gravity predicted. Le Verrier added an object: an unseen planet whose pull would account for the deviation, with a computed position. Neptune was found close to that position. Thirteen years later he tried the same move on the anomalous precession of Mercury's orbit and proposed another unseen planet, Vulcan, inside Mercury's orbit. Vulcan was searched for and never found, and the anomaly was explained decades later by general relativity, which revised the concept of gravity underneath. The two moves were formally identical. Each added one body to save a theory. One was a discovery and the other a patch, and no inspection of the equations could have told them apart. What told them apart was that one object was seen at the predicted place and the other was not.

The same test applies to renormalization, and it explains why its status is still disputed. The procedure was introduced to remove infinities, which makes it look like a patch. But renormalized quantum electrodynamics then predicted quantities it had not been fitted to, among them the magnetic moment of the electron, and those predictions agree with measurement to many decimal places. An object that predicts something the formalism without it would not have predicted has moved some way from surplus towards structure. How far it has moved is the open question, and it is a question about the world, which the formalism alone cannot answer.

Patches are hard to see for a reason that has nothing to do with the people who introduce them. Whoever adds an object usually knows that it was added and why. The next generation learns the formalism with the object already inside it, and learns it as part of the structure, because that is how the textbook presents it and because the calculations need it. By the third generation the knowledge that the object was once a repair has gone. What remains is a formalism that works, taught by people who have never seen it without the patch, to students who have no reason to ask which of its parts were there first.

The correction sharpens the original suspicion. The claim that patched formalisms are inconsistent would be false and easy to refute. The claim that they cannot tell us what they describe is true and harder to escape, and it gives the reading limit: no formalism certifies its own ontological reading. A formal system can check its own derivations. It has no internal means of checking whether a given structure in it belongs to the world or to the representation, because that question concerns the relation between the system and something it is not, and the system contains only itself.

Two procedures sort surplus from structure. Conceptual analysis asks of each object what role it plays and whether the role could be played without it. Experiment asks whether the object predicts anything that the formalism without it would not. Both are carried out by someone, from somewhere, with a model already in hand: the analyst brings concepts that are themselves a model, and the experimenter brings instruments whose readings mean something only relative to a theory of the instrument. Neither procedure can be carried out from outside every perspective, because there is no such place to carry it out from. The sorting is always provisional and always local, for every formalism and every analyst.

## A rule for the science of mind

The sciences of mind are where these errors cost most. Their concepts are less settled than those of physics, and the experimental check that keeps physical patches honest is weaker, so a formalism attached to an unclear concept can survive on its elegance. Settle the concepts and their logic before formalizing, and admit a new mathematical object only if it predicts something new or is labelled as bookkeeping.

# Chapter 2 — Counting and Its Imports

## Only the natural numbers count

Numbers began as a way of counting things, and only one kind of number does that. One sheep, two sheep, three: the natural numbers answer the question "how many?", and their rules of computation are what counting does when it is done in bulk. Addition is counting on; multiplication is counting groups of equal size. Every rule of arithmetic on the natural numbers can be read back as a fact about collections of things.

Even here there is one idealization, and it returns later at cosmic scale. The natural numbers never end: every number has a successor. Counting has never been done that way. Nobody has counted to ten to the power of a hundred, and there may not be that many things in the observable universe to count. The endless successor is a rule extrapolated from every count anyone has made, and it is a harmless one, because any particular count stays finite and the rules hold for all of them. But it already describes more than any counter could do. The infinity of the natural numbers is countable and every member of it is reachable in principle, which separates it from the imports further up. It is still an extrapolation beyond anything observed.

Every extension of the number system beyond this point imports something that counting does not supply. Each kind of number that is added carries a small model with it, a set of rules that fit some situation other than counting, and the rules are adopted because they fit that situation. Calling all of these objects "numbers" hides the import. It suggests that the same thing has merely been made more general, when what has happened is that a new model has been attached, with its own rules of computation, and given the old name.

The complex numbers make this most obvious, which is why they attract the suspicion. They are neither numbers in the counting sense nor simply pairs of numbers. They are objects that obey different rules of computation, mini-models of a particular kind of transformation. But the complex numbers are only the most visible case. Each step on the ladder imports a model, and the import is larger at some steps than others.

## The ladder of imports

Negative numbers model debt or direction. There is no collection of minus three sheep. There is a debt of three sheep, or a position three steps to the left of a chosen origin, and the rule that minus times minus gives plus is a rule about reversing a reversal. The model is simple and its fit is good, and still it is a model: someone who owes nothing and owns nothing has zero sheep, and the negative numbers say what the state of a ledger is, not what is in the field.

The rationals model the division of wholes. Half a loaf is a loaf divided into two parts, one of which is taken. The rules for adding fractions are the rules for bringing divisions to a common size before counting the parts. Again the fit is good wherever wholes can be divided evenly, and again it is a model: it assumes that division can go on as long as one likes, which is false for loaves and sheep and, as far as anyone knows, false for anything physical below some scale.

The real numbers model an idealized continuum, and here the import becomes large. A real number can be defined as a Dedekind cut: a division of all rational numbers into a lower and an upper set, with every member of the lower set smaller than every member of the upper. The square root of two is the cut between rationals whose square is less than two and those whose square is greater. The construction is rigorous and it gives the continuum the property that analysis needs, that every bounded sequence of increasing values has a limit. It also gives the continuum uncountably many members: there is no list, however long, that contains all real numbers. Counting, the activity numbers were invented for, cannot enumerate them.

The complex numbers model rotation with scaling. Formally a complex number is a pair of reals, *a* and *b*, written *a* + *bi*, with a multiplication rule under which *i* times *i* is minus one. Equivalently, each complex number is a two-by-two matrix of reals with *a* in both diagonal places and *b* and minus *b* in the two off-diagonal places, and multiplying two such matrices is the same as multiplying the complex numbers. Read geometrically, multiplying by a complex number rotates a point in the plane by some angle and stretches its distance from the origin by some factor. Multiplying by *i* is a quarter turn. Doing it twice is a half turn, which sends every point to its opposite, and that is all "*i* squared equals minus one" says.

That the rules come from the modeled situation, and not from some general notion of number, is shown by what happened when Hamilton tried to go one step further. Complex numbers model rotations in a plane. Hamilton looked for a similar system for rotations in three-dimensional space and found that it could not be built from triples. The system that works, the quaternions, needs four components, and its multiplication is not commutative: *a* times *b* is in general different from *b* times *a*. For anyone who took quaternions to be numbers this was a scandal, since commutativity had seemed part of what multiplication means. For a model of rotation it is what one should expect. Rotations in space do not commute — turning a book a quarter turn about one axis and then another leaves it in a different position from doing the same turns in the opposite order — and a model of them has to inherit that. The multiplication rule of the quaternions is a fact about rotations in space, written in the costume of arithmetic.

## The ladder gets worse upward

The natural reading of this ladder is that suspicion should grow as one climbs it: integers are harmless, rationals mostly so, reals a little exotic, complex numbers suspicious. On the account given here the order of suspicion is different, and the largest import sits one rung below the complex numbers.

Turing (1936) defined a computable number as one whose digits an algorithm can produce, as many of them as one asks for. Every number one has ever written down or calculated with is computable in this sense: the square root of two, pi, *e*, any rational, any value a physical theory has ever predicted. But algorithms are finite descriptions over a finite alphabet, so there are only countably many of them, and therefore only countably many computable numbers. The real numbers are uncountable. So almost all real numbers are uncomputable. No finite procedure produces their digits, no finite description singles them out, and no one will ever write one down. The continuum consists overwhelmingly of objects that no process running for any finite time could generate. Turing's own result yields an example: a number whose successive binary digits record, program by program, whether each program in a fixed enumeration ever halts. The number is perfectly well defined, and an algorithm producing its digits would solve the halting problem, which Turing proved no algorithm can do.

The uncomputable reals are also inaccessible to measurement. Every measurement returns finitely many digits, within some error. A measured value is therefore compatible with uncountably many real numbers, and it is equally compatible with a computable one, or with a rational. No experiment at any finite precision can show that a physical quantity has a real value as opposed to a computable approximation of one at that precision. The continuum enters physics through the formalism and never through the data, and an observer inside the world has no measurement that could detect it.

Physics has used the continuum for three centuries with enormous success, and that success shows that the continuum is a good model of something. It does not show that the world contains uncountably many positions between any two points. If the world is a computation, as the cellular-automaton cosmology I have proposed assumes (Gruber, 2026b), then every physical quantity is in principle the output of a finite process, and the uncomputable reals correspond to nothing at all. The continuum is then the largest imported fiction in physics, far larger than *i*. The complex numbers add a rule for rotation to the reals; the reals add uncountably many objects to a world that could at most contain countably many. A critique of mathematical imports that stops at the complex numbers is aimed one rung too high.

This does not make continuous mathematics wrong. A differential equation is an excellent compression of the behaviour of very many very small parts, and the compression is valid wherever the parts are small and numerous enough. What the compression cannot do is certify that its idealization is the world. That is the limit of the previous chapter, appearing at the foundation of nearly every physical theory: the formalism works, and its working does not say which of its structures are there.

## Numbers that are algorithms

Turing's definition says more than it first appears to. A computable number is identified by the algorithm that produces it. The square root of two, for the purposes of computation, is a procedure that generates digits, and two procedures that generate the same digits define the same number. In this sense some numbers are algorithms, and the exchange between them is routine: a programmer stores a constant as a function that computes it on demand, and a mathematician defines pi by a series.

The exchange runs in the other direction as well: every algorithm can be written as a finite string and coded as a single natural number, and that coding, which looks like bookkeeping, is what lets a formal system describe its own workings and then limits what it can prove about itself (Chapter 5).

The exchange goes further when one looks at what multiplication by a number does. Multiplying by *e* raised to *i* times theta rotates every point of the plane by the angle theta. Multiplication by this object is a rotation operator wearing the costume of a number. The notation presents it as one more value, of the same kind as 3 or 1.2, and the operation it performs is a geometric transformation that counting never contained. When physicists write a quantity as a complex amplitude and multiply it by a phase factor, the arithmetic looks like multiplication and does the work of rotation. The costume hides what is being done, and so we trick ourselves: the formalism looks simpler than the model it contains, and the simplicity is a property of the notation.

The point needs one correction to hold. An algorithm that represents a value is not identical to that value under every interpretation. A procedure that outputs the digits of pi and the number pi agree on every digit, and they differ in how one accesses them: the procedure has to be run, takes time, and gives only finitely many digits in any finite time. Multiplying by an encoded constant is not free computation either. Someone has to encode the constant, carry it at some precision, and decode the result, and each step has a cost that the notation omits. The omission matters most where the continuum is taken literally. A machine that could store an arbitrary real number in a physical quantity and read it out exactly could store the halting number above and answer questions no algorithm can answer. Proposals for such machines fail at exactly the steps the notation leaves out: no physical quantity is known to hold infinitely many digits, and no readout extracts them. So the trick does not come from numbers and programs being the same thing. It comes from places where the costume hides an operation, so that the reader of a formula sees a value where the formula performs a transformation.

## The complex numbers in quantum theory

Quantum theory is where the complex numbers seem least avoidable, and where the question of whether they belong to the world or to the representation has been tested most directly.

The first test came in the form of a theorem about real-valued formulations. Renou et al. (2021) showed that a version of quantum theory using only real numbers, combined with the standard rule for composing systems — the tensor product — makes predictions that differ from those of complex quantum theory in a specific network experiment, and experiments of that kind have since come out on the side of the complex theory (Li et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2022). Taken at face value, this says that nature uses complex numbers. What it shows is narrower: real quantum theory *with the standard composition rule* fails. The composition rule is an additional assumption, and it can be changed. Later real-vector-space formulations with a modified rule for combining systems reproduce the predictions of complex quantum theory (Hoffreumon & Woods, 2025). So "experiment rules out real quantum theory" is too strong. What experiment rules out is one particular way of building it.

The second observation concerns what the successful real formulations contain. They are real in name only: they carry an operator *J* whose square is the negative of the identity, and *J* enters the time evolution (Hoffreumon & Woods, 2025). *J* is multiplication by *i* renamed. Remove the symbol *i*, and its role comes back as an operator that does the same thing. What survives every change of notation is a represented structure: something that acts as the generator of rotation in a two-dimensional plane of states. The notation can be changed at will; the structure cannot be removed without losing the predictions.

This can look like a defeat for scepticism about the complex numbers. It confirms instead that *i* was a packaged structure from the start, a model of rotation and not a number in the counting sense, since renaming it does not remove it. And it leaves open the question that matters. Nothing in any of the formulations says whether that generator of rotation is fundamental, a feature of the world at its lowest level, or emergent, the large-scale form of something that is not rotation at all.

The third step is my reading of what that something could be. In a world whose substrate is a cellular automaton and whose spacetime is a holographic projection of a lower-dimensional computation, continuous rotation and continuous phase need not exist at the bottom. A lattice has no continuous rotational symmetry. Rotating it by an arbitrary small angle does not map it onto itself, and the symmetry reappears only approximately, at scales large compared with the lattice spacing. What such a substrate does have is oscillators: subsystems that cycle through a finite sequence of states and return. 't Hooft (2016), in his cellular-automaton interpretation of quantum mechanics, works with models of exactly this kind, in which classical states evolve by permutation and periodic orbits play the role that phase plays in the quantum description. On that picture a quantum phase is the position of an oscillator in its cycle, and the continuous phase of the formalism is a large-scale idealization of discrete cycles.

Stated that way, the disagreement with standard physics is narrower than it first sounds. Quantum field theory already describes the world as coupled oscillators, so the claim that there are only oscillators agrees with the physical content of *J* and adds one claim about it: that the phase is discrete at the bottom and continuous only in the limit. That is an argument against the continuum, the largest of the imports on the ladder, and it says nothing against interference. Interference is measured. It is data that any substrate theory has to reproduce, and a theory that called interference a fiction would have discarded the evidence it needs to be tested.

## The costume problem

A formalism that works hides which of its objects are the world and which are the costume, and its working is the reason it hides them so well. The only remedy available from inside is the one Chapter 1 arrived at, applied object by object: ask of each object what it predicts, and treat as costume whatever predicts nothing.

# Chapter 3 — Why Mathematics Works Where It Works

## The puzzle

Wigner (1960) called it the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences. Mathematical structures developed for their own sake, with no physical application in view, turn out years or centuries later to describe physical phenomena with precision far beyond the data that suggested them. Complex numbers, introduced to solve cubic equations, become the language of quantum mechanics. Group theory, developed to study which equations can be solved by radicals, becomes the language of particle physics. Wigner offered no explanation.

The effectiveness has more than one source, and each accounts for part of it. Three of them explain why mathematics works, and together they make it reasonable within a scope. A fourth explains why its working cannot prove what it seems to prove.

## Selection on the observer

The first answer concerns where we are. We live in a part of the universe in which simple logic works. If we did not, there would be no brains to notice. A brain survives by predicting likely futures from present evidence: where the predator will be, whether the fruit is ripe, what happens if one steps on that branch. Prediction from limited evidence works only where the world has regularities that can be extracted from a small sample and extended to unseen cases. A world without such regularities produces no organisms that rely on prediction, and so no mathematicians. That simple logic works where we find ourselves is therefore a condition of our being there to find it.

The same condition has an upper bound. A world in which simple logic worked perfectly, in which everything was predictable from a short description, would leave nothing for a predictor to do and nothing for success or failure to mean. Life as a competition between better and worse predictions needs a world that is regular enough to be predicted and uncertain enough that prediction is hard. We live in a subset of the universe that has both properties, and we could not live in one that lacked either.

The kind of regularity that selection requires is specific. A predictor needs regularities that hold tomorrow as they held today, and that hold here as they hold a short distance away, because it learns in one place and time and acts in another. The laws physics has found are of that kind: the same everywhere and at every time within their scope, and local, relating what happens at a point to what happens next to it. That this is the form our mathematics of nature takes is what selection on the observer would lead one to expect. It is the form of regularity a learner can extract from local experience.

This is anthropic selection, and it works on the observer, not on the mathematics. It explains why observers who find mathematics effective exist. It does not explain why any particular piece of mathematics works, and it does not say that the whole universe is as regular as our part of it. The subset in which simple logic works may be a small and special region of something much larger and much less regular, and selection would put us inside it either way.

## Selection on the mathematics

The second answer concerns the mathematics. Mathematicians have explored a vast range of consistent structures, far more than physics has ever used. Most of them describe nothing physical and are remembered only by specialists. When physics needs a structure, it looks through this stock, and it usually finds something that fits, because the stock is large. Afterwards the fit is remembered and the many structures that fit nothing are not.

This is coverage plus survivorship. Its effect is to make the effectiveness look larger than it is. The story of group theory and particle physics is told often; the structures that turned out to describe nothing physical have no story, because nothing happened to them. The selection is real, and it removes a large part of Wigner's surprise. It does not remove all of it. Selection explains why something in the stock fits. It does not explain why the stock contains anything that fits so well, or why the fit, once found, keeps holding at scales and precisions far beyond those at which it was chosen.

## Mathematics as serialized compression

The third answer comes from the Four-Model Theory and from the account of meaning in Chapter 1, and it addresses what the first two leave open.

A world-model is a compression. An organism cannot store its world; it stores regularities, which let it reconstruct and predict much from little. The Four-Model Theory describes the brain as building such models in two kinds, implicit ones grown into the substrate and explicit ones run as a simulation, and the explicit kind is where reasoning and language happen (Gruber, 2026a). Mathematics, on the account of Chapter 1, is a serialization of world-model structure. It writes the regularities down in a form that other model-bearing systems can reconstruct and check. Its abstract structures are abstractions from regularities that minds had already compressed, extended by rules that preserve the structure of the compression.

On this view the effectiveness is no surprise inside its scope. Mathematics works on the kind of thing world-models were built to compress, because it is a written form of those compressions. When a structure developed "for its own sake" later fits a physical phenomenon, the structure was not developed out of nothing. It was developed by minds whose concepts of space, quantity, symmetry and change were already compressions of the physical world they live in, and the abstract development preserved those regularities while generalizing them. That a generalization of a compression of the world should fit more of the world is reasonable.

Geometry shows the pattern in one line of descent. It began as the measurement of land, a compression of the spatial regularities that surveyors and builders relied on, and Euclid wrote those regularities down as axioms. For two thousand years the parallel postulate looked like the one axiom that ought to follow from the others. When mathematicians in the nineteenth century dropped it, they obtained consistent geometries of curved space, with no physical application in view. General relativity later found that the space around masses is curved in exactly this way. The non-Euclidean geometries were Euclid's compression of everyday space with one constraint removed, and the constraint that was removed turned out to be the one that held only approximately, at the scales where surveyors work.

The same view predicts where mathematics should fall silent. It says nothing beyond the scope of the compression. Where the world contains structure that no world-model of ours was built to compress — because no organism ever needed to predict it, or because it lies beyond what any finite compressor can hold — mathematics has no special purchase. There its effectiveness is simply absent, and the absence shows up only as a domain where our theories stop working and we cannot tell why.

The pattern of success and failure inside the world we can access fits this account. Mathematics is most effective on systems that are either very simple or very numerous: two bodies in orbit, or ten to the twenty-three molecules in a box of gas. In the first case the system is small enough for its regularities to be written down exactly. In the second, the parts are so many and so alike that their individual behaviour averages out, and what remains is a regularity of the aggregate. Between those extremes lie organisms, brains, weather beyond a few days, economies — systems with many parts that are neither few nor interchangeable. There the formalism retreats to statistics, to rules of thumb, or to simulation.

Simulation is the telling case. For a system whose behaviour is computationally irreducible, no shortcut to its future exists; the only way to learn what it will do is to run it, step by step (Gruber, 2026b, §2.3). For Rule 30, a one-line cellular-automaton rule (Chapter 5), no shortcut shorter than running it is known. Mathematics in the classical sense — a closed-form expression that delivers the answer without the work — is not available for such systems. What remains is the rule itself, and the rule can only be executed. Where our world contains irreducible processes, mathematics can state the rule and say nothing shorter about the outcome. That is a limit of compression, and it holds for any observer whatever, because the absence of a shortcut belongs to the process, whoever is looking for one.

## The ceiling

The fourth answer comes from the cosmological side, and it limits what the first three can establish.

The cellular-automaton cosmology I have proposed sorts the behaviour of rule-based systems into five classes (Gruber, 2026b, §2). Classes 1 to 4 are what finite, expressible rules can produce from finite data: stable states, cycles, reducible patterns, and Class 4, the irreducible complex behaviour of systems such as Rule 110 or Conway's Game of Life, which can support universal computation. Class 5 is ontic randomness, output that no expressible rule generates. Class 4 is therefore the expressibility ceiling of finite rules: the most complex behaviour that anything that can be written down can produce (Gruber, 2026b, §2.4).

If observers are themselves Class 4 systems, and a brain running open-ended computation inside a Class 4 universe would be one, then the most complex pattern they can detect or formalize is Class 4. A world above the ceiling, if it had Class 4 regions, would appear to observers in those regions as Class 4 throughout, because Class 4 is the most complex pattern they have the means to register, and whatever lies above it would be read as noise, as complexity of the familiar kind, or not at all (Gruber, 2026b, §9.5).

There is a more concrete form of the same worry. Human perception is strongly tuned to symmetry: bilateral symmetry is a reliable cue that something is an animal, predator or prey, and the visual system detects it quickly and before attention is directed to it. A large part of mathematical physics consists of symmetry principles, and a cosmology such as mine is at bottom a symmetry claim, the same boundary structure at every scale. Symmetry may be everywhere in the world. It may also be what minds tuned to symmetry find wherever they look. From inside, the two are indistinguishable, because the evidence for the first passes through the apparatus that would produce the second.

This changes what the success of mathematics can certify. It cannot certify that the world is mathematical. It certifies that the world, as accessed by observers of this kind, is compressible to their ceiling. The difference between those two claims cannot be detected from inside, because detecting it would need a pattern above the ceiling, and observers below it have none. The same objection applies to my own cosmology: it is a Class 4 picture of the universe drawn by Class 4 minds, and if the universe is something more, those minds would draw exactly this picture and be wrong. I do not know how to resolve this objection from within, and I am not sure it can be resolved from within.

The objection has a general structure. The cognitive apparatus that detects patterns contributes to every pattern it detects, and it cannot appear among the patterns, as a whole, as a separate item that could be subtracted. To check how much of the observed order comes from the world and how much from the observer, one would need to observe the observer's whole contribution from somewhere else. There is nowhere else. Parts of the apparatus can be studied inside the picture; the apparatus that is generating the picture, taken as a whole, is what the picture cannot contain.

The ceiling is not the same claim as selection on the observer. Selection says we live in a regular region. The ceiling says that even if we did not — even if the region were less regular than it looks — we would see as much regularity as our class permits and could not see past it. Selection explains the effectiveness. The ceiling explains why the effectiveness, however large, proves nothing about the world beyond our access to it.

## The opposite pole

Tegmark (2008) answers Wigner from the opposite direction. On his Mathematical Universe Hypothesis, physical reality is a mathematical structure, and every consistent mathematical structure exists. Mathematics is effective because there is nothing else for it to describe. We find ourselves in a structure complex enough to contain observers, and that structure is all there is to our world.

The perspective argument does not need this and does not support it. It explains effectiveness by selection: of observers, of mathematics, of the regularities that world-models compress. Selection works whether or not all structures exist; it needs only that observers exist in some structure and model what they can of it. The cosmology I have proposed makes the same choice at its foundation: its elimination argument selects one class of dynamics from the deterministic candidates, as the class consistent with what we observe, and admits no others (Gruber, 2026b, §3.2). Tegmark's hypothesis is maximally permissive where this argument is selective.

The hypothesis also takes on a debt the perspective argument avoids. If every consistent structure exists, almost all of them are vastly more complicated than the one we observe, and most observers inside them would see something other than simple laws. Explaining why we see simple laws then requires a measure over structures and observers, which the hypothesis does not supply. And it runs into the ceiling in a specific way: it claims that the world is mathematical in exactly the sense the ceiling says no embedded observer can establish. It may be true. Nothing available from inside could show it.

## Very little structure

How much structure does a universe need? One case suggests that it could be very little, given the right implementation.

The prime numbers are as simple a structure as mathematics has: the whole numbers divisible only by one and themselves, defined in a sentence. Their distribution is governed by the zeros of the Riemann zeta function. Montgomery (1973) studied how those zeros are spaced along their critical line and derived, under the Riemann hypothesis and for a restricted range, a formula for the statistical correlation between pairs of them, a formula that Freeman Dyson recognised as the one describing pairs of eigenvalues of large random Hermitian matrices. Odlyzko (1987) computed very many zeros and found the spacings matching the random-matrix prediction closely. The same random-matrix statistics describe the energy levels of chaotic quantum systems without time-reversal symmetry. Nobody knows why. From the definition of a prime, with nothing physical put in, comes a structure with the statistical signature of quantum chaos.

The match needs one qualification before anything is built on it. Random-matrix statistics are not specific to the primes and quantum chaos. Spacing laws of the same random-matrix family turn up in the energy levels of heavy nuclei and in many other systems with no common mechanism, which suggests that they describe a broad class of complicated structures and not any one of them. The zeta zeros belong to that class. What they show is that a very simple definition can land in the same statistical class as complicated physical systems. They are no model of any particular physics, and the match by itself says nothing about a shared mechanism.

My reading is that very little structure can lead to a universe like ours, given the correct implementation. The case shows that quantum-like statistics need little input: a definition one sentence long carries them already.

Two things limit the claim. First, the primes are static. They are a fixed set, not a process; their zeros do not evolve, and a universe that contains observers needs something that at least looks like change from inside. Whether static structure can be enough for that is the question of Chapter 14, and nothing about the primes settles it. Second, "given the correct implementation" is where the content of the claim lives. That a simple rule can produce quantum-like statistics is established. That some simple rule, suitably implemented, produces our universe with its particular interactions and its particular constants is the whole problem, and the primes do not solve it. They show that the gap between a one-sentence definition and rich structure can be crossed. They do not show where the crossing is, and finding it is the work that the elimination argument of the cosmology leaves undone.

# Part II — Logic Seen From Inside

# Chapter 4 — The Base Case: Where Logic Comes From

## 4.1 Knowledge is assigned, and the assigning stops somewhere

Take the definition I gave in 2015 and have used since: knowledge is information assigned to a reference system (Gruber, 2015). Information is data to which a coding can be assigned; data are differences that a reading device, an organism or a machine, can register. Nothing in the chain mentions truth. A reference system can be assigned information that does not hold, and the result is knowledge by this definition all the same — false knowledge, of which there has always been more than the other kind. Before Copernicus educated Europe knew that the earth rested at the centre of the cosmos; it knew this in the defined sense, the information was assigned and used, and it was as false then as it is now. Whether an assignment holds is a separate relation between the model and its input, and Chapter 6 deals with that relation on its own.

The definition recurses. A reference system is not given; it is built, and it is built of prior assignments. The concepts a reference system contains were themselves information assigned to an earlier reference system, and that one was built from assignments to an earlier one still. The 2015 book offers the recursive form itself: knowledge is information that can be assigned to knowledge already present. The form is right as far as it goes, and the question is how far it goes. Either the recursion runs without end, in which case nothing was ever assigned, because there was never a first system to assign to; or it ends. It can end only in a layer that was never assigned by interpretation at all — a layer that was grown, by plasticity during a life and by selection over the lives before it, and that was in place before any interpretation ran. In the Four-Model Theory that layer lies in the implicit models — the part of them that was grown rather than consolidated (Gruber, 2026a, §3.6). The implicit models are usually motivated by dissociation data, anosognosia and blindsight and the rest. This argument motivates them from the definitions alone, and it survives a reader who rejects every word of the neurobiology.

Two features of the base layer matter for what follows. It is not knowledge in the defined sense, since it was not assigned; it is what assigning is done against. It is used, not held. Consolidated content joins it as a second kind of implicit material, once assigned and no longer held as content; section 4.3 and Chapter 7 use that distinction. And nothing in the argument mentions consciousness: a system that has a reference system has knowledge whether or not it is conscious, and the regress ends in the same kind of layer for a thermostat and for a mathematician. The difference between them lies in what is built on the base, not in whether there is one.

## 4.2 Logic is not exempt

The natural move is to exempt logic. Everything else an observer knows was assigned against something earlier, but the rules of inference — from A and if-A-then-B, conclude B; a thing is identical to itself; no statement holds together with its denial — seem to stand under all the rest, needing no ground because they are the ground. One reply to the regress is therefore that it ends in logic, and that logic is the one layer that is both prior to interpretation and fully explicit.

The exemption does not survive the question of how a rule of inference is held. Suppose the rule is content: a sentence stored in the reference system like any other sentence, "from A and if-A-then-B, B follows." A well-known puzzle shows what happens next. If the rule is a premise, then applying it requires a further rule — from A, from if-A-then-B, and from the stored rule, conclude B — and that further rule is a premise as well, and the regress never arrives at an inference. An inference happens only when some rule is not stored but performed, when the system takes the step instead of contemplating it. That performed step is where the recursion of 4.1 ends for logic. The rule printed in the textbook is a serialization of a competence that was exercised long before anyone wrote it down and that was never derived from anything, because deriving is what it does.

So the rules of inference stand where the rest of the base layer stands: in place before interpretation, exercised in every interpretation, and not themselves content assigned against anything. The explicit axioms and rules of a formal system are what an observer gets when it tries to write its base layer down. The writing is a genuine achievement. A formal system is a better instrument than the competence it serializes, for the reason Chapter 1 gives about the proof checker: it refuses what the competence would let pass. But the written rule has not replaced the competence. It is read, applied and checked by it.

This is a claim about where the rules come from in a system that uses them, and about what such a system can access of their ground. It is not the claim that the rules are arbitrary, nor that an inference is valid because a brain is disposed to make it. The standard objection to any account of logic's origin is that origin settles nothing about validity, and the objection is correct. Whether a rule of inference is a good one is a different question with a different answer: a valid rule never takes input the system admits into a contradiction, and its failures can be checked from inside; Chapter 6 shows how far the check travels. The present question is only what the observer is standing on while it checks.

## 4.3 Self-evidence

What accompanies an axiom is the experience that it needs no argument. The experience is real, and "self-evident" is a report about the observer before it is a report about the proposition.

In the Four-Model Theory, content moves in both directions across the boundary between the implicit and the explicit models. What is learned explicitly becomes implicit through consolidation: practice writes it into structure, and the explicit layer stops carrying it. In the other direction, implicit content enters the running simulation as the given — as what the scene already contains — and not as something derived within the scene (Gruber, 2026a, §3.6). A consolidated model arrives with no derivation attached, because whatever derivation it once had was discarded when it was consolidated. And it resists explicit revision, because the explicit layer has no handle on it except its output. The visual illusions make the structure visible. In the Müller-Lyer figure, measuring the two lines and finding them equal does not make them look equal; the implicit model that reads line length from the arrowheads is not available for correction by a statement in the explicit one. It can be overruled in action, by trusting the ruler. It cannot be argued out of its output.

Self-evidence in the axioms of logic has the same shape. "No statement holds together with its denial" presents with no derivation attached, and it cannot be refused by argument, because every argument relies on it. What the presentation reports is the observer's relation to its own base layer: the statement serializes a competence that was never content, so no content stands behind it to be inspected. A statement whose ground is used rather than held presents as groundless and certain at once. That is what "self-evident" names. The same account applied to inferences, and to the sense that they hold from no perspective, is proposed in Chapter 7.

Kant called some of this a priori. In 2015 I listed eight statements most people accept as true without a second thought: water is wet; this sentence is written here; a coin added to a coin on my hand makes two coins; an elephant does not appear from nothing and does not dissolve into nothing; a heavy object, dropped, falls downward; effect follows cause and never the reverse; the world is still there when I close my eyes; no earthworm speaks German — and I argued that each can be dismantled (Gruber, 2015). The first is Kant's case: it is a priori because it follows from the definitions of the words. Nobody needs to have touched water to accept it. The word "wet" was invented for water in the first place, and "the bachelor is unmarried" is the same kind of statement: the terms are defined so in our culture, and any competent member of the culture recognizes the statement as true at once and without further information. The catch, which the 2015 argument presses, is that such a statement holds only in the epoch and culture in which the usage holds. Suppose the language refines itself over the centuries. Water is an unusual liquid, and handling it with the same adjectives as oil could go out of fashion the way "Mama is eating a milk" goes out of fashion in a child, so that in the twenty-third century a new wet-word exists for water and "water is wet" sounds like a category mistake. Then the assertion of seven billion people of the twenty-first century stands against the denial of fourteen billion of the twenty-third, and the only thing to conclude is that semantic truths are matters of agreement and are relative to usage. Put in terms of reference systems: an analytic truth is a statement whose denial is inconsistent with the admitted usage of a language community. The community is the reference system, the inconsistency is local to it, and it is a falsity relation of exactly the kind Chapter 6 describes — within a system, against its input. It travels no further than the system does.

The other statements on the list are not analytic. Nobody agreed that effect follows cause. The expectation is in place before any agreement, and in every animal that anticipates anything. This is the part of Kant's a priori that is independent of an individual's experience, and the regress says where it comes from: the implicit layer, grown by selection on lineages that predicted well enough to leave descendants. A priori for the organism, a posteriori for the lineage. What Kant took for the form of any possible experience is the form of the experience of the kind of system that got selected. That the form holds where we live is not a coincidence — Chapter 3 gives the reason, and it is the same reason mathematics works at all. That it holds everywhere is not established by its feeling that way.

The axioms of mathematics are the same case one level up. A coin added to a coin makes two coins, and the 2015 book concedes that for the natural numbers the addition is correct — with the remark that mathematics is itself a language, a very strictly regulated one, in which one and one can make zero or ten depending on the number system and the algebra (Gruber, 2015). The axioms of arithmetic serialize counting, a competence animals have in crude form and children have before they can state a rule, and they are self-evident in proportion. Where mathematics goes beyond counting, into the imported mini-models of Chapter 2, self-evidence thins. Nobody finds the parallel postulate or the axiom of choice self-evident, and the history of both is a history of people trying to derive them from something more evident and failing. That is what a base-layer element looks like when it sits in the base layer of a practice and not in the base layer of the organism: it has to be stipulated, because nothing in the observer performs it unasked.

## 4.4 Induction, deduction, and the invention of syntax

The 2015 book places logic in an account of learning, and the placement does more work than it was given there (Gruber, 2015). Reinforcement learning — reward and punishment, down to simple conditioning — has one great advantage and one great limit. The advantage is that in nature it rarely learns anything superfluous or false; superstition is a luxury of a different mechanism. The limit is that some things cannot be learned by it at all. A deadly poison permits no reinforcement trial; one trial is one too many. Humans could not have learned to avoid the death cap by eating it. They learned it from a third person: by observing someone else die, relating the observation to what they already knew, and inferring that the mushroom was responsible. That is an induction — the derivation of a theory from observations. The second kind of cognitive learning is deduction, the application of a theory to a new case: once the concept of a poisonous mushroom exists, an unknown mushroom is recognized as a risk, and the prudent forager gives it to the cave neighbour who snores before eating it himself. The book adds a third operation to the same machinery: the rationalization of spontaneous decisions after the fact. Explaining to the rest of the tribe why one poisoned the snorer is social cover, and it is also discovery: it finds rational connections among decisions that have already been survived, so that they can be used in future planning, even where the original decision was made for no reason at all. Cognitive learning converts implicit knowledge into explicit knowledge that can be communicated; the receiver, by repeated application, converts it back into implicit knowledge of his own. And it is by this route, the book says, that humans could formalize their originally unsystematic animal language: by deriving rules from a language that had grown naturally, and so "inventing" syntax and grammar.

Apply the account to logic itself. Whoever first wrote down the forms of valid inference did it the way grammar was written down: by looking at inferences people already made and accepted, and extracting the pattern. The codification of logic was an induction — a theory derived from observed cases of arguing that worked — performed by a method the codified logic does not itself license. The extracted rules were then applied deductively to new arguments, including arguments whose conclusions no one's intuition had reached, and that is the gain the formalization bought. And the proof, the serialized justification that a checker will accept, is written after the result in exactly the way the forager's explanation is given after the poisoning. Anyone who has done mathematics knows the order: the theorem is found first, by whatever route, and the proof is constructed afterwards as a serialization that others can check. The order of discovery and the order of justification differ, and the second is a rationalization of the first — a useful one, because it exposes the finding to a check the finding did not have, and a rationalization nonetheless.

Syntax shows the whole cycle in one object. A language grows; its rules are derived from it by observation; the rules are taught; and the next generation acquires the language partly through the rules and mostly, still, by growth. A native speaker applies a grammar she cannot state, and the 2015 book counts that grammar as implicit of the grown kind — never content of any reference system, in place before any rule of it was stated (Gruber, 2015). The grammar book stands to her competence as a formal system stands to the logician's: a serialization that the competence reads and checks. Logic is a cultural formalization of an animal capacity, and the capacity is older than the culture by a great deal.

## 4.5 No logic without a non-logical ground

A system whose entire content is assigned while it runs has no base case: there is nothing in it for the first assignment to be assigned against, so it cannot bootstrap knowledge at all (Gruber, 2026a, §3.6). A base layer that was shaped before the system ran — by growth, by selection, by training, by construction — is what makes assignment possible, and it is not itself assigned. There is therefore no logic without a non-logical ground. The rules by which assignments are checked are not among the assignments. They are performed, and what performs them was never derived.

And no observer has access to its ground, taken as a whole, as content. The base layer is what the observer uses to make anything into content; making the whole base layer into content would require a further base layer to do the making, which is the regress of 4.1 run from the other side. This is the principle that the generator never appears completely in what it generates, at the scale of a single observer: parts of the ground can be written down, and the ground as a whole cannot be content of the observer it grounds. An observer can write down a serialization of its ground — a logic textbook is that — and the serialization can be examined, improved, found inconsistent and replaced. What the observer cannot do is examine the ground directly. It examines a written copy, and it does the examining with the ground.

Which logic an observer should use is not settled by any of this. Several formal logics exist, they differ in what they refuse, and the choice among them is made on grounds that have not been touched here. Nor is an observer's knowledge true because it rests on a ground, or false because the ground was grown. Knowledge was decoupled from truth in the first paragraph and stays decoupled. The resting central earth was knowledge; it was false; and its falsity was shown from inside astronomy, by observations astronomy's own methods recognised, without any view from outside astronomy. The phases of Venus ruled out the Ptolemaic arrangement in 1610, and stellar aberration showed the earth moving in 1729 — nearly two centuries after Copernicus had proposed the replacement. That a ground is required for knowledge, and that the ground is not inspectable, says nothing about which knowledge holds. It says only that whoever checks is standing on something while checking, and that the something was not put there by the check.

# Chapter 5 — Four Limits on Any Embedded Description

## 5.1 The describer as a proper part

In the cosmology paper I set out three limits on any physical theory of the universe, with Gödel's theorems as a fourth that applies specifically to formal theories (Gruber, 2026b, §6.4). The argument there used nothing specific to physics and nothing specific to the universe. It used three facts: that the theory is implemented inside what it describes; that the implementation has less capacity than the whole; and that the dynamics of the whole may admit no shortcut. Any describer that is a proper part of what it describes meets the first two conditions by definition, and wherever it actually performs irreducible computation it brings about the third by its own presence (5.3). A brain describing a world that contains the brain, or a mathematician describing a formal system in which her own reasoning can be formalized — the limits concern the relation of part to whole, and they hold for whichever part is doing the describing.

The setting can be kept finite. The whole has some information content; the part has strictly less, because a proper part of a finite system has fewer degrees of freedom than the system. A description held by the part is complete, for present purposes, if it determines, together with whatever procedures the part can run, every fact about the whole that it claims to cover. Four limits then apply: on what the part can store, on what it can compute in time, on what a formal theory it holds can prove, and on what a description is. The first three are about information and computation. The fourth is not.

## 5.2 Capacity

Kolmogorov (1965) and Chaitin (1966) independently defined the complexity of a finite object as the length of the shortest program that produces it. The definition comes with a counting fact. There are 2^n binary strings of length n, and fewer than 2^n programs shorter than n bits, so most strings of length n have no description shorter than themselves; the fraction that can be shortened by c bits is less than 2^−c. A string with no shorter description is incompressible, and incompressible strings are the typical case, not the exception.

The consequence for an embedded describer is immediate. If the state of the whole is incompressible, or compressible only to a length above the part's capacity, then no part holds a complete description of that state. This is a counting argument and nothing more: it uses no theorem about logic and no property of the describer beyond its size. The cosmology paper applies it to a layer that holds information at the maximum density a region can carry, where a typical state is incompressible (Gruber, 2026b, §6.4). The argument needs none of that detail, only that the whole contain more information than the part can store, and a proper part of an incompressible whole is in that position by arithmetic.

Compressible wholes are the interesting case. A whole generated by a short rule from a short initial condition has a short generative description, and a part can hold it: the rule plus the initial condition fit where the state does not. What the part then holds is the generator, and whether holding the generator gives it the state is the next limit's question. One consequence of the capacity limit stands on its own, and Chapter 8 uses it. With fewer resources than the whole, every description the part holds of the whole is a compression. Compression keeps regularities and drops what has none, and it is lossless only to the extent that the whole is compressible. The remainder — what the whole contains and no regularity captures — is what the part cannot have as content, and the part cannot know from inside how large the remainder is, because measuring it would require holding it.

## 5.3 Irreducibility

Wolfram (2002) called a computation irreducible when the only way to learn its state after t steps is to carry out t steps: no closed form, no formula in t, no procedure that arrives at the answer faster than the computation arrives at it. Two elementary cellular automata show the distinction (Gruber, 2026b, §2.3). Rule 90 is additive: the state of any cell at any time is given directly by binomial coefficients modulo 2, and a part holding that formula computes any cell of the whole without running the steps in between. The Sierpiński pattern it draws is a fractal and a closed form at once. Rule 30 has no such formula, as far as anyone knows; its central column has served as a pseudorandom generator for decades; and its irreducibility is a conjecture, not a theorem. For Rule 110 the matter is settled from the other side: it is proven universal (Cook, 2004), and a universal system admits no general shortcut to its eventual behaviour.

It admits none because Turing (1936) showed that no machine decides, for every machine and every input, whether the computation will ever finish; his own formulation concerned whether a machine ever prints a given symbol, and the modern halting form is equivalent in strength. A procedure that returned the eventual behaviour of arbitrary programs without running them would decide exactly that, so no such procedure exists. Two bounds belong next to this step. Turing rules out a general procedure for the outcome, not a rendering of each step that beats the original by a constant factor; where such a rendering exists, a part with fewer resources than the whole is still behind. And for a finite whole the argument shifts from impossibility to size: the future of a finite system is in principle a lookup table, and the table is larger than the system, which returns the matter to capacity.

This is where the presence of a describer matters. A describer that can model arbitrary structure is, at the least, a universal system: whatever else it is, it can carry out any computation that is carried out at all, up to its memory. Universality alone does not make the world irreducible; a universal machine running a trivial program has a trajectory with a closed form. Two weaker statements hold. No general shortcut exists for a world that contains universal describers, because such a shortcut would decide what they compute. And a whole whose describer actually performs irreducible computation has no closed form for its actual trajectory: if it had one, every part's state would be a function of the whole's and would inherit it. The condition under which the cosmology paper states this limit, that the dynamics admit no shortcut, therefore holds in general for any world with describers in it, and holds for its actual history whenever a describer in it is doing irreducible work.

A part that holds the exact rule of the whole obtains the whole's future only by running the rule, and with fewer resources than the whole it runs more slowly than the whole runs itself. Its prediction is always behind: the whole already is wherever the part's computation is heading. Capacity said the part cannot hold the state. Irreducibility says that even holding the generator, the part cannot get ahead of the state with it. The two are complementary: an incompressible whole defeats storage, and a compressible one defeats computation wherever a describer in it is doing irreducible work.

## 5.4 Incompleteness and undecidability

The third limit is the one specific to formal theories, and it has to be stated with its conditions attached, because almost every misuse of it drops one. Take a formal system in the sense Gödel (1931) studied: a fixed alphabet, a set of axioms and rules of inference such that whether a given string of symbols is a proof can be checked mechanically, and enough expressive power to carry out elementary arithmetic — addition and multiplication of the natural numbers suffice. The first incompleteness theorem says that if such a system is consistent, there is a sentence of its own language that it neither proves nor refutes, and that under the intended reading of the arithmetic the sentence is true. Gödel's own proof assumed a condition slightly stronger than consistency; the stronger assumption was removed within a few years. The second theorem says that such a system, if consistent, does not prove the natural arithmetical statement of its own consistency.

The mechanism shows something the result alone does not. Gödel coded the system's syntax into numbers, so that statements about proofs became statements about arithmetic, and the system could talk about its own derivations in its own language. He then constructed a sentence which, under the coding, says of itself that it is not provable. If the system proved it, the system would prove a falsehood and be inconsistent; so if the system is consistent, the sentence is unprovable, which is what it says, so it is true. The construction also fixes the exact form of the principle about a generator and what it generates. The system's proof relation is represented inside the system, and represented exactly: the generator does appear in what it generates, as content. What fails is closure. The representation cannot be both complete and consistent — a consistent system that represents its own derivations leaves truths about them unproved, and a system that proves everything about them is inconsistent. For formal systems the principle therefore takes its sharp form: the generator can appear in what it generates, but not completely, if what it generates is to remain consistent.

Drop any of the three conditions and the theorem is silent. An inconsistent system proves everything, its own consistency included. A system whose axioms are all the true sentences of arithmetic is complete, but its axioms cannot be mechanically recognized, so it is not a formal system in the required sense. And arithmetic with addition but without multiplication is complete and decidable; the theorem needs the multiplication. The 2015 book made the same point in plainer words: there are complete and consistent mathematical theories, they are subject to strong restrictions, and the theorem applies, roughly speaking, only from a certain strength of the axiomatic system upward (Gruber, 2015).

Turing (1936) gives the computational counterpart. He defined a computable number as one whose digits a machine can produce in order, showed that the computable numbers are countable, and proved the undecidability stated above: no general procedure settles whether an arbitrary computation finishes. The family extends to the objects of 5.3. Culik and Yu (1988) proved that for a formal version of Wolfram's behavioural classes, deciding which class an arbitrary cellular automaton belongs to is undecidable: no procedure takes a rule and returns its class. The sorting of rules into reducible and irreducible therefore cannot be done in general. It is done case by case, by proof where a proof exists — Rule 90's closed form, Rule 110's universality — and by conjecture where it does not, as for Rule 30. Even the classification on which the second limit rests is, in general, out of reach of any general method, which is the second limit applied to itself.

In 2015 I wrote that the limitation Gödel proved for the language of mathematics holds, roughly put, for every language powerful enough to describe itself, and so for algorithmics, which includes mathematics, and for the natural languages, which are more powerful still; the restrictions are inherited upward, by increasing expressive power (Gruber, 2015). The book states the caveat a page later: the theorem holds for closed constructs within their own rules, and for natural languages it is as a rule irrelevant — one should simply not try to run a mathematical proof in natural language, which is the wrong tool for that purpose. What is inherited is this. Whatever can express arithmetic and fixes its own derivations can formulate a statement about those derivations that it cannot settle. Algorithmics inherits it as undecidability. A natural language, which fixes neither axioms nor a mechanical proof check, is not a formal system, and the theorem does not apply to it as a theorem; what it inherits is that any fragment of it fixed into a formal system of the required strength is incomplete or inconsistent, and that the unfixed remainder cannot be checked at all. The 2015 inventory of an axiom system — an alphabet, axioms, a calculus; in a natural language, a vocabulary, the semantic truths, and grammar together with ordinary logic — shows where the trouble sits. The alphabet and the calculus are matters of definition. The axioms are the problem: how many semantic truths does German contain, and when does one know that all of them have been listed? From a handful of well-chosen ones further ones can always be derived, and the list never closes. That is Chapter 4's observation seen from Gödel's side: the base layer of a natural language is not a finite set of sentences, and any finite set written down in its place leaves some of the competence out.

## 5.5 What Gödel does not say about brains

A well-known argument runs: a human mathematician can see that the Gödel sentence of a given formal system is true, the system cannot prove it, therefore the human is not that system, and by extension not any machine. Three things are wrong with it.

The theorem has an antecedent: a consistent, mechanically checkable system of sufficient arithmetical strength. Nothing establishes that a human's inferential dispositions form such a system. Humans make errors of every kind and routinely hold inconsistent beliefs (Gruber, 2015), and an inconsistent system proves everything, its own Gödel sentence included; about such a system the theorem is silent. The argument needs the human to be a consistent formal system in order to exempt her from being one.

Seeing that a Gödel sentence is true requires knowing that its system is consistent. For a system one is not, that can sometimes be known by other means, and the mathematician who sees the Gödel sentence of a standard arithmetic to be true is doing exactly that: she occupies a perspective on that system which the system does not have on itself. She has no such perspective on whatever system she is, if she is one. By the second theorem she could not establish its consistency from inside, and without that she cannot see its Gödel sentence to be true. The argument shows that one system can see what another cannot see about itself, and nothing about any system seeing it about itself.

And the limits that apply to observers as such are the structural ones: capacity, irreducibility, and the constitutive limit of the next section. They need no consistency, no axioms and no arithmetic; they follow from being a proper part. Incompleteness bounds what a formal theory can prove about a whole that contains it — not what a brain can feel or do. Gödel says nothing mysterious about brains. Language is an imprecise instrument, and one can accept that without his theorem.

## 5.6 The constitutive limit

The fourth limit is of a different kind. A description of a process, however complete, does not give its holder an instance of the process (Gruber, 2026a, §3.4.5; Gruber, 2026b, §6.4). The cosmology paper states it as the reason nothing short of the universe serves as the universe's complete description. The Four-Model Theory states it for experience: a complete substrate description of a running self-model would be informationally complete — nothing missing, no dualism, no strong emergence — and reading it would still not be having the experience.

What separates this limit from the first three is that it is not about information at all. The description may contain everything, and the limit stands, because it is relative to the holder: a description held by a proper part is not, for that part, an instance. The part can read the description, check it and run it, and running is a further operation that the part performs on its own resources. What the same structure is for anything inside it is a different question, and Chapter 14 takes it up. In this reader-relative form the limit survives in a world that is small, reducible and too weak for arithmetic, and it is the only one of the four that would survive there for the reader.

## 5.7 The halting problem of serialization

The 2015 book, after setting the halting problem aside as irrelevant to how brains work, allows it one application (Gruber, 2015). Whether a mental model — even a consistent one — can be serialized is something we can begin to judge only when we try to serialize it. If the attempt succeeds, the answer is given. If it does not, we cannot decide whether serialization would have been possible in principle. In plain words: we cannot decide in advance whether we will be able to explain what we think.

The shape is exact even where the content is informal. Halting is semi-decidable: if a machine halts, one learns it by waiting; if it does not, no finite observation tells one so. Serializability of a model has the same asymmetry. A successful serialization is a certificate, inspectable by anyone. A failed attempt certifies nothing, because the next attempt might succeed, and there is no procedure that returns "never". I do not claim that this is a theorem about minds; "mental model" and "serialize" have not been formalized. The asymmetry holds for a plainer reason: the serializer is part of the thinking it tries to serialize, and the only way to find out what it will produce is to run it. That is the second limit again, applied to the observer's own output. There is no shortcut to the result of an attempt to explain except the attempt.

Some models never serialize. The 2015 book lists the reasons: a model can be too complex to transport in language — to sequentialize — because of linguistic differences between sender and receiver, because of high multimodality, or because of high individuality in the face of a very different receiver. Everyone knows the case: in the head it is clear and simple, and it still cannot be described. The pun that does not translate is the plainest instance. The Four-Model Theory records the same limit against itself: the models it describes operate simultaneously in high-dimensional state spaces, any description in natural language serializes them, and the serialization introduces a representational loss that no care in the writing removes (Gruber, 2026a, §10.3).

Mathematics is the most disciplined serialization there is, and its discipline is the proof checker of Chapter 1. It inherits the limit intact. Whether a given structure can be formalized is known when the formalization succeeds; a failure to formalize decides nothing, and the history of mathematics is a history of concepts that resisted serialization for a long time and then did not — the infinitesimal, or the random sequence itself, of which Kolmogorov's definition is one serialization among several. The checker guarantees that what has been serialized can be checked. It guarantees nothing about what has not been, and it cannot tell its user in advance which of her models will pass through it.

## 5.8 Quasi-infinity, the observer's condition

The cosmology paper defines a system as quasi-infinite when it is effectively unbounded for any internal observer and yet has finite information content accessible from any given point (Gruber, 2026b, §4.2). The condition differs from mathematical infinity, where no bound exists, and from finitude, where a bound can be reached and inspected. A quasi-infinite system is bounded by surfaces that limit information rather than by edges: horizons that recede faster than they can be approached, a resolution floor that retreats as it is probed, termini that end a world-line without arrival and reveal nothing beyond.

That is the condition under which the four limits are stated, and it is the condition of every embedded describer. From where the describer stands the whole has no edge, so the describer cannot complete its description by exhausting the whole; the horizon moves with it. And from where it stands the accessible information is finite, so the counting arguments of 5.2 apply in full. The two halves together make the limits structural. A describer at an edge could, in principle, turn around and have the whole in view; a describer with infinite capacity could store an incompressible whole. An embedded describer has neither the edge nor the capacity, and no improvement in its instruments changes either, because the instruments are inside too.

Every description from inside is therefore a description from a point, bounded by what reaches the point, held in less capacity than it describes, and run on a clock no faster than the whole's. None of the four limits is a statement about human weakness. A mathematician describing arithmetic from inside arithmetic and a cell describing the organism it belongs to meet the same four, and so would any observer that a universe can contain.

# Chapter 6 — Falsity Without Truth

## A schematic history

Take a schematic history, simplified past anything a historian would accept, but exact in its logic. A community holds that the earth is flat. It holds this on evidence: the ground looks flat as far as anyone can walk, and the horizon looks like an edge. The claim is geometric, and geometry is what disposes of it. Ships disappear hull first over the horizon. The shadow the earth throws on the moon during an eclipse is round every time, whatever the angle. Travellers going south watch new stars rise. None of these observations needs anything beyond the geometry the flat-earth claim already used. The community's own framework, applied to its own new observations, contradicts the claim.

The replacement is "roughly a ball", and it has a different status. It is the best present fit, held by consensus and open to revision, and it has already been revised: an oblate spheroid, then a geoid with measurable bumps. A more radical revision is not excluded either. The earth could turn out to be a three-dimensional ball that is a sub-object of a six-dimensional cube, so that what we call its shape is one section of a structure nobody yet suspects. Present observation cannot rule this out, because a successor framework only has to reproduce what we see.

That subsumption would not make flat true again. Whatever the six-dimensional cube is, it has to reproduce the hull-down ships and the round shadow, because those are observations and the successor keeps them. The refutation travels with the input. Every framework that keeps the observations inherits the verdict that the earth is not flat. No framework is obliged to inherit the verdict that it is a ball, since a different structure fitting the same observations would displace it.

The principle is that absolute falsehood does not require absolute truth. A claim can be refuted relative to input that every successor keeps, without anyone holding the claim that replaces it as final, and without any standpoint from which the final claim could be read. Such a refutation is not immune to revision. It is more resistant to revision than any positive claim held on the same evidence.

## Why falsity needs no outside

The principle follows from the reference-system account of knowledge (Chapter 1, Chapter 4). Knowledge is information assigned to a reference system, and a reference system is a model (Gruber, 2015). There is no reference system that is not some system's model. To establish a positive claim of the form "this model matches the world", one would need to set the model beside the world and compare them from a position that is not itself a model. No such position exists. Every comparison of a model with the world is a comparison of one model with another, because the observation used for the comparison is itself information assigned to a reference system. Truth in the strong sense, a match certified from no perspective, would need a standpoint outside all reference systems, and an embedded observer has none.

Falsity needs much less. A reference system can be shown inconsistent with input it itself admits. The relation has two terms, the system and its admitted input, and both are on the inside. Establishing the verdict, relative to that input, takes one contradiction: a consequence the system draws that the input it accepts denies. Once the contradiction has been exhibited, anyone with access to the system's claims and its input can check it, whatever their own model of the world. The check is local and cheap, and it is the same check from every perspective.

Formal systems show the same asymmetry in sharp form. For a system of the kind Gödel's theorems govern, an inconsistency, if there is one, has a finite derivation that can be written down and checked step by step. Consistency is a different matter: a consistent system of that kind cannot prove its own consistency (Gödel, 1931). The negative verdict comes with a certificate any checker can verify. The positive verdict has no certificate available from inside.

Set theory supplies the mathematical case of inheritance. The principle that every property determines a set, applied without restriction, yields a contradiction: the set of all sets that are not members of themselves is a member of itself if and only if it is not. The derivation is a few lines long and anyone can check it. Every set theory built since keeps the input that generated the contradiction, namely the ordinary rules of logic and the definition of membership, and every one of them restricts the principle to avoid it. The refutation of unrestricted comprehension has passed into all of them. None of them has a proof of its own consistency from inside, for the reason Gödel gave. Mathematics knows, with complete security under classical logic, one thing about sets that is false, and holds its positive theory of sets on trust.

A successor framework that keeps the input inherits the verdict that the old framework contradicted that input. It does not inherit any guarantee that it is itself correct. The refutation is a fact about the old system and the input, and it stays a fact for as long as the input is kept, whatever else changes.

Keeping the input is a condition, and it is where a refutation can be escaped. A system can refuse an observation: declare the instrument faulty, reinterpret the reading, add an auxiliary assumption that turns the contradiction into a fit. This is the patch move of Chapter 1, and it is always available. It also has a price. Every other system that keeps the input sees that the refusing system has had to give the input up, and the refusal itself becomes a checkable fact about that system. Refusal relocates the contradiction into the patch.

Two senses of "absolute" have to be kept apart here. As a relation, falsity is not a consensus property. The flat earth was not true while most people held it and false once they stopped; the contradiction with the round shadow was available to anyone who admitted the observation, before anyone did, and what consensus adds is only who has admitted it. Epistemically, our verdicts, about falsity as much as about truth, are revisable, because observations can be wrong and frameworks misapplied. The verdict and its security are also different things. One exhibited contradiction establishes the verdict relative to the input. What accumulates afterwards, refutation on refutation, is security against the input itself being withdrawn. A falsity verdict is never read off an absolute truth, which is why giving up absolute truth and keeping absolute falsity in this sense is consistent.

## A gradient

A stronger version of the principle says that a refutation, once made, survives every change of framework. History refutes that version. Continental drift was declared false and came back. So did the bacterial cause of most stomach ulcers. Refuted claims do return. The defensible claim is a difference in resistance: when the deciding population and its observations change, both truths and falsehoods can change, and falsehoods change less easily. Two independent mechanisms produce the difference.

The first is evidential. A refutation is pinned to one specific contradiction. To overturn it, one has to overturn that observation or show that the framework misapplied it, which is a narrow target and an expensive one to hit. A positive claim is underdetermined by the same observations, since many models fit any finite body of data, and it is displaced the moment a better-fitting model arrives. Each new competitor is another way for it to fail. A refutation is exposed to one line of attack; a confirmation is exposed to every alternative that fits the evidence better. The asymmetry is therefore a matter of how many ways each verdict can fail, which makes it a matter of degree. The returning cases fit this account. They are the cases where the narrow target was actually hit: the refuting observation was weak, or the framework was applied to it wrongly.

The second mechanism is social, and it works on top of the evidential one. Consider the three transitions a claim can make. A claim moving from unknown to accepted needs positive evidence. A claim moving from accepted to rejected needs positive evidence as well, namely the refuting observation. A claim moving from rejected back to accepted needs positive evidence and something more: the removal of everything a community has built to support the rejection. Once a claim has been ruled false, the ruling accumulates reasons, textbook explanations, teaching examples, professional reflexes and reputations, all of them organised around the logic of its falsity. Reversing the ruling means writing off that investment. As I would put it, reversal would be a second conversion of knowledge that has already been turned through 180 degrees. The rejected state is a post-reversal state. The community has already paid for one turn to get there, and that payment is what resists a second.

Both mechanisms yield a prediction that neither yields alone in so specific a form: the resistance of a falsity ruling to reversal scales with the cost of the conversion that established it. A falsehood established expensively, across generations and against institutional resistance, should be close to irreversible. Geocentrism is the case: Copernicus proposed the replacement in 1543; the phases of Venus refuted the Ptolemaic arrangement in 1610, while leaving Tycho's earth-centred alternative standing; and the observations of the earth's own motion, stellar aberration in 1729 and parallax in 1838, came nearly two and three centuries after the proposal. A falsehood established cheaply, on a background assumption or on authority without a hard-won empirical reversal, should be comparatively easy to rehabilitate. The two returning cases fit. Continental drift was rejected mainly because no mechanism for moving continents was known, not because a costly observation contradicted it. The bacterial cause of ulcers was rejected largely on the background assumption that stomach acid keeps the stomach free of bacteria. Both rejections were cheap, and both were reversed once evidence arrived.

Two findings would count against the prediction. One is a falsehood established by a costly empirical reversal that returned easily. The other is cheaply rejected claims that stayed rejected after strong evidence for them appeared. The prediction also needs a measure of conversion cost that is fixed before the outcome is known: the duration of the original controversy, the institutional resources spent on it, the volume of teaching built on the verdict. Cost judged after the fact, from whether a claim came back, would make the prediction circular.

## Where a refutation is stored

The Four-Model Theory gives the same asymmetry a third source, internal to a single mind. In FMT, explicit models are the running simulation, held as content, and implicit models are the learned structure from which the simulation is generated (Gruber, 2026a, §3.3, §3.6). Content that has been held explicitly and used repeatedly is consolidated: written back into the implicit layer, where it no longer appears as a claim and instead shapes what appears. Implicit models resist explicit revision, because revision works on content and consolidated structure is not content.

A falsity ruling that a person or a community has lived with goes through exactly this process. "That was debunked" becomes a reflex. Most people who know that the earth is not flat cannot reconstruct the refutation; they have the verdict without the argument, and the verdict arrives as an immediate rejection of the claim, before any reasoning about it. The current best positive model, by contrast, tends to be held explicitly and known to be provisional. People who know that the earth is roughly a ball usually know that "roughly" is a reservation they would act on, and would accept a refinement without distress. Negatives are consolidated more thoroughly than positives, which makes them harder to revise for the reason implicit models are harder to revise. This is the evidential and social asymmetry again, now located in the architecture of a single reference system.

The three mechanisms are independent. One concerns how evidence pins a verdict, one concerns what a community builds around a verdict, and one concerns where a mind stores a verdict. Their agreement on one result is the main reason I take the gradient to be real.

## What logic carries between perspectives

What logic can carry from one perspective to another is negation and inconsistency. Any perspective can verify that a given perspective contradicts its own input, provided it has access to that perspective's claims and to the input it admits. No perspective can verify that a model is the world. The contradiction check is the part of logic that needs no agreement about what the symbols refer to, because it compares a system only with itself and its own admitted input. Mathematics inherits this property through its proof checker (Chapter 1): a derivation of a contradiction from a set of axioms is valid for every reader who accepts the rules of derivation, whatever that reader takes the axioms to be about.

The transfer has one condition. Two perspectives can share a contradiction check only if they share a rule under which a contradiction counts as a failure. Classical logic has such a rule: from a contradiction anything follows, so a system that contains one has said nothing. There are logics built to tolerate contradictions locally, without everything following from them, and a system reasoning in such a logic need not treat an exhibited contradiction as fatal. Among perspectives that treat contradiction as failure, which includes every perspective that acts on its predictions, a refutation transfers without any agreement about the world. A perspective that does not treat contradiction as failure has given up the one instrument that could have told it anything about its own errors.

Popper is the obvious neighbour, and the logical half of the asymmetry is his. In *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* he derived it from the form of universal statements: no finite set of observations verifies a universal law, while a single accepted counter-instance contradicts it. He then proposed falsifiability as a methodological rule, a criterion for which theories count as empirical and a recommendation for how science should treat them (Popper, 1959). The evidential mechanism of the gradient — one contradiction pins a refutation, every better-fitting competitor threatens a positive claim — is that asymmetry, applied to admitted input instead of to observation statements.

The reference-system architecture adds something different. It locates what is unavailable from inside: truth as a match between a model and the world, which would need a standpoint outside every reference system. Positive verdicts as such are not unavailable — "this model fits the input I admit, here and now" is checkable, and no harder to check than its negation. The account also applies to any reference system, not only to scientific theories: an organism's implicit model of its surroundings, a child's expectation about where a ball will land. And it supplies what the logic of universal statements does not contain: the social ratchet, the consolidation of rulings into the implicit layer, and with them the prediction that resistance to reversal scales with the cost of the conversion that established a ruling. Nothing in the account recommends a method. It describes what any embedded system can and cannot establish, whether or not it adopts a method at all.

Popper also conceded that the basic statements which do the refuting are themselves accepted by decision, and are not given by experience with any finality; he compared the empirical basis of science to piles driven into a swamp, deep enough to carry the structure for now but resting on no bedrock (Popper, 1959). The present account has the same feature in the clause "admitted input". What a system admits is up to the system, and a refutation is relative to what it admits. The difference lies in what follows. A refutation relative to admitted input is inherited by every successor that admits the same input, and the cost of refusing an input is visible from every other perspective that keeps it. The swamp the refutation stands in is shared.

## The liar under a measure of agreement

The positive direction is not empty. It is graded. In my 2015 account, agreement between reference systems is a measure: the proportion of verdicts on a piece of knowledge that coincide when different reference systems assign it (Gruber, 2015). That measure is what consensus about positive claims amounts to. It is useful, it is social, and nothing about it is absolute.

A graded measure of this kind behaves well on the liar. "This sentence is false" defeats a bivalent truth predicate: if it is true it is false, if it is false it is true, and a semantics that insists on one of the two values either contradicts itself or has to exclude the sentence. A measure of agreement among verdicts has no such trouble. Every attempt to evaluate the sentence reverses the previous verdict, the verdicts split, and the measure settles in the middle. The liar receives an intermediate value, which matches the ordinary intuition that it is neither true nor false, and the measure does not break.

I do not claim that this solves the liar in the sense formal semantics asks for. A measure of agreement is a different instrument from a truth predicate, and showing that the instrument survives the sentence says nothing about how a formal language should treat it. Two narrower points hold.

The first is that the liar contradicts no input. It has no input. It makes no claim about anything outside itself that an observation could deny, so the falsity criterion finds nothing to test. Its trouble is of another kind: it is a sentence whose content is its own evaluation.

The second is that the sharp direction and the graded direction need different instruments, and confusing them produces exactly the kind of paradox the liar is. Falsity, the relation between a system and its admitted input, is sharp and needs no measure. Agreement, the relation between systems about how information is to be assigned, is graded and needs a measure. A sentence that turns the act of evaluating it into its own content belongs to neither. It belongs to the problem of a system trying to make its own evaluation complete content of itself, which no consistent system can do (Chapter 5).

# Chapter 7 — The Apparatus That Cannot Appear Whole

## The one thing missing

Your simulation models the world, your body, other people and yourself. It does not model the apparatus generating the simulation as what is generating it. Everything else that matters to you can appear in the scene: objects, places, sensations, the faces and intentions of others, your own position, mood and history. Pieces of the producing process can show up too; the process itself, taken as a whole, is not among the things it produces.

The Four-Model Theory states this in three places, each from a different side. The first is the observability constraint of self-referential closure (Gruber, 2026a, §3.4.3). In a system whose world-model contains a model of the system itself doing the modeling, the explicit self-model's horizon is bounded by the explicit world-model. It cannot see, with any useful resolution, past the explicit world-model to the implicit structure that generates both. Every characterization the system forms of its own processing is already part of the simulation, and there is no internal vantage point from which the processing can be described without that description being part of what is processed.

The second is the meta-problem (Gruber, 2026a, §3.8). The generating process, taken as a whole, cannot be content of the simulation it is generating; parts of it can leak in (Chapter 8), and a leak is not the generator. When the explicit self-model tries to represent the basis of its own experience, it therefore finds no mechanism in the scene that could be that basis. The persistent sense that something is left out of every physical explanation of consciousness is what a self-model in this position should report.

The third is the unrepresentable apparatus (Gruber, 2026a, §4.2.2). The simulation can contain descriptions of brains, including its own brain seen in a scanner. Substrate activity can intrude into the scene as anomalous content, as phosphenes or the filling-in of the blind spot. What the simulation cannot contain is its own current generation, represented as the cause of what it is generating at that moment. This omission is different in kind from the many things any model leaves out. Most unrepresented causes are covered by stand-ins: hunger is referred to an empty stomach, with no representation of the physiology that produces the feeling, and the stand-in is good enough that nothing seems missing. The apparatus has no adequate stand-in, because a stand-in for the whole of what is generating this scene would have to be generated inside the scene by the very process it stands for, and would then be one more item in the scene, not the process.

This is the organism-scale instance of the principle that the generator never appears completely in what it generates. It is also the regress of Chapter 4 seen from the inside. A reference system cannot be content of itself as a whole, since that would require a further reference system in which to hold it; the implicit models are the reference system through which the simulation is generated, and so the generation they carry out, taken as a whole, cannot be among its own products.

## Felt freedom

The case in which the omission is felt most directly is choice. A decision enters the simulation with its generating cause absent from every position the simulation can survey. The simulation assigns every event it places somewhere, and a decision is assigned to the self. There the regress ends, because what stands behind the self-model is the apparatus, and the apparatus cannot appear as the cause of this choice. The choice presents as originating in the self with nothing behind it. Felt freedom is what an origin feels like from inside (Gruber, 2026a, §4.2.2).

Reasons are no counterexample. When I deliberate, reasons appear in the scene, and I weigh them. But they appear as contents being weighed. What produces the outcome of the weighing does not appear as its producer, and the outcome arrives as mine. Freedom, on this account, is the phenomenological signature of the model's one blind spot that it cannot fill.

The account explains why the question of free will resists settlement from inside. A path not taken stays representable in the simulation, as paths others took are. The path I took is fully explained by reasons the moment I look for them. Both observations survive every case, and neither dislodges the other, because deciding between them would require seeing the apparatus.

The claim is narrow. Whether the system is free is a question about physical determinism. FMT declines that question; it belongs to physics. Why choosing feels free from inside is a question about the architecture of a self-model, and it is the only one answered here. The answer does not depend on how the physical question is settled. In a deterministic universe the apparatus is unrepresented and the choice presents as originating in the self. In an indeterministic universe the apparatus is still unrepresented and the choice still presents that way. The account is neutral between the two and is no evidence for either. "Freedom is the signature of a blind spot" must not be read as "therefore there is no free will"; that inference is not available to a theory that has declined the physics.

Two descriptions of one event follow, each correct at its own level. From inside, the choice is free, because its cause is not among the things there are. From another perspective, nothing is missing: the choice issues from the organism's substrate and history, given its input. The inside description does not misrepresent a fact the model contains. It reports the structure of a model in which that fact has no place.

## A proposal: felt logical necessity

I propose an extension that FMT does not contain: felt logical necessity has the same structure as felt freedom. An inference is performed by an implicit competence: the trained capacity, consolidated over a lifetime and grounded in structure that was never itself derived (Chapter 4). That competence is part of the apparatus. When an inference runs, its premises can appear in the scene and its conclusion appears in the scene. The competence that carried the one to the other does not appear as what carried it, for the same reason the apparatus does not appear as the cause of a choice. The conclusion therefore presents as compelled by the content of the premises. "It follows" is how the scene registers a transition whose producer is absent from it.

Felt freedom and felt necessity then differ only in where the scene places the outcome. A decision is placed in the self, and with nothing visible behind the self, it presents as originating there. A conclusion is placed in the world-model, as a relation between propositions, and with nothing visible behind the relation, it presents as a property of the propositions themselves. The conclusion seems to have been in the premises all along, waiting to be read off. In both cases the generator is absent as cause; in one case the gap is filled by an origin, in the other by a compulsion. The two can also pass into each other. When the reasons in a deliberation become decisive, the decision stops feeling open and starts to feel forced ("I had no choice"): the outcome has moved from being placed in the self to being placed in the reasons.

The proposal accounts for a feature of logical truth that has impressed philosophers for as long as there has been logic: it seems to hold from no perspective at all. A valid inference does not feel like my opinion about the premises. It feels as if it would hold for anyone, anywhere, whether or not anyone drew it. On the proposal, this is what one should expect of a conclusion whose producer cannot appear as producer. A perspective is visible as a perspective only where something in the scene marks it as the source of what it delivers. Where the source is the one thing that cannot be in the scene as source, the delivery carries no mark of a source, and it presents as perspective-free. Logical truths feel as if they hold from nowhere because they are produced by the part of a perspective that cannot be seen, as producer, from within it.

The proposal has testable consequences for the phenomenology. Felt necessity should track consolidation, not validity. An inference pattern that has been thoroughly consolidated should feel compelling whether or not it is valid; and the familiar experience of an invalid inference feeling obvious, until someone constructs the counterexample, is consistent with this. An inference pattern that has not been consolidated should feel like work: a procedure the self performs step by step, with each step an effort. A learner working through a proof by contradiction for the first time does not feel the conclusion as compelled; the feeling of compulsion arrives with practice, as the competence moves out of the scene and into the apparatus. The same holds for self-evidence generally, which Chapter 4 described as the way a consolidated implicit model presents in the explicit layer. On the proposal, felt necessity is self-evidence attached to a transition, where self-evidence in the ordinary sense attaches to a state.

Insight is the extreme case. A solution that has resisted deliberate work sometimes arrives all at once, after the work has stopped, with no steps visible and a strong sense that it is right. On the proposal, this is an inference whose entire production happened in the apparatus, with neither premises nor intermediate steps in the scene, so that only the conclusion appears. It should therefore present as found, given, or seen, and that is how people describe it. The same account predicts the known weakness of the experience: the sense of rightness attaches to the arrival and not to the content, so it is present for insights that later turn out to be wrong.

The proposal also carries over to any system built to the architecture, biological or not. A system that models itself richly, places conclusions in its world-model, and cannot represent the competence that produces them should present its valid and its merely consolidated inferences alike as following from their premises, and should describe them that way if it describes them at all. This is the counterpart of the prediction that such a system should report felt freedom. It inherits the same limit: a system can be trained to produce reports of this kind without having the architecture, so any test needs a control that separates reports taught to the system from reports that arise from its structure.

The proposal has a scope, and it is the same kind of scope as the free-will account. It explains the phenomenology of necessity. It says nothing about the validity of any inference. An inference can feel necessary and be invalid; an inference can be valid and feel like nothing at all to someone who has not consolidated the pattern. Validity is assessed by the criterion of Chapter 6, which works without any feeling. A rule of inference is rejected when there is a case in which it takes premises a system admits to a conclusion that contradicts input the same system admits. A rule that survives every such check has not thereby been certified as true from no perspective. It has been shown not to fail, which is what an embedded system can show.

The proposal is also neutral, in the way the free-will account is neutral about physics. Whether logical truths hold independently of every perspective is not decided by an account of why they feel as if they do. The phenomenology would be the same either way: a conclusion produced by an unrepresentable competence presents as perspective-free whether or not there is, in addition, a perspective-free fact to which it corresponds. The case against such facts, where one is made, rests on the regress of Chapter 4 and the falsity principle of Chapter 6. It does not rest on the feeling, and the feeling cannot be used against it.

## Self-reference as the formal shadow

The self-reference paradoxes show the same architecture in formal systems. A system that tries to make its own current generation into complete content ends up with one of two outcomes: incompleteness or contradiction.

Gödel's construction, set out in Chapter 5, is the first outcome (Gödel, 1931). The system represents its own derivations as objects; it cannot, from inside, certify the consistency of the apparatus doing the deriving. What it is barred from is its own generation taken as an object of certification.

The liar is the second outcome. A language that contains an unrestricted truth predicate for its own sentences, and evaluates them bivalently, can form a sentence that says of itself that it is false, and the evaluation contradicts itself. The language has made its own act of evaluation into content without any restriction, and it pays in consistency. A graded measure of agreement (Chapter 6) absorbs the same sentence by assigning it an intermediate value; that measure avoids the contradiction by declining to treat the evaluation as a two-valued fact about the sentence.

The analogy has limits. Gödel's theorems are results about consistent formal systems that represent arithmetic. Brains are not such systems; they are inconsistent, they make errors, and they are not bound by the theorems in the way a formal system is (Chapter 5). The claim here is not that the self-model is a Gödel sentence, or that consciousness is explained by incompleteness. The claim is that the formal results and the self-model's blind spot share a structure: a generating process that tries to take its own present operation as complete content meets a limit, and the limit takes the form of incompleteness where the system keeps its consistency and contradiction where it does not. In the formal case the limit can be proved. In the self-model it is a consequence of the architecture. In both, the generator does not appear completely in what it generates.

## The ceiling at cosmic scale

The cognitive ceiling of Chapter 3 (Gruber, 2026b, §9.5) is the meta-problem at cosmic scale: to separate "the universe has this structure" from "my cognition can only model the universe with this structure", the theory would need its own generating apparatus, taken as a whole, among the things it represents. The cosmological model thereby predicts its own potential unfalsifiability, which is either its best confirmation, since the model foresees exactly the limit we find, or the strongest objection to it, since the model may be an artefact of the observer — and which of the two cannot be decided from inside. The form is not peculiar to cosmology; every theory takes it whose author is a part of what it describes.

The falsity criterion still applies, and it is what keeps the ceiling from becoming a licence for anything. The cognitive ceiling prevents the framework from certifying that the universe is Class 4. It does not prevent observations from contradicting the framework. A Class 4 cosmology that predicted something our instruments deny would be refuted by the ordinary route of Chapter 6, ceiling or no ceiling. What cannot happen is the converse: no accumulation of fits establishes that the structure we find belongs to the world and not to the finder.

## The blind spot of a logician

FMT names this limit for itself (Gruber, 2026a, §10.3). The theory proposes that consciousness rests on a self-model, and the modeler and the modeled are the same system. The instrument is the object of study, and the explicit self-model cannot observe, as a whole, the implicit models whose operation it would have to describe. Any theory of consciousness generated by a conscious system will carry a residual blind spot.

The same holds for any logic generated by a logician. A logic is a serialization, made by a reference system, of competences that reference system did not derive and cannot hold, as a whole, as content. The logician can write down rules, check derivations, find contradictions and pass them to anyone. The logician cannot write down, completely, the competence that makes the rules seem compelling, because that competence is doing the writing. The rules can be shared between every perspective that accepts them; the source of their felt necessity stays behind each perspective, in the one place none of them can look.

# Chapter 8 — Useful Contradictions: The Cuts Every Theory Needs

The level distinction in the Four-Model Theory between implicit and explicit models is, at its core, a didactic pattern and not an attempt at absolute truth. The distinction imports a semipermeable boundary between the two levels it separates, and in doing so it contradicts itself, since a boundary that content crosses is not the kind of boundary a level distinction draws. The theory keeps the boundary anyway, because the explanatory power it lends is not available without it. A physical theory does the same when it separates time from space, and my own cosmological model does the same when it separates the boundary of a computation from the interior projected from it. Humans cannot understand absolute truths, because the brains doing the understanding are limited and embedded in what they try to understand, and the cuts are what understanding costs under that condition.

## What a cut is

A **cut** is a separation that a theory introduces so that a mind with fewer resources than its subject can hold the subject at all. The separated parts are treated as if each ran on its own rules, each is described in a vocabulary fitted to it, and the whole is assembled from the parts plus whatever has to be said about the place where they meet. Physics cuts the time coordinate from the space coordinates and writes its laws in the form that cut allows: rules that take one configuration to the next. My cosmological model cuts a compressed substrate from the decompressed world it projects (Gruber, 2026b). The Four-Model Theory cuts models that are stored from models that are running, and calls the first implicit and the second explicit (Gruber, 2026a).

A cut is not a patch. Chapter 1 described the patch error: an object added to a formalism to rescue it when it gets stuck, which then has to be sorted from world structure by analysis or experiment, because the formalism cannot do the sorting itself. A cut adds no object. It removes a connection, provisionally, so that two halves of a domain can be thought about one at a time. Both are legitimate tools, and both become errors in the same way, by reification: the patch when its object is taken for a thing in the world, the cut when its seam is taken for a joint in the world. The reading limit of Chapter 1, that no formalism certifies its own ontological reading, covers cuts as well as patches. A theory cannot tell from inside whether the line it drew is in the world or in the drawing.

This makes a cut a didactic pattern in the strict sense of the term: a device for understanding, to be judged by what it lets a mind see. Whether the world has the seam is a separate question, and one a theory cannot answer from inside. Chapter 5 showed that a proper part of an incompressible whole cannot hold a complete description of the whole, so a mind that is a part sees nothing without devices of this kind; a finished theory, held by a part of its own subject, is made of them.

## The implicit and the explicit

The Four-Model Theory distinguishes two kinds of models by where they live (Gruber, 2026a, §3.3). Implicit models are stored in the structure of the substrate, in brains in synaptic weights and connectivity, and they accumulate through learning and consolidation. Explicit models are patterns of activity generated from that structure and from current input; they are the running simulation, and on the theory they are what experience is. The pairing of implicit with unconscious is not stipulated. The implicit models are the reference frame through which the generation runs, and a reference frame cannot, as a whole and at the same time, be the content it generates; to represent it, a further frame would be needed, and that frame a further one. This is the regress of Chapter 4 at the scale of one organism, and it is why the implicit side is described as lights off. The theory then names the two sides as levels: a real side, the substrate, which has no experience, and a virtual side, the computational level, where qualia are constitutive (§3.4.6). The level distinction is what relocates the hard problem. Asking why synaptic weights feel like something is like asking why transistors are a spreadsheet; the question looks for a property at a level of description where that property is not defined.

The same theory describes the boundary between the two sides as permeable (§3.6). Implicit content becomes conscious when it enters what the running simulation currently represents; how readily it is drawn in varies with neuromodulation, attention and arousal, and that variation explains a range of phenomena nothing else in the theory reaches. Anosognosia is a local decrease in permeability: the implicit self-model holds the information that a limb is paralysed, and the information does not reach the explicit self-model. A psychedelic is a global increase: intermediate stages of processing that are normally implicit become accessible, which is why the imagery progresses from phosphenes through geometric form constants to whole scenes. The waking boundary leaks as well; the visual system's filling of the blind spot is a piece of processing that shows up inside the simulation as if it were world. In the paper's words, the boundary is a graded filter and not a wall.

Gradedness is not the contradiction. A boundary can be wide without ceasing to separate: the theory calls the implicit-explicit boundary a graded transition zone, and that is consistent with everything else it says, as a mountain range is a graded border between two countries that remain two. The contradiction is one of kind. A level distinction is a distinction between descriptions. Nothing is transferred from the vocabulary of voltages to the vocabulary of sums; the sum is not a voltage that crossed over, it is what the voltages are at another level of description, and the theory says itself that its claim concerns levels and not parts (§3.4.1). A permeable boundary is defined by transfer. Content that was on one side is, after a change in serotonergic tone, on the other. A line across which content travels is therefore not a line between levels of description. It is a line between two regions of one level: between what is stored and what is running, or between what is represented and what is not. The Four-Model Theory draws one line and gives it both roles. In §3.8 the implicit level is by definition outside the explicit one, which is the level reading; in §3.6 processing stages that are normally implicit become accessible, which is the transfer reading. Both cannot hold of one line.

Each reading pays for a different part of the theory. The level reading buys the relocation of the hard problem; the anaesthesia dissociation, in which cortical activity is suppressed, synaptic structure is spared and experience goes, which places experience on the activity side; and the meta-problem, since the machinery that generates the simulation is not in the simulation, which is why consciousness feels as if every explanation of it left something out. The transfer reading buys anosognosia, the psychedelic progression, hypnagogic imagery, trained access in meditation and the normal-state leaks, none of which can be described without content changing sides. Remove the level reading and the theory no longer says that the substrate cannot feel; it has to argue that separately, from the closure condition. Remove the transfer reading and it has nothing to say about the clinical dissociations it was built to explain. The theory keeps both, and the seam is visible in its own text. One sentence says that the entire structural substrate cannot simultaneously serve as the generative frame and appear as content within what it generates (§3.3); that is the level claim, restricted to the whole at once. A sentence in the same section says that the pairing of implicit with unconscious is a consequence of which side of the generative divide a content currently occupies, and not an intrinsic property of the content; that is the transfer claim. Taken as one claim, they say that the generator as a whole cannot appear and that any of its contents can. That is a consistent position. It is not the two-level ontology as the paper's headline states it, because on it the real side is not a fixed set of models. It is whatever is not being read at the moment.

The theory states where this cut fails. Its first open question asks whether all four models are virtual: the implicit models are models and not raw physics, so if they have virtual properties too, what is the real side, the physical substrate with no model-level description at all (§9)? The cut is what makes that a question. On the transfer reading the implicit models are objects at the computational level that happen not to be running, and the line between substrate and computation falls below them, somewhere in the physics. On the level reading they are the substrate. The theory needs the first reading to explain anosognosia and the second to say that the generator has no experience, so it draws them as one line and records the place where they come apart as open. I do not know a way to draw the line once that keeps both payoffs, and I do not think the inability is mine. A reader who wants the theory's explanations gets them with this seam in or not at all.

## Time and space

Physics separates the time coordinate from the space coordinates. The computational form of that separation is the form every dynamical law is written in: a configuration, and a rule that takes it to the next. That form is what makes a law expressible for a mind that holds one configuration in memory and compares it with the next. Physics has known for over a century that the separation is not absolute. Minkowski's union of space and time into one four-dimensional manifold (Minkowski, 1908/1909) makes simultaneity relative: observers in relative motion disagree about which distant events happen at the same time, and a Lorentz transformation mixes the time coordinate with the space coordinates much as a rotation mixes two spatial axes. Time is one dimension of four.

It is also not. The metric that measures intervals on that manifold carries one sign for time and the opposite sign for the three directions of space, and no transformation the theory allows removes the difference. What the sign separates is not one axis from three — any timelike direction serves some observer as a time axis, and no transformation singles one out — but one class of directions from another. An interval that is timelike for one observer is timelike for every observer, a spacelike interval is spacelike for every observer, and the order of two events that can influence each other is never reversed. The theory that unified space and time preserved their separation in the one place it had left to put it. That minus sign is the residue of the cut: what remains inside the formalism of the fact that along the timelike directions, and only along them, configurations generate configurations. The state-and-update form of a law survives the unification intact: a four-dimensional formulation is still solved forward from data on a spacelike surface, which is the same cut in another notation. In a computation time is the update; a substrate's update picks one axis in the timelike class and our level sees only the class, as it sees no preferred frame (Chapter 10); the four-dimensional block is the sequence of updates laid side by side (Chapter 11).

The cut is contradicted and kept for the same reason as the Four-Model Theory's. Without the unification there is no invariant speed and no relativity. Without the separation there is no evolution of a state and nothing to predict; Tegmark's (1997) argument that a second time dimension makes the governing equations ultrahyperbolic and prediction impossible means that a predicting brain requires the cut to be made exactly once. The geometry keeps the fourth dimension and the minus sign together, and it is right to, because each half of the contradiction is the half in which the other half's predictions are stated.

## Boundary and projection

My cosmological model separates the boundary of a computation from the interior it projects (Gruber, 2026b, §6.2). The boundary is a surface of maximum information density, holographically saturated; the interior is the lower-density, organized, observable universe that the boundary's rules decompress into. The boundary is the substrate, and it is inaccessible from within: no observer who stays inside the computational domain reads anything from behind it (§8.2). The interior is the projection. The separation is what lets the model say anything about either side. Conservation of information across a horizon is stated as a transformation between the two forms, and the arrow of time is the coarse-grained interior face of a reversible boundary computation (§8.4).

The model then closes the two into one. Its central condition is a fixed point: encode the interior onto the boundary, decompress the boundary into an interior, and the result is the universe one started with (§6.3). The rules generate the structure and the structure embodies the rules, with no specification outside. A substrate that is the lossless encoding of its own projection is not a thing apart from the projection; it is the same information in another form. Elsewhere the model says that the information needed to invert the dynamics is sequestered behind the boundary and unavailable to any interior observer (§8.4). Identical, and inaccessible. The seam is drawn in the paper's own words where it makes the parallel to the Four-Model Theory explicit: the laws of physics play the role of the permeability function, determining what information from the substrate enters the observable interior (§8.3). A substrate that cannot be read from inside, and whose selected content is what we know as physical law, is a boundary that seals and transmits. It is the implicit-explicit boundary at cosmic scale, with the same contradiction built in and imported for the same reason.

The paper holds the two faces of its fixed point apart on purpose. Read as a condition on the whole block, Φ(U) = U is a timeless consistency condition on the whole; read from inside, it is an encoding operation running in emergent time, and only the inside reading supports the model's trigger for cyclic renewal. The paper says that both readings can hold, of the same condition at different levels (§8.4). That is a cut used knowingly: two descriptions of one condition, each needed, neither reducible to the other from inside, and the place where they would have to be reconciled, outside the block, is the one place no embedded observer stands.

## Why the cuts contradict themselves

A theory of mind, a theory of spacetime and a theory of the computation beneath both show the same pattern: a separation the theory needs, a seam where the separated parts interact, and a contradiction at the seam that the theory keeps. The repetition has a reason, and the reason does not depend on who wrote the theories.

Chapter 5's first limit is capacity: a proper part of an incompressible whole cannot store a complete description of the whole, by counting (Kolmogorov, 1965; Chaitin, 1966). An embedded mind therefore compresses. One way to compress a domain, perhaps the only way open to a mind that holds one thing at a time, is to separate it: treat the parts as if each ran on its own rules, store the rules of each part, and describe the whole as the parts plus their interaction. The separation saves capacity because the rules of a part are shorter than the rules of the whole. It costs something at the place where the parts interact. If they did not interact there would be no whole to describe, only two domains mentioned together; so the interaction is a fact about the whole and about neither part, and it has to be stored somewhere. It is stored at the seam, as a term that belongs to neither part's rules and is written in a vocabulary built for parts in isolation. In the three theories it is, respectively, a minus sign among plus signs, a boundary with a transfer rate, and a sealed substrate whose selected content is the laws.

That is why the seam reads as a contradiction and not as an awkwardness that better wording would remove. The vocabulary of the parts supplies the predicates: a dimension is spatial, a level boundary is impassable, a substrate is sealed. The seam, described in that vocabulary, negates each predicate in the sentence that applies it. The theory has no other vocabulary for the seam, because the only vocabulary it has is the one the cut produced. A description without a seam would be a description without a cut, of the whole as one thing, and that is what the part cannot hold; Chapter 7 added that even if it could, the apparatus doing the holding would be missing from it. A self-contradicting cut is what compression by a part looks like from inside the compression. It is expected. A theory of an embedded mind with no seam in it would be evidence that its author had not found where the cut was made.

Absolute truth, in the sense Chapter 6 gave the term, is truth from no perspective. A description with no cut would be that: the whole, held without separation, by nothing in particular. This is why an embedded brain cannot understand absolute truths, and the reason is not confined to humans. Any embedded perspective with fewer resources than the whole makes cuts, every cut has a seam, and so the best theory of any such perspective contradicts itself somewhere, in the place where it put the interaction it could not afford to describe.

## Using a cut without being misled

A contradiction inside a single derivation is fatal: from a statement and its denial anything follows. A theory can nevertheless keep a self-contradicting cut without losing everything, because a theory is not one derivation. In the terms of Chapter 4 it is a reference system, a set of models each used within a region, assembled on a base that was never derived. The cut appears with one strength in one region and another strength in another; the level reading of the implicit-explicit boundary carries the hard-problem relocation, and the transfer reading carries anosognosia. The contradiction lives between the regions. It becomes an inconsistency only in an inference that imports both strengths at once, and the first discipline of using a cut is that no derivation may do that. Nothing in the Four-Model Theory is to be concluded from the premise that the implicit models are a fixed and sealed set, because the theory says elsewhere that they are not. What may be concluded is what follows from the generator as a whole being unable to appear and from any content being able to change sides. As far as I can see, the theory's stated predictions are of that kind: they are written over the graded boundary and over the closure of the running self-model, and none of them requires the real side to be a determinate set of models. The meta-problem shows that the two readings can also meet inside one explanation without contradiction, once each is scoped. That something is always left out of every account consciousness gives of itself needs only the whole-at-once constraint: the generator as a whole cannot be content of its own product. That the self nonetheless catches occasional glimpses of its machinery needs only gradedness: particular contents can cross. The two claims are about different things, the whole and its parts, and the explanation uses both without importing either at the other's strength (Gruber, 2026a, §3.8).

The second discipline is to keep the seam visible and to say, for each cut, where it fails. The Four-Model Theory's first open question does this for the implicit-explicit cut, and the cosmological model's two readings of its fixed point do it for the boundary-projection cut; the signature does it for spacetime, though physics does not usually put it that way. A hidden seam is a seam that will be used as if it were not there, and that use is the step from a cut to a reification: the theory concludes that because its description has two levels the world has two kinds of thing, or that because its geometry has four axes the world is a block, or that because its boundary is sealed in the model nothing from behind it can be known. Any of those conclusions may happen to be true. None of them follows from the cut.

The third discipline is that the falsity criterion of Chapter 6 applies unchanged. A cut is not refuted by having a seam; every cut has one. It is refuted when a prediction that depends on it contradicts input the theory has admitted. Permeability was written with its own falsifiers for this reason: a non-monotonic relation between serotonergic agonism and the entropy measures proposed to track permeability, or identical neural signatures for the global increase under a psychedelic and the local decrease in anosognosia, would count against the construct (Gruber, 2026a, §3.6), and neither failure would have anything to do with the boundary being also a level. The cut is judged by what the predictions made with it do against the world, which is the only judgment an embedded perspective can pass on anything.

The fourth limit of Chapter 5 is a cut as well: a description of a process is not an instance of it. That is a separation between a thing and its account, and it has a seam like the others. Chapter 14 locates it: the same structure is a description to the observer who reads it and a world to the observer whose frame it is, and which side of the cut a structure falls on depends on the side of the generating relation from which one reads. The test of these cuts is the one every cut has. Whether a psychedelic can return to a patient with anosognosia the knowledge of her own paralysis is a question an experiment can answer, and the implicit-explicit boundary was drawn so that it could be asked.

# Part III — Physics From Inside

# Chapter 9 — Compressed Data Is Not an Assumption

## The suspicion

Every physical law we have was found by observers at one level of one world, with instruments made of the things the law describes. General relativity predicts its own breakdown: it produces singularities at which its equations stop returning values, and it says nothing about the regime in which quantum effects and gravity are both strong. Quantum mechanics returns probabilities for the records a measurement will produce, given a preparation, and is silent about what a measurement is, what exists between two of them, and why the records are the ones they are. Both theories have been tested to high precision within their scopes. Neither says where its scope ends; that is learned from the other theory, or from an experiment neither predicted.

My suspicion is this. The laws we have — relativity, quantum mechanics, locality, reversibility, the conservation of probability — describe our level and only our level. General relativity is incomplete by its own admission. Quantum mechanics matches reality closely within its scope, and only for that scope; and since it supplies no account of what is there, it is better described as a probability calculus than as a physical theory. The reconstructions of quantum theory from information-theoretic axioms (Hardy, 2001) and the QBist reading of the quantum state as an agent's degrees of belief make this explicit from inside physics: the formalism can be rebuilt from postulates about information and probability in which no physical object is named. A calculus matched to a scope tells us nothing about how wrong it is beyond the scope. Newtonian gravity agreed with every planetary observation to the limit of measurement for two centuries, except for forty-three seconds of arc per century in the perihelion of Mercury; the Uranus patch that turned out to be Neptune (Chapter 1) gave no sign that the next anomaly would not yield to the same remedy. Its mechanism, a force acting instantly across empty space, is absent from the theory that replaced it. The precision was real. The ontology was not. Nothing inside Newtonian gravity could have told its users which of its objects were the world and which were the representation — Chapter 1's reading limit. The same holds of every current theory until a deeper one shows where its scope ends, and that deeper theory will be in the same position relative to the one after it.

## Where the suspicion overreaches

Pushed one step further, the suspicion overreaches. The step is an inference: X holds only at our level; therefore X is an assumption; therefore X should be dropped. Applied to locality, to reversibility, to probability conservation, to the metric signature, it makes each of them a candidate for deletion. The inference fails at both steps.

The first step confuses three statuses a statement can have. A statement can be an axiom of a theory, assumed in order to derive the rest. It can be a regularity in data, measured and recorded. And it can be a claim about what exists. The conservation of probability is an axiom of the quantum formalism, a regularity in every experiment ever recorded, and an ontological claim only if someone makes it one. "Holds at our level" is a statement about the second status: the regularity has been measured here and not elsewhere. It says nothing about whether the theory that contains it treats it as an axiom, and nothing about whether anyone has reified it. A law that holds at our level is compressed data. It summarizes a very large number of measurements in a short statement that reproduces them. Being valid within a scope is what a compression is: a compression is a regularity in some data, and the data have an extent.

The second step treats "assumption" as a synonym for "false". An assumption is a statement used without being derived. Most assumptions in physics are compressions promoted to postulates because they have never failed within the domain being formalized; the promotion is a bookkeeping decision about where to start, and it says nothing about truth either way. The fallacy identified in Chapter 1 was reification: taking the compression for the fundamental thing. Using a compression inside its scope is not reification. Taking it to be the substrate is.

The consequence of the overreach is the decisive objection to it. Suppose every regularity that holds only at our level is discarded as an artefact. Then a theory of the substrate has nothing left to reproduce. Its success condition has been emptied: there is no observation it must recover and no observation that could refute it, and it becomes exactly as untestable as the interpretations of quantum mechanics I criticize for predicting no deviation from the formalism. The regularities at our level are the only data any substrate theory will ever be tested against.

## Precision about quantum mechanics

Two formulations conflate things the programme must keep apart.

Quantum mechanics is sometimes charged with inconsistency on account of its infinities. The charge is wrong. Non-relativistic quantum mechanics — a Hilbert space, a unitary evolution, the Born rule — is a mathematically consistent structure; nothing in it derives a contradiction. The infinities belong to quantum field theory, the relativistic extension, where they appear in perturbative calculations and are removed by renormalization. Renormalization is a legitimate target for Chapter 1's suspicion about patches, and the physicists who built it said as much; a patch is not an inconsistency, and a theory that needs one is not thereby contradictory. The defect quantum mechanics does have is incompleteness: it does not contain gravity, and general relativity does not contain it. Incompleteness and inconsistency are different defects. A consistent theory can be arbitrarily incomplete; an inconsistent theory proves everything and is complete in the worst possible way.

The second formulation is that quantum mechanics is not a physical theory. The defensible reading is narrower than the sentence. Quantum mechanics is a theory of what observers will record. It takes a preparation and a measurement procedure as inputs and returns a distribution over records; it does not say what the system is doing between preparation and record, and every attempt to add such an account — pilot waves, branching worlds, spontaneous collapse, deterministic automata — adds something the formalism does not require and predicts, in most cases, nothing the formalism does not predict. That is what it means to call it a probability calculus without an ontology, and the information-theoretic reconstructions show that the calculus can be derived from postulates in which no physical object appears (Hardy, 2001). None of this makes the calculus false, approximate, or unphysical within its scope. It makes it silent about the substrate.

## Compression and its boundary

A compression carries more than the data it summarizes. It carries, at the edges of its scope, the first information about the level below.

The ideal gas law is the plain case. Pressure times volume is proportional to temperature: one line, and it reproduces the behaviour of every dilute gas ever measured. It is a compression, and nothing about it is an assumption about the nature of gases; it was found in the data before kinetic theory was accepted. It fails at high density and low temperature, and the manner of its failure — gases that occupy more volume than the law allows, and that attract one another into liquids — is the signature of molecules with a finite size and a short-range force between them. The deeper theory, statistical mechanics, reproduces the law exactly where it held and explains the failure exactly where it failed. The law was never the substrate. It was the substrate's most visible regularity at the level where dilute gases are observed, and the boundary of its scope was the first measurement of what lay underneath.

Reification would have been to conclude that each molecule obeys the gas law. Nobody did, because molecules were discovered through the law's failure. Deletion would have been to discard the gas law as a level-bound artefact before statistical mechanics existed. Had that been done, there would have been no failure to explain and no boundary to measure, and kinetic theory would have had nothing to reproduce. The gas law's scope-boundedness was no reason to distrust it. It was the one place the next level showed through.

The pattern generalizes. A regularity that holds only at our level is a compression whose scope is our level, and its scope boundary — the regime in which it fails — is the only place where the structure of the level below becomes observable from here. Quantum mechanics and general relativity each predict where the other's scope ends, and both predict that neither holds at the Planck scale. Those boundaries are the only data about the substrate any observer at our level has. A substrate theory is obliged to reproduce the regularities inside the boundaries and is tested by what it predicts at them.

A law written in mathematics is a world-model's compression serialized, its validity within scope is the whole of its content, and whether its symbols — the wavefunction, the metric, the pressure — name things is the ontological reading no formalism certifies (Chapter 1).

## Four regularities, read as compressions

The regularities my suspicion singled out can each be read this way, and reading them so fixes what has to be decided about each.

Locality. No influence has been measured to outrun light, and no operation on one system changes the statistics observed at another. That is the compression. Its reification is the claim that the substrate is a set of separate things at separate places. Its scope boundary is already visible: entangled systems violate the Bell inequalities, so one of the premises of any local account of their correlations fails somewhere, and which one fails is the question Bell's theorem leaves open.

Reversibility. The microscopic dynamics, wherever they have been measured, can be run backwards — unitarity in the quantum formalism, invertible equations of motion in the classical one — while every macroscopic process runs one way. That is a pair of compressions, one for each scale, and the relation between them is understood: the one-way behaviour comes from a boundary condition and a coarse-graining, the two-way behaviour from the law. The reification is the claim that the substrate is reversible because the formalism is. The scope boundary is wherever the formalism stops, which nobody has found. Experiments that look for spontaneous collapse — information leaking out of unitary evolution and showing up as noise — keep tightening their limits and have found nothing.

Probability conservation. In every experiment, something happens, and the probabilities of the things that can happen add to one. The compression is nearly empty, as a compression should be when the regularity is nearly universal. Its reification is the inference from "probabilities add to one" to "the evolution is reversible", which does not follow (Chapter 11).

The signature. One speed is the same for every observer, and the intervals observers agree on are those in which time and space enter with opposite signs. That is the compression. The reification would be the claim that the world is a four-dimensional manifold carrying that metric; the deflation would be the claim that the sign is an assumption bolted on to make general relativity work. Neither is what the data say. The data say there is an invariant speed.

In each case the same three questions apply: what has been measured, what has been inferred from it beyond the measurement, and where the measurement stops. The first is the compression. The second is where reification enters. The third is where the substrate shows.

## The productive programme

The programme that replaces deletion has three steps. List the regularities observed at our level. Deny that any of them is fundamental. Find a substrate that reproduces them within their scopes and predicts where each scope ends.

This is the method of my cosmology paper (Gruber, 2026b). The paper assumes, and labels as an assumption, that the substrate is deterministic. It then takes what physics has recorded at our level as data: that the universe supports universal computation, that embedded systems perform computation with no predictive shortcut, that critical dynamics occur and sustain themselves, that static matter, periodic motion and fractal structure all exist. It asks which classes of deterministic rule could produce that data, and eliminates the ones that could not: a static or periodic rule cannot generate open-ended computation, a reducible rule with a closed form would make the universe predictable without being run, and lawless dynamics would make the success of physics an unexplained coincidence. What remains is the class at the edge of chaos, by elimination and conditional on the assumption (Gruber, 2026b, §3.2–3.3). At no point does the argument treat a measured regularity as false because it holds at our level. Every regularity is retained as a constraint the substrate must satisfy; what is denied is only that any of them is the substrate.

What the method obliges is as specific as what it forbids. A substrate theory must reproduce the quantum statistics within quantum mechanics' scope and the relativistic geometry within general relativity's. It may deny that either is fundamental. It may not deny that either is data, and it is refuted the moment it contradicts a measured regularity inside that regularity's scope. The asymmetry of Chapter 6 applies: a contradiction with admitted input is decidable here and inherited by every successor; reproducing the input is not confirmation, since any number of substrates might reproduce it. A substrate theory is tested at the scope boundaries, where it predicts something the compressions do not. The automaton derivations of the free field equations give the form such a prediction takes: a Lorentz symmetry that is exact in the long-wavelength limit and deformed near the lattice scale, so that the dispersion of a free field at wavelengths approaching the lattice scale departs from the relativistic relation by an amount the compression puts at zero (D'Ariano & Perinotti, 2014). Whether any such departure exists is an empirical question, and it is the right kind of question: it is asked at a scope boundary, and the compression and the substrate theory answer it differently.

## What the scope boundary tells, and what it does not

Matching reality closely within a scope tells nothing about how wrong a theory is beyond the scope. Newtonian gravity was precise to the limit of measurement for two centuries and wrong about its own mechanism from the first day. Quantum mechanics is precise to many decimal places and silent about its mechanism, and the silence may hide an error of the same size as Newton's or none at all; the precision does not say.

Two inferences are blocked by this, and they are the same error with opposite signs. One cannot infer from the precision of quantum mechanics that its objects exist: that amplitudes rotate in a complex plane, that a wavefunction occupies configuration space, that the Hilbert space is the arena of the world. That inference is reification. One also cannot infer from its scope-boundedness that its objects do not exist, or that its regularities are artefacts to be dropped. That inference is deletion, and it discards the data. What remains between the two is a flat statement of what a compression's content is: its predictions within its scope. Its ontology stays open until a deeper theory shows where the scope ends — and the deeper theory settles the ontology only relative to its own scope, from inside which it was built. There is no theory of a level that was not made at some level. The generator of a description never appears complete in the description, and a description of physics made by physical systems is no exception.

## From laws to experiments

Laws are compressions; the compressions are data; the substrate is constrained by the data and tested at the boundaries. What remains is how an embedded observer obtains the data at all. Every regularity listed above was established by experiment, and an experiment is performed from inside. The apparatus is made of the matter whose laws are under test. The settings are chosen by processes that run on the same substrate as the system being measured. The experimenter who reads the record is a subprocess of the computation whose randomness, locality and independence the experiment is meant to probe.

# Chapter 10 — Randomness, Independence, and the Observer Inside the Experiment

## Two kinds of randomness

A deterministic rule with a finite description cannot produce output more complex than itself. The statement is Kolmogorov's and Chaitin's (Kolmogorov, 1965; Chaitin, 1966): the complexity of a string is the length of the shortest program that produces it, and the output of a rule applied to an initial condition is produced by the program "apply this rule to this initial condition for this many steps", whose length is fixed apart from the step count. Any prefix of the output of a cellular automaton is therefore compressible to the rule, the initial condition and a step counter; a sequence that no program shorter than itself can produce — a genuinely random one, in the only definition that makes the word precise — is beyond any such rule. My cosmology paper calls randomness of that kind **ontic**, generated by no expressible rule, and reserves a class for it that no automaton occupies (Gruber, 2026b, §2.3–2.4).

What an automaton can produce is **effective** randomness: output that passes every statistical test an observer with bounded resources can run, and that cannot be predicted except by running the rule. Rule 30 (Chapter 5) is the standard case. The rules that behave like this — irreducible, and universal where universality has been proved (Cook, 2004, for Rule 110) — are the Class 4 rules, and effective randomness is their ordinary product.

An observer inside such a system cannot tell the two kinds apart, and the reason is structural. The observer is a subprocess of the computation it is trying to classify, with strictly fewer cells and a slower clock than the whole (Gruber, 2026b, §2.5). To certify that a sequence is ontically random it would have to show that no rule produces it, which is a claim about every program, and the quantity that would settle it — the sequence's Kolmogorov complexity — is not computable by any procedure. To certify that a sequence is effectively random it would have to exhibit the rule, which for an irreducible rule means running the computation it is part of, with fewer resources than the computation has. Neither certification is available from inside. Quantum measurement outcomes, which pass every statistical test, can on this reading be effective randomness from a deterministic substrate, which is the position of 't Hooft's cellular automaton interpretation ('t Hooft, 2016) and the assumption my cosmology paper declares (Gruber, 2026b, §3.2). The limit applies in both directions: the assumption cannot be confirmed from inside either. What the observer measures is indistinguishability.

The limit applies to the certification of randomness by experiment as well. Protocols exist that certify randomness from the violation of a Bell inequality with no trust placed in the devices; they are the strongest randomness certification physics has. Each of them assumes, as an input, that the settings fed to the devices were chosen independently of the devices' internal state. The certification cannot reach the assumption it stands on.

## What Bell's theorem assumes

Bell's theorem is a constraint on correlations.

Two wings of an experiment, far apart. At each wing an observer chooses a setting — x on the left, y on the right — and obtains an outcome, a or b. Suppose there is some variable λ, the complete state of whatever the two wings share from their common past, such that three things hold. First, **factorization**: given λ and the local settings, the outcomes are independent, P(a,b|x,y,λ) = P(a|x,λ)·P(b|y,λ). This is Bell's local causality: once everything in the shared past is specified, what happens at one wing depends on that wing's setting and on nothing at the other. Second, **measurement independence**: the distribution of λ does not depend on the settings, P(λ|x,y) = P(λ). Third, each run has one outcome at each wing. From these premises the CHSH combination of correlations is bounded, S ≤ 2. Quantum mechanics predicts S up to 2√2 for suitable entangled states and settings, and experiments measure values above 2, with the known loopholes since closed.

Three things are not among the premises. Determinism is not assumed; the outcomes may be stochastic given λ. Randomness is not assumed; nothing in the derivation requires that any process be ontically random. And the truth of quantum mechanics is not assumed; the bound is derived from the premises alone, and the violation is measured. Any theory of the substrate, an automaton included, must reproduce correlations with S > 2, and must therefore give up at least one premise.

The ways out correspond to the premises. Give up factorization, and the theory is nonlocal in Bell's sense: Bohmian mechanics does this, is fully deterministic, retains measurement independence, and reproduces the predictions of non-relativistic quantum mechanics exactly. Give up measurement independence, and the theory is superdeterministic, or retrocausal, which is a way of making λ depend on settings chosen later. Give up single outcomes, and the theory branches. Determinism appears on none of these routes because it is not a premise, and a deterministic world does not, by being deterministic, take any of them. Bohmian mechanics and the many-worlds interpretation are both deterministic and take different routes; quantum mechanics does not require ontic randomness.

I have held a stronger objection: that in a deterministic world everything with a shared past is correlated, so that measurement independence is unattainable in principle. It is wrong. Correlation requires more than a shared past; it requires that the dependency structure of the dynamics route information from the common past into both variables in a way that survives everything in between. Take the settings from the binary digits of π, computed by a machine in the laboratory, and the hidden state from the polarization of photon pairs emitted by a crystal. Both are determined. Both lie in the causal past of the measurement. Nothing in the dynamics correlates the digits of π with the state of the crystal, and a superdeterminist owes the mechanism that would. Determinism makes every variable a function of the initial state; it does not make every pair of variables statistically dependent, and chaotic mixing generically destroys whatever dependence the common past did seed. That is why randomized trials work in a world that may be deterministic throughout. Measurement independence is not a claim that the settings are uncaused. It is a claim about one joint distribution, P(λ, x, y), and determinism says nothing about which joint distribution obtains.

## The observer who cannot step outside

A different claim does hold, and it does not depend on determinism at all.

Measurement independence is an assumption about the joint distribution of the hidden state and the settings. Testing it requires settings whose independence from the hidden state is known. The only way to know that is to generate the settings by a process whose independence from the source is assumed, which is the assumption under test. An experimenter may push the setting generator as far from the source as the universe allows — to a random number generator, to a human choice, to the light of a quasar — and at each step the question is the same: is this generator independent of λ? The cosmic Bell test did exactly this (Rauch et al., 2018). Settings were fixed by whether arriving photons from two quasars were redder or bluer than a threshold wavelength, photons that had travelled for billions of years, 7.8 billion in the case of the nearer source; under the stated assumptions — that the setting was fixed when the light was emitted and that no influence outruns light — any local common cause of settings and hidden state must have acted before that light left, which the authors compute as the exclusion of such a mechanism from about 96 per cent of the space-time volume of the experiment's past light cone. That is a constraint on a specified class of explanations under specified assumptions. It is not a demonstration that the settings and the hidden state have no common dependence, and the authors do not claim one; the remaining few per cent include the big bang, and the assumptions include the locality whose denial is one of Bell's exits.

The limit is a first-person limit, and first-person means any embedded perspective. No observer can certify, from inside, its independence from the system it is part of. The process that generates its choices runs on the same substrate as the process that generates the outcomes it will measure; whether the two are independent is a fact about the substrate's joint distribution, and that distribution is what the observer has no access to except through experiments whose settings it must choose. The generator of the settings cannot appear in the experiment as a measured object. It appears only as an assumption about its own independence. Wheeler's suggestion that there is only one electron, threading backwards and forwards through time, is the extreme picture of what cannot be excluded: Feynman's Nobel lecture records the suggestion, and records his own objection, that the world does not contain as many positrons as the picture requires (Feynman, 1965). It is a historical possibility and not a measured coupling, and it is the limiting case of a question that has no inside answer: how tightly is this apparatus coupled to that quasar?

The inability to certify independence is not evidence of coupling. By the asymmetry of Chapter 6, a refutation requires a specific contradiction with admitted input; "it cannot be excluded" is not an input. And a theory that is permitted to fit an arbitrary dependence between settings and hidden state predicts nothing: for any observed correlation there is a dependence that produces it, so no observation refutes the theory and no observation confirms it. The reflexive dismissal of superdeterminism as a conspiracy is poorly argued, and Hossenfelder and Palmer (2020) are right that most of the standard objections rest on intuitions about linear classical systems; the charge that holds is the narrower one, that a superdeterministic theory owes a specific dependence and a specific prediction that follows from it. Hossenfelder and Palmer also say where such a theory could be tested — in regimes where its specific dependence predicts departures from quantum statistics. That is the shape a testable superdeterminism has. A merely permitted one has no shape at all.

## Locality and its price

The second premise, factorization, is the one my cosmology paper gives up.

Factorization presupposes that the two wings are two systems in two regions, separated by a spacelike gap, whose joint state decomposes into local states once the shared past is specified. If spacetime is an emergent projection of a lower-dimensional computation, that presupposition is a fact about the projection and need not hold at the substrate. Cells adjacent in the substrate need not be adjacent in the emergent spacetime, and two points at opposite ends of the emergent space may be one locus in the substrate's encoding. Locality at our level — no measured influence outruns light, and no operation on one system changes the statistics at another — is then a regularity of the projection, compressed data in the sense of Chapter 9, and no evidence of locality at the bottom. The cosmology paper takes exactly this route: an entangled pair is one configuration on the holographic boundary that appears as two separated particles only in the decompressed interior; the correlations are two interior reflections of one locus, and no influence crosses the gap because at the substrate there is no gap (Gruber, 2026b, §6.5). This is Bell's nonlocality option, the oldest consistent one, and Bohmian mechanics is the existence proof that the corner of theory space it occupies — deterministic, nonlocal, measurement independence retained — is coherent.

Economy follows. A theory that gives up factorization has already escaped the bound; it does not also need to give up measurement independence. Superdeterminism and nonlocal projection are two payments for one debt, and a theory that makes both has overpaid. My cosmology paper rejects factorization and keeps measurement independence. It keeps it as every Bell-type argument does, by assumption: the paper's remark that the apparatus settings are not entangled with the pair before the measurement gives a reason to expect the assumption to hold and does not derive it, since nothing inside a world certifies that its setting generators are independent of what they measure. The choice is the economical one.

The price of the choice is specific, and the paper names it (Gruber, 2026b, §6.5). Giving up factorization dissolves the Bell bound too thoroughly: the correlations that no-signalling alone permits extend to S = 4, while quantum mechanics and nature stop at Tsirelson's bound of 2√2. A nonlocal projection therefore owes an account of why its correlations stop exactly there, and reproducing the quantum value is a near-empty achievement until it does, since any number of nonlocal mechanisms can be tuned to emit 2√2. The paper offers a conditional derivation and names the condition; the debt is open.

## No-signalling and the fork

The regularity that disciplines every nonlocal proposal is no-signalling: the statistics at one wing do not depend on the setting chosen at the other, so the correlations cannot be used to send a message. Within quantum mechanics this is a theorem. In nature it is a measured regularity, confirmed every time an entanglement experiment is run and never violated. It is compressed data in the strict sense of the previous chapter: without a theory of the substrate, nobody can know that information transfer at our level is impossible, only that it has not been observed within the scope of the theories we have. A lattice substrate, moreover, has something relativity lacks: a preferred frame, the lattice itself. The paradoxes of faster-than-light signalling — messages that arrive before they are sent — arise from combining superluminal transfer with Lorentz invariance, which lets a boost turn a fast signal into a backwards one. With a preferred frame there is no such boost. A substrate could carry influences faster than light without any causal loop, and the only consequence at our level would be a violation of Lorentz invariance at the scale where the frame shows.

There is a theorem that turns this into a test. Bancal and colleagues (Bancal et al., 2012) consider a specific class of models: Bell correlations are produced by hidden influences that propagate at some finite speed v, faster than light but finite, in a preferred frame, and the model reproduces the quantum predictions whenever its influences arrive in time. They show that for any such v there are four-party arrangements in which the model must do one of two things: predict correlations that differ from quantum mechanics, or produce correlations that themselves allow signalling faster than light — not hidden signalling in the substrate, but signalling available to the parties at our level. The result constrains that class and only that class: finite propagation speed, preferred frame, influences that arrive or do not. A projection in which nothing propagates because the two wings are one locus is not a member of the class, and the theorem says nothing against it. Scoped this way, the result is an asset to the substrate view, because it produces a fork with a different prediction on each branch. If the substrate's nonlocality is carried by influences moving at a finite speed through the lattice, then signalling exists in principle and multi-party experiments can look for it. If the projection is genuinely nonlocal — one locus, nothing moving — there is no signalling at any speed. My cosmology paper asserts the second branch (Gruber, 2026b, §6.5): correlations, never signals. It is a commitment, and the fork states what would refute it: any finite speed at which influence travels inside the lattice.

## What the automata have done

Cellular-automaton physics has produced specific results, each with a specific scope.

D'Ariano and Perinotti (2014) derive the Dirac equation from a quantum cellular automaton — a lattice of cells each carrying a finite-dimensional quantum system, updated by a unitary step — under the principles of unitarity, locality, homogeneity and a discrete isotropy, with the Dirac dynamics recovered in a limit of wavelengths long compared with the lattice scale. Lorentz symmetry emerges approximately in that limit and is deformed near the lattice scale, which is in principle a prediction. Two scopes attach to the result. The automaton is already quantum: it does not derive quantum mechanics from a classical rule, and its cells carry amplitudes from the start. And the fields are free: no interaction, no gravity. 't Hooft's programme ('t Hooft, 2016) starts further down, with classical states — ontological states, in his term — that evolve by permutations, and treats the quantum formalism as a tool for describing their statistics; by his own account no model has yet been found that reproduces known particle physics. Wolfram's (2002) programme has produced a vast catalogue of simple rules with complex behaviour and the Principle of Computational Equivalence as a conjecture; it has not produced a quantitative prediction. Free fields from automata exist. Interacting fields with gravity from an automaton exist in no programme.

The argument from universality adds less than it seems to. It runs: a Class 4 automaton is Turing complete, so it should be easy to produce in it uncountably many systems that behave, on the projected plane, exactly as the Bell experiments require. Two things are wrong with it. The rules are countable: a rule is a finite description, and there are only countably many finite descriptions; what is uncountable is the set of infinite configurations, and the two are different objects to count. And universality guarantees existence, not abundance. A universal automaton can simulate the quantum formalism, so an automaton exists whose emergent description contains Bell-violating correlations; that is cheap, and it says nothing about how many rules do it, how large a rule it takes, or whether a random rule applied to random data will. The simulation that universality guarantees stores amplitudes globally — which is the nonlocal projection again, at exponential cost. The problem that remains is the one no programme has solved: the minimal natural rule from which the quantum statistics, the relativistic geometry and gravity all emerge without being written in by hand. That the behaviour exists somewhere in rule space was never in doubt. Where, and how rarely, is the question.

## What an embedded experimenter establishes

An observer inside the computation can measure correlations. It can refute specific models — local ones, finite-speed ones, superdeterministic ones that commit to a dependence — by finding correlations they forbid. It can place the substrate on a branch of Bancal's fork by looking for signalling in multi-party experiments. It cannot certify that any sequence is ontically random, cannot certify its own independence from the system it measures, and cannot read off from locality at its level whether the substrate is local. Those three are not failures of apparatus. They are the condition of being a subprocess of what one measures. Every experiment is also ordered in time, and the clock by which it is ordered is inside too.

# Chapter 11 — Time Without a Clock

## The suspicion

Relativity made time a coordinate on a four-dimensional manifold and then distinguished it from the other three by a sign. The textbook gloss is that all four dimensions are on an equal footing and that we happen to move through one of them at the speed of light: the whole observable universe does, which is an asymmetry strange enough to make one suspect that it belongs to the observer rather than to the world. The suspicion has three parts. Time is not obviously a dimension. The metric signature, the minus sign that separates time from space, is an assumption needed to make general relativity work, not a trace of reality. And dynamics are not fundamental; they emerge as a point of view, after the fact, from a structure that does not itself change.

The third part stands, and Chapter 14 defends it. The first two rest, as stated, on formal artefacts.

## Three replies

The first concerns the sentence "we all move through spacetime at the speed of light." It is true, and it is empty. The four-velocity of a body is the derivative of its position in spacetime with respect to its own proper time, and proper time is defined so that the four-velocity has magnitude c — the normalization is written into the definition. The statement that everything moves at c through spacetime is the statement that the four-velocity has the magnitude the four-velocity was defined to have. It is a tautology of the bookkeeping, and it is exactly the kind of artefact Chapter 1 warned about: a formal consequence of a notation, read as a fact about the world. It also fails for light, which has no proper time, and so no four-velocity in this sense at all. What the bookkeeping does record is that there is an invariant speed, the same in every frame; that is a fact, and the four-velocity's magnitude is one of its costumes.

The second concerns the signature. It does not come from general relativity, and it is not an assumption added to make general relativity work. It is in Maxwell's equations, published fifty years before general relativity: the wave operator that governs light, ∂²/∂t² − c²∇², carries the minus sign between the time derivative and the space derivatives, and that minus sign is the Lorentzian signature before anyone had named it. Minkowski's geometry of 1908 made the sign into the structure of a space, with light cones as its null directions (Minkowski, 1908/1909). The signature is the geometric form of an invariant speed: if there is one speed that every observer measures the same, the intervals that observers agree on are the ones in which time and space enter with opposite signs. General relativity inherited the signature; it did not introduce it.

The signature is also, in a definite sense, what my own view predicts, and the sense is narrower than the reply usually given. A substrate that updates locally, one cell influencing its neighbours per step, has a bounded propagation speed, and therefore a **dependency cone**: the set of cells whose state can affect a given cell at a given step. A dependency cone is not Lorentz symmetry. A lattice has a preferred frame and a discrete symmetry group; the cone is a diamond, or a cube, with corners; nothing about it is invariant under boosts. Lorentz symmetry, if it emerges, emerges in a limit and under further conditions, and D'Ariano and Perinotti (2014) needed unitarity, homogeneity and isotropy as well as locality, and a limit of long wavelengths, to recover it from their automaton — approximately, with deformations at the lattice scale. What the dependency cone gives by itself is weaker and correct: a bounded information speed, which at our level appears as an invariant speed, which the signature encodes. The signature is a trace of bounded information speed at our level. That is consistent with the substrate view and is some evidence for it; it is not an assumption against it.

The third concerns the number of time dimensions. If all dimensions were equal, nothing would single out one of them as time, and the number of time-like directions would be a free parameter. It is not free. Tegmark (1997) showed that with more than one time dimension the field equations of physics become ultrahyperbolic: the initial-value problem is ill-posed, data on a surface do not determine the solution, and an observer could predict nothing. With one time dimension the equations are hyperbolic and prediction is possible. The argument is the anthropic argument of Chapter 3 applied to time: brains that survive by predicting likely futures can exist only where there is exactly one time dimension. That the whole observable universe shares exactly one time dimension is then no egocentric accident; it is a condition on there being anyone to observe it. The asymmetry is real, and it is in the world, in the sense that the world contains observers only where the asymmetry holds.

## Time is the update

In a computation, time is not a dimension.

A cellular automaton has a lattice and a rule. The lattice is space: an index set of cells that exist simultaneously, with an adjacency relation among them. The rule is the update: given a configuration of the lattice, it produces the next. Time is the update, the successor relation between configurations. It is not a fourth axis of the lattice, and no cell lies along it. The familiar picture of a one-dimensional automaton — rows stacked downwards, each the successor of the one above, forming a two-dimensional pattern — is a representation that lays the update out as if it were a spatial axis. It is useful, and it is a choice. The rows exist in sequence, generated one from the other; the picture exists all at once, because somebody drew it.

Treating time as a fourth coordinate of spacetime is the same choice made at the level of physics. It is the representation under which the update appears as a coordinate, and it is productive: it makes the symmetries of the equations visible as symmetries of a space. What remains of time's difference from space inside the representation is the minus sign. Within a spacelike slice, the relations are simultaneous constraints among the cells of one configuration. Across slices, along any timelike direction, the relations are dependencies between configurations, one generating the next. The signature is where the cut shows through — the cut between time and space that Chapter 8 names as one every physical theory makes in order to be thinkable, and that the theory then contradicts by treating the two as coordinates of one manifold. The residue of the cut is the sign. It does not mark one of the four coordinates as the time axis; relativity has no such axis, since any timelike direction serves some observer as one. It records that one class of directions is unlike the others: the directions along which configurations generate configurations. The substrate's update picks a single axis in that class, the lattice's own; our level sees only the class, as it sees no preferred frame (Chapter 10).

The update is the generating relation of the computation, and the principle of Chapter 5 holds of it: the update as such never appears complete in any configuration as content; what appears is its trace, the order of configurations. An observer inside the computation is a pattern in configurations. It can represent, as content, the states of cells and the relations among them in a configuration, the order of configurations as recorded in its own memory, and the rule table itself, if it has found it; the generator's contents are not barred. What it cannot represent is the step that is producing the configuration it is representing in, because that step is in no configuration. It is between them. What the observer has of that step is order: that this configuration follows that one. Time is what an embedded observer never has complete as content, only as order, and that is the first reason time is not obviously a dimension.

## The inherited arrow

A reversible update has no direction of its own. It can be run from either end, and nothing in the rule says which end is the past. The direction has to come from somewhere, and in both of my theories it comes from the same place.

My cosmology paper commits to a reversible substrate: no two distinct substrate states evolve to the same state, and total information is conserved (Gruber, 2026b, §8.4). The arrow of time inside is emergent, and it comes from two things together. One is a boundary condition: the compressed phase from which each cycle of the interior decompresses, the counterpart in that architecture of the Past Hypothesis — the low-entropy early universe that on the standard analysis is the source of the thermodynamic arrow (Albert, 2000). The other is coarse-graining: an interior observer tracks the decompressed projection and cannot see behind horizons, where the information needed to invert the dynamics is sequestered. From inside, the dynamics look like Rule 30: one-way, entropy-producing, illegible. At the substrate, they are a reversible computation. A reversible law does not erase the arrow; the arrow was never a property of the law. It is a property of a boundary condition seen through a coarse-graining, and the observer sits inside the coarse-graining.

The Four-Model Theory makes the matching claim about the observer (Gruber, 2026a, §3.4.4). The explicit models that constitute experience are implemented in the coarse-grained regime in which the thermodynamic arrow is defined. Records, traces and memory formation are entropy-gradient phenomena; a memory is a low-entropy configuration that correlates with a past state and not with a future one, because that is what the gradient permits. The cognitive arrow — the direction in which a mind remembers and anticipates — is therefore inherited from the thermodynamic one. There are not two arrows that happen to align. There is one arrow, and the modeler is inside the layer that has it. Neither theory assumes a direction of time. Both derive the experienced direction from where the experiencer sits.

## Felt order is constructed

The direction is inherited. The order of events within the direction is something else again, and here the Four-Model Theory makes a stronger claim: felt temporal order is constructed at the explicit layer and is not read off the order in which signals reach the substrate (Gruber, 2026a, §3.4.4).

The evidence is the postdiction literature. Two differently coloured flashes shown in sequence produce apparent motion, and observers report the colour changing abruptly midway along the path — content assigned to a moment before the second flash, which could not have been specified until the second flash had occurred (Kolers & von Grünau, 1976). Taps delivered to widely separated points on the skin are felt as a regular progression hopping along the arm, with taps at places never touched (Geldard & Sherrick, 1972). In the flash-lag effect the position attributed to a flash depends on what happens in the eighty milliseconds after it (Eagleman & Sejnowski, 2000). Dennett and Kinsbourne (1992) drew the conclusion: for such revisions there is often no fact of the matter whether the content was altered before or after it "entered consciousness", because the question presupposes a finish line in the substrate at which content becomes conscious, and there is none. On the theory's reading this is expected. Each frame of the simulation assigns an order to its contents as part of constructing them, and the assignment answers to coherence and not to arrival time.

The "now" of experience is a computational construct, one frame of a recursive simulation, and it is not a physical instant; physics has no now. The flow of time as experienced is simulation output, produced by a system that assigns order to its content after the content has arrived. "Dynamics emerge as a point of view after the fact" is, at the level of felt time, literally true. What the construction does not supply is its own direction, which is inherited as above. Order is made; direction is given; and neither is a property of the substrate's clock, because the substrate has none that the observer can read.

## Becoming is irreducibility seen from inside

If the order is constructed and the direction inherited, what is left of becoming — the sense that the future is open and the present is being produced? The cosmology paper's answer is the one I hold (Gruber, 2026b, §8.4).

The whole block — the complete history of the substrate — can satisfy a timeless condition: it is the configuration that encodes itself, a fixed point, a reading no embedded observer occupies. The same block, described from inside, is a process. The two readings do not contradict each other; they are the same condition at two levels. What makes the inside reading unavoidable for an embedded observer is irreducibility. Class 4 dynamics admit no predictive shortcut, and an observer inside such dynamics cannot obtain the next configuration except by being part of its computation. It cannot see the block, because seeing it would be a shortcut. It lives through the computation because that is the only access a subprocess has to the output of the whole. Becoming is what irreducible computation is like from a position inside it.

Barbour (1999) is the nearest neighbour to this position and differs from it at one point. Barbour removes time from fundamental physics and keeps a space of configurations, some of which — time capsules — contain records that imply a history; experienced time, for him, is the content of such records. The present account agrees that the substrate needs no clock and that the records are inside the configurations. It locates the sense of passage in the irreducibility that prevents any embedded observer from having the block as content, where Barbour locates it in the records. A record is static; a mind reading it is a computation, and a computation inside an irreducible whole proceeds step by step because no other mode is available to it. That is the difference between a timeless world with memories in it and a timeless world that is lived.

## Reversibility, probability, and the operator J

Reversibility and the conservation of probability are the two regularities of the quantum formalism most exposed to the suspicion, and the operator that encodes them together — J, with J² = −I, the real-valued name of multiplication by i — is its natural target.

Normalization does not imply reversibility. That probabilities sum to one says that something happens at every step; it is bookkeeping. A deterministic map that sends two distinct states to one output preserves total probability — all of it ends up in the output — and loses the information about which input was there. Unitarity asserts much more: that the evolution is invertible, that no two states merge, that total information is conserved. Anyone who derives reversibility from the fact that probabilities add to one has derived it from nothing.

For a finite deterministic system a weaker statement does hold, and it should be given its exact scope. If the total state is finite and the rule autonomous, every trajectory is eventually periodic — there are only finitely many states, so some state recurs — and on the cycle the update is bijective: each state on it has exactly one predecessor on it. A system without beginning has been on its cycle forever. So a finite deterministic substrate is reversible on its attractor, even if its rule, written out, is many-to-one. This is a fact about the total state. It says nothing about the variables an observer inside the system can track. The coarse-grained variables of an interior observer can be irreversible — many microstates to one macrostate, information leaving the observer's description at every step — while the total state cycles reversibly underneath. Rule 30 run on a finite ring is eventually periodic and bijective on its cycle; it still looks, from inside any row, like one-way decay of structure. Reversibility of the whole and irreversibility of the observer's level coexist, and the reply that my own framework produces reversibility is true of the first and silent about the second.

The cycle constrains the arrow. A substrate that is finite and reversible, and that passes through a compressed phase, returns to it: on a bijective cycle every state recurs, the compressed one included. The interior arrow therefore cannot run one way over the whole cycle. Between one visit to the compressed phase and the next, coarse-grained entropy rises as the interior decompresses; by the return it is low again, so somewhere on the cycle the direction reverses, or the trajectory passes through a transition at which it is reset. My cosmology paper's candidate for the turn is the CPT alternation of its cyclic renewal (Gruber, 2026b, §5.4): a saturated boundary decompresses into a successor whose signature under the combined inversion of charge, parity and time is flipped, so that each cycle has an arrow and consecutive arrows are not the same — a possibility the paper offers and does not derive. I do not claim that this settles what happens at the turn. What the recurrence does settle is where the question of Chapter 13 becomes unavoidable. A cycle that passes through every state on it again and again, the compressed phase and the disordered stretches alike, is a setting in which observers can arise from the disorder as fluctuations as well as from the decompression; which observers such a world contains, and with what weight, is then a question the model owes an answer to, and the arrow by itself does not give one.

J itself divides in two. In a real representation of quantum mechanics, J is the operator on pairs of real numbers that sends (u, v) to (−v, u); J² = −I, and J is multiplication by i under another name. As ontology — the claim that there is a plane in the state space of the world in which things fundamentally rotate — J can be rejected, and I reject it: nothing in the formalism tells whether that rotation is a feature of the world or of the representation, which is the costume problem of Chapter 2 in the clothing of time evolution. As compression, J is data. Interference, the stable phase relations between the components of a superposition, and the proportionality of energy to frequency, E = hf, are measured facts, and J is the shortest way yet found to write them. To reject J as data is to reject the measurements. To reject J as ontology is to decline an inference the measurements do not support. Those are different acts, and only the second is open.

Stone's theorem is the result most often cited as if it derived J from first principles, and it does no such thing. The theorem (Stone, 1932) says that a strongly continuous one-parameter group of unitary operators on a Hilbert space has a unique self-adjoint generator: if U(t) is such a group, then U(t) = e^{−iHt} for a self-adjoint H. Each clause of the hypothesis is an assumption. The group is parametrized by a real number t, assumed in advance to exist and to vary continuously; the operators are assumed unitary, which is reversibility assumed; the continuity is assumed. It does not produce time: t is its input. It does not produce reversibility: unitarity is the hypothesis. What it says is that if there is a continuous, reversible evolution labelled by a real parameter, then that evolution has the exponential form, with J, in the guise of −i, in the exponent. The theorem is conditional on exactly what was in question. Nobody who doubts that time is a continuous parameter is answered by a theorem that begins by assuming it.

Reversibility and time-reversal symmetry are different properties, and doubting one does not license doubting the other. A dynamics is **reversible** if its update is invertible: from the present state the past state can be recovered. It is **time-reversal symmetric** if the inverted dynamics has the same form as the forward one — if running the film backwards shows a process the same laws allow. A rule can be reversible without being time-symmetric; the weak interaction is the physical case, where the evolution is unitary and therefore reversible, while the processes it governs violate time-reversal symmetry, as the observed violation of CP symmetry together with the CPT theorem requires. Doubting time-reversal symmetry at the substrate is defensible. Doubting effective reversibility at our level fights the data: apparent, local reversibility at our level is a measured regularity, compressed data in the sense of Chapter 9, and what remains open is only what the substrate does, which the regularity does not determine. My cosmology paper commits to a reversible substrate as an architectural choice; the regularity at our level is consistent with that choice and does not force it. The CPT theorem, which is sometimes offered as a proof that the laws are reversible, is a theorem about a symmetry: it says that a Lorentz-invariant, local, unitary field theory is invariant under the combined operation of charge conjugation, parity and time reversal. Unitarity — reversibility — is among its hypotheses. It does not prove reversibility; it assumes it, and proves a symmetry.

## What time is for an embedded observer

Time, for an observer inside a computation, is four things, and none of them is a clock. It is the update, the generating relation the observer never has complete as content and has only as order. It is a direction inherited from the coarse-grained layer in which the observer's records are made. It is an order the observer constructs, frame by frame, to make its contents cohere. And it is a becoming that is irreducibility felt from inside: the step-by-step access that is the only access a part has to a whole that admits no shortcut. What the substrate does — whether its update is reversible, whether it has a clock at all, whether the block is static — is settled by none of them. A creature whose entire world is laid out, step by step, on a storage medium would have all four, and that is the case Chapter 14 takes up.

# Part IV — The Inside of a Structure

# Chapter 12 — Infinity and Quasi-Infinity

## No evidence either way, and why

There is not the smallest piece of evidence that anything physical is infinite. No measurement has ever returned an infinite quantity, and none could, because a measurement is a finite record made by a finite apparatus in a finite time. A detector registers a count, a voltage, a position to some number of digits. Whatever the world contains, what reaches an observer is a string of finite length, and a finite string is compatible with an infinite source and with a finite one alike.

Two conclusions are tempting here and both are wrong. The first is that physical theories using infinite objects are thereby refuted. Calculus takes limits over infinitely fine divisions; thermodynamics defines a sharp phase transition only in the limit of infinitely many particles; field theories assign a value to every point of a continuum. None of these is refuted by the absence of observed infinities, because none of them predicts that an infinity will be observed. The idealization is a compression: it replaces an enormous finite quantity by a limit that is easier to calculate with, and it is judged by whether the finite predictions it yields match finite records. The second tempting conclusion is the reverse, that the success of such theories confirms that the infinite objects exist. It does not. A limit that works as an approximation to a very large finite system works equally well whether or not anything in the world is actually infinite, so the success carries no information on the question. The continuum is the costume of Chapter 2: its usefulness hides which of its features belong to the world and which to the representation, and the costume fits whether or not anything is underneath.

The position that survives is narrower and harder to escape. Infinity is unobserved, and it is unobservable from any embedded perspective, for the reason already given: every observation is a finite relation between an observer and what it observes. That is a limit on evidence. It is not a proof that the world is finite. It leaves the question open in a specific way — open for good, from inside — and the useful move is to ask what kind of unboundedness an observer can actually have.

## Quasi-infinity

In the cosmology paper (Gruber, 2026b, §4.2) I defined **quasi-infinity** as the condition of a system that is effectively unbounded for any internal observer while the information accessible from any given point is finite. The definition sits between two familiar notions. Mathematical infinity means that no bound exists. Finitude means that a bound exists and can be reached and inspected. A quasi-infinite system has bounds, but none of them is an edge one can walk up to and examine. They are surfaces that limit information, and they come in three kinds.

The first kind recedes. A cosmological horizon in an accelerating universe sits at a finite distance from each observer and is defined relative to that observer's world-line; no journey reaches it, because the expansion carries it away as fast as it is approached. An object falling toward a black hole's event horizon behaves the same way from outside: it redshifts toward invisibility and never, in the exterior description, crosses.

The second kind shields. Spatial resolution improves with the energy of the probe, but only down to the Planck regime. On the standard minimal-length arguments, concentrating more energy into a smaller region beyond that point forms a horizon whose size grows with the energy invested, so every attempt to resolve finer structure regenerates the boundary at a larger scale (Gruber, 2026b, §5.3, where the heuristic status of these arguments is stated).

The third kind terminates. The Big Bang, and a Big Crunch if there is one, end world-lines at finite proper time. Nothing arrives there, because a curvature singularity is not a place in the spacetime; a world-line simply has finite length and admits no extension. No information crosses in either direction.

Each of the three is a boundary of the domain that is never an element of the domain. The observer is always somewhere ordinary — at finite distance, finite resolution, finite curvature — and the bounds are present only as the places where access stops. This is why quasi-infinity is the only infinity an embedded observer can have. From inside, a world whose bounds recede, shield or terminate looks unbounded in every direction one can try. Whether the whole is infinite, or finite with its finitude hidden behind such surfaces, makes no difference to any record the observer can make: both predict the same finite access from every point. The distinction between a truly infinite universe and a quasi-infinite one is therefore real for a description from outside and empty for every description from inside, and there is no description from outside.

The cosmology paper goes further than this and treats the horizon as an ontological boundary: on its single-surface ontology, everything physics can mean by "what lies beyond" is exhausted by what is encoded on the boundary itself (Gruber, 2026b, §5.2). That is a commitment of that model and can be dropped without losing the epistemic point. The epistemic point needs only the three access limits, and they are standard physics, with the caveats the cosmology paper already attaches to the Planck floor.

Quasi-infinity also restates the capacity limit of Chapter 5 in geometrical form. A proper part of an incompressible whole cannot hold a complete description of it (Kolmogorov, 1965; Chaitin, 1966). The horizon is where that limit becomes a place on the sky: the region from which an observer can ever receive information carries a finite amount of it, and the rest of the world, however large, contributes to that observer's description only through what crosses the surface.

## The Möbius grid

My own working picture of the substrate goes beyond the cosmology paper in one direction. A cellular automaton is usually pictured as rules applied to cells on a grid with edges, a first step and a last. That picture is a simplification made for minds that like beginnings. The structure I have in mind is a Möbius-like, quasi-infinite grid, closed on itself so that there is no edge, with no first or last state, and with rules that co-evolve with the states they govern, much as the connectivity of a brain is shaped by the activity it carries. On this picture, asking how the universe began is like asking where a Möbius strip starts.

The picture has three consequences.

The first concerns infinity itself. With no evidence for physical infinity, a grid that is actually infinite cannot be called quasi-infinite. The coherent version is finite but unbounded: a closed surface, like a torus or a Möbius strip, which has no edge and finitely many cells. A finite deterministic system ends on a cycle, and on the cycle the update is a bijection of the total state, whatever the rule does elsewhere (Chapter 11).

A system on a cycle has no beginning and no end, which is what the Möbius picture asked for; the cycle supplies the absence of a first state without any appeal to infinity. But the bijection holds for the total state only. It says nothing about the coarse variables an embedded observer tracks, which can lose information while the whole loses none, and it says nothing about whether any observer could ever detect the recurrence. A cycle through a state space of astronomical size has a period no record inside it can span. From inside, a world on such a cycle is quasi-infinite in time in the same sense it is quasi-infinite in space.

The cycle also constrains the arrow of time: the compressed phase recurs in every period, so the interior arrow cannot run one way all the way round, and the recurrence is the setting in which the Boltzmann argument of Chapter 13 applies (Chapter 11).

The second consequence concerns the co-evolving rules. If the rules change, something determines how they change. Call that a meta-rule. If the meta-rule changes too, something determines that, and so on. Either the regress stops at some level where a rule holds fixed, or nothing determines how the rules evolve, in which case their evolution is lawless — ontic randomness, the Class 5 that the cosmology paper's elimination argument excludes under the assumption of a deterministic substrate (Gruber, 2026b, §3.2). The brain analogy that motivated co-evolution makes the same point. Synaptic weights change with experience, but the plasticity rules that change them are fixed by biochemistry that the experience does not rewrite. This is the base case of Chapter 4 reappearing at cosmic scale: a system whose entire content is assigned at run time has no ground, and a universe whose rules are entirely rewritten by its own states has no rule. Co-evolution is legitimate, and it does not escape the regress; it moves the fixed logic one level up.

There is a further cost. A rule that co-evolves with the state is a rule written into the state, and the cosmology paper already prices that move: writing the rule into the state consumes capacity that would otherwise encode content (Gruber, 2026b, §6.3). A system with a rule, a meta-rule and a fixed ground is, taken as a whole, one larger fixed rule acting on a larger state. Co-evolution changes how the rule is distributed and what it costs to store. It does not remove the need for something that holds still.

The third consequence concerns chance. A tempting claim is that any sufficiently large grid-rule system, started from random initial conditions, will produce a world like ours by chance. Two facts stand in the way. The first is that most rules produce nothing of interest. Among simple cellular automata, the large majority settle into uniform or periodic behaviour, Wolfram's Classes 1 and 2, and the rules that sustain open-ended complex structure are a small minority (Wolfram, 2002). Universality does not change this. That Rule 110 can emulate any computation (Cook, 2004) shows that a world like ours can be produced by a very simple rule; it says nothing about how often a rule picked at random does so. Existence is cheap. The frequency is the content of the claim, and the frequency is unknown.

The second fact is about counting. "By chance" means "with high probability", and a probability needs a measure over some set of possibilities. Finite rule descriptions are countably many. Initial configurations on an infinite grid are uncountably many. On a finite grid they are finitely many but astronomically numerous, and the fraction that yields observers depends entirely on which configurations are counted and how they are weighted. There is no natural uniform measure that settles this, and the obvious candidates disagree. The claim that our world arises by chance therefore has no truth value until someone says over what the chance is taken. That debt is the observer-measure problem (Chapter 13). Its canonical form is the observation that random initial conditions favour small fluctuations producing a lone observer over a whole ordered universe.

The Möbius picture, so stated, is a finite, edgeless substrate on a cycle, with no first state, whose rule is fixed at some level and may be partly written into its own state at a measurable cost, and which owes an account of its arrow at the return. What it does not deliver is a world produced by chance from arbitrary rules, or an escape from a fixed ground.

## Many-worlds

I regard the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics as the clearest case in physics of infinity taken literally. On the usual popular account, every quantum event splits the world, and the branches multiply without end — infinity times infinity, for an object of which there is not the smallest evidence. A second charge is often added: that it is unscientific, since no observer can ever visit another branch.

The defenders of many-worlds give two replies that have to be answered on their merits. The first is that the interpretation adds nothing. It is standard quantum mechanics with the collapse postulate removed: the wavefunction evolves deterministically and smoothly at all times, and the appearance of a single outcome is explained by decoherence and by the observer becoming correlated with what it measures. On this view collapse is the object bolted on to rescue the formalism — the patch error of Chapter 1 — and many-worlds is what remains when the patch is taken away. The second reply is that branch counting is ill-defined. Decoherence produces branches only approximately and relative to a choice of coarse-graining, so there is no fact about how many branches there are, and "infinity times infinity" counts something the theory itself does not count.

Both replies have force against the infinity objection in that form. They do not rescue the interpretation, because its real difficulties lie elsewhere. One is the Born rule. If every outcome of a measurement occurs, it is unclear what it means to say that one outcome occurs with probability 0.9 and another with probability 0.1, and the attempts to derive the familiar probabilities from branch structure alone remain disputed. The other is the preferred-basis problem: the wavefunction can be decomposed in many ways, and something has to say which decomposition corresponds to the worlds. Both are problems of weighting and selection, and both reappear, in a different setting, as the observer-measure problem.

The "unscientific" charge needs the same treatment. If an interpretation predicts no deviation from the predictions of quantum mechanics, no experiment can decide for or against it. That holds for many-worlds, for the collapse interpretations that add no new dynamics, and for 't Hooft's (2016) cellular automaton interpretation, which I find far more congenial. The charge either applies to all of them or to none. What separates them is what each would predict if pushed beyond the current formalism, and for the cellular automaton view that is where its testable commitments would have to come from: deformed Lorentz symmetry at small scales, or in-principle signalling if hidden influences propagate at finite speed (Chapter 10). An interpretation earns empirical standing by naming such a deviation, and none of the three has yet been confirmed by one.

The infinity objection survives in a sharper form, aimed where Chapter 2 aimed the critique of number: at the reals. The state space of quantum mechanics is a vector space over the complex numbers, and the complex numbers are pairs of reals. A wavefunction taken as the furniture of the world, which is what many-worlds asks for, carries the whole continuum with it: amplitudes that vary continuously and can take uncomputable values. That is an import of infinity into the ontology, and it is the same import whichever interpretation reifies the wavefunction. The objection is therefore an objection to reifying the continuum, and it applies to every reading that reifies the wavefunction, many-worlds included. It is not an argument that the number of branches is finite, and it would be a mistake to claim that a finite-dimensional state space makes the branches finite: without a definition of what a branch is, there is no number to be finite.

## Nothing

Infinity has a twin at the other end of the scale. If an embedded observer can never encounter the infinite, can it encounter nothing? The question is old, and the answer I gave in the cosmology paper is a disjunction with the same structure as the argument about infinity (Gruber, 2026b, §3.1).

A claim that nothing exists, or existed, or could exist, takes one of two forms. Either the nothing is located — before the Big Bang, after a vacuum decay, in some possible world — or it is not. A located nothing is placed relative to what exists, and placing anything relative to anything implies a separation and a dimension along which it holds. Whatever has a position in a structure has a property, so the located nothing is a something with an unusual content. This is why cosmology says there was no time before the Big Bang, not that there was nothing before it: a "before" is already a frame for the nothing to sit in. A decay to true vacuum ends in an energy level, which presupposes an ordered range of levels. A possible world with nothing in it is an empty cell in a space of worlds, and the space is something. In each case what has been described is a universe with nothing in it, which may well be possible, and not nothingness.

An unlocated nothing escapes all of this, and it is the only form that stays true to the word. But a nothing that is nowhere and nowhen in particular is the claim that there is nothing at all, anywhere, at any time, and that claim is refuted by anything whatever, including the existence of whoever is in a position to make it. The observer is the counterexample. There is no third form, because the nothing either has a where and a when or it does not.

The same conclusion follows from the side of evidence, and here the parallel with infinity is exact. To observe a state of affairs is to stand in some relation to it, and whatever stands in a relation to an observer is not nothing. The observer's presence is already a counterexample to what was to be observed. Nothingness is therefore unobservable in principle, as infinity is, and for a closely related reason: observation is a finite relation between something and something else. An infinity cannot appear in a finite record, and a nothing cannot be one term of a relation.

Both limits share the structure of the three bounding surfaces. The boundary of a domain is never an element of the domain: a horizon recedes, the Planck floor regenerates itself, a terminus has no "at", and nothingness has no place for an observer to stand (Gruber, 2026b, §5.3, §9.5). From inside, the world presents as unbounded and as existing, and neither presentation can be checked against its opposite, because the opposite would have to appear inside the domain to be checked and would then no longer be what it was supposed to be. I accept as a starting point that something exists, because its denial cannot be stated without refuting itself, and I accept that what exists is unbounded for every observer in it, because no observer can find the bound. Whether it is infinite is a question I can formulate and no observer can answer.

# Chapter 13 — The Evidential Poverty of the First Person

## The position at full strength

I think the Boltzmann brain is one of the most consistent ideas in physics. In its canonical form it runs as follows. A universe that persists long enough in thermal equilibrium will, by rare fluctuation, assemble any finite arrangement of matter, including a brain with a full set of memories, a sense of a body and a world, and a single moment of experience before it dissolves again. Fluctuations of that size are vastly more probable than a fluctuation large enough to produce an entire ordered universe with a low-entropy past. If the universe lasts long enough, such momentary observers will outnumber observers with real histories, and a typical observer with experiences like mine would be one of them.

My own version is broader. Nothing in my point of view allows a proof against solipsism. I have no outside view of anyone else's qualia, and no outside view of my own origin. My local, temporally finite existence may be one temporary thought of a larger mechanism, and every other conscious observer may be instantiated in the same way, as a thought of that mechanism. If anything, my own position is evidence that something like this is possible: I find myself as exactly the kind of bounded, momentary, self-narrating thing such a mechanism would produce.

The replies to this position are less decisive than they are usually taken to be, and the part of it that survives says something about every theory of observers, including mine.

## What the first person establishes

First-person experience establishes one thing beyond doubt: that at least one experience occurs. That is the Cartesian minimum, and nothing in the present argument disturbs it.

It establishes nothing about where the experience comes from. Take four hypotheses about my origin. On the first, I am an embodied organism with a real history, as I appear to be. On the second, I am a simulation running on some host. On the third, I am a thermal fluctuation with false memories. On the fourth, I am a timeless instantiation — a pattern in a static structure, or a thought of a larger mechanism, with no execution in any external time. Each hypothesis can be stipulated to include exactly the experience I am having now, with exactly its content, down to this room and these memories. An observation that every hypothesis predicts equally well cannot shift the balance among them. The experience is consistent with all four, and consistency with all four is evidence for none over the others.

My position is therefore evidence for a larger mechanism only in the weak sense of being consistent with what I find. The same experience is equally consistent with an ordinary embodied history, so it supplies no reason to prefer the mechanism. The inability to disprove solipsism is a limit on discrimination. It is not a reason to believe any particular account of what generates the solipsist's experience, and it tells against the Boltzmann hypothesis as much as for it, since the Boltzmann hypothesis is one more account that the first person cannot certify.

The canonical Boltzmann brain and my broader proposal should also be kept apart. The canonical version is a thermal model: it names a generating process — fluctuation in an equilibrium bath — and its force comes from a comparison of fluctuation probabilities. "A temporary thought of a larger mechanism" names no such process. It is a hypothesis about nested or timeless instantiation, in which observers are products of a generator whose nature is left open. That makes it harder to refute and harder to support, for the same reason: without a specified generator it yields no probabilities, and without probabilities it cannot be compared with its rivals on any evidence at all.

## Two replies and their limits

Two replies to the Boltzmann brain are standard, and neither settles it.

The first is Carroll's (2017) argument from cognitive instability. Suppose a cosmological theory predicts that most observers with experiences like mine are Boltzmann brains. Then, if I accept the theory, I should conclude that I am probably one, and that my memories, including my memories of the observations and calculations that supported the theory, are probably false. The theory undermines the evidence for itself. It is not contradicted by any observation; it simply cannot be rationally held, because holding it removes the grounds for holding it. On this view, a cosmology that predicts a preponderance of Boltzmann brains should be rejected for that reason.

The argument identifies something real. A hypothesis about my origin is also a hypothesis about the reliability of the faculties I use to assess it, and a hypothesis that makes those faculties unreliable is in an odd position. But it does not show the hypothesis false. It shows only that I cannot reach it as a conclusion from evidence I trust. That is a statement about my epistemic situation, and I may be in exactly that situation whether I like it or not. Elga (2025) argues that cognitive-instability considerations of this kind do not satisfactorily settle the problem, and the dispute remains open.

The second reply is the argument from excess order. Our universe is far more ordered than any single observer needs. A lone fluctuating brain requires a few kilograms of organized matter for a moment; we observe billions of galaxies, a cosmic microwave background smooth to high precision, and a coherent history running back billions of years. If we were the products of the cheapest fluctuation compatible with our experience, we should expect to see much less. The order we observe is therefore evidence against pure chance as our origin.

This is the standard argument, and it is a good one as far as it goes. It is itself an observer-measure argument. It assumes that I should reason as if I were a typical member of some class of observers who share my evidence, and it assumes a measure over the ways that class can be realized, so that a small fluctuation counts as more probable than a large one. Both assumptions are reasonable in a thermal model, where the measure comes with the physics. Neither is available for my broader proposal, which specifies no measure. The argument from order therefore defeats the canonical Boltzmann brain on the canonical model's own terms, and it does nothing against a hypothesis that declines to say how observers are weighted. The broader hypothesis escapes the argument only by giving it nothing to work on, which leaves it untested.

## Why the first person is poor

Every theory faces underdetermination of some kind. The poverty of first-person evidence about its own origin is a stronger thing than ordinary underdetermination, and the Four-Model Theory explains why.

On the theory, the world one experiences and the self one experiences are explicit models — two kinds of models, of the world and of the self, with the self-model embedded in the world-model — and the process generating them cannot, taken as a whole, be content of the simulation it is generating: the simulation cannot contain its own current generation represented as the cause of what it is generating (Gruber, 2026a, §3.8, §4.2.2; Chapter 7). Parts of the apparatus can leak into the scene, as anomalous content or as one's own brain seen in a scanner (Chapter 8), and a leak is not the generator.

The same constraint governs every hypothesis about one's origin. An embodied history, a host computer, a thermal fluctuation and a timeless structure are four hypotheses about the apparatus. The evidence available to the first person is content of the simulation: percepts, memories, a felt present. That content is what all four hypotheses agree on, by stipulation. What they disagree about is the generator as a whole, which is what no leak delivers. The evidential poverty is the unrepresentable apparatus once more, at the largest scale it can take.

The theory adds a sharper point about memory. On the theory, the explicit self-model does not maintain continuity by persisting; it is regenerated after every interruption — waking, anaesthesia — and each time it reconstructs a continuous self-narrative from whatever the implicit self-model currently contains (Gruber, 2026a, §4.2.2). Continuity is confabulated from present material, every morning. A momentary instantiation equipped with the right implicit content would therefore present to itself exactly as I present to myself on waking: as a self with a past, continuous with yesterday. The Boltzmann brain with false memories is the limiting case of an operation the theory says ordinary brains perform daily. This does not make the Boltzmann hypothesis more probable. It explains why its falsity cannot be felt: a felt past is a present construction in every case, and its being present tells me nothing about whether the past it describes occurred.

The diagnosis also accounts for something the standard debate leaves unexplained, which is why the first-person intuition against the Boltzmann brain is so strong while the arguments against it are so weak. The world presents as simply there, with a history given in memory, for the same reason a choice presents as originating in the self: the cause of the presentation, taken as a whole, is absent from every position the simulation can survey, so the presentation is taken at face value. The conviction that I am obviously not a fluctuation is the conviction an observer must have under every one of the four hypotheses. Its strength is a fact about the architecture of self-models and carries no information about which hypothesis is true.

The poverty is one-sided, and the side it spares matters. The first person cannot confirm any hypothesis about its generator, but it can refute some. A hypothesis that predicts content contradicting the content I have — a world dissolving into noise as I look at it, memories that fail to cohere from one moment to the next — is inconsistent with my admitted input, and that inconsistency is decidable from where I stand, with no access to the apparatus required. This is the asymmetry of Chapter 6 at the scale of a single observer: falsity is a relation between a hypothesis and the input it must accommodate, and it needs no standpoint outside the simulation. The four hypotheses survive this test only because each was stipulated to reproduce my content exactly. Any version of them specific enough to predict something about content I have not yet examined — what the next observation will show, how much order lies beyond what I have already seen — becomes exposed to refutation by it. The first person is poor in confirmations and adequately supplied with refutations, and the refutations are what an observer measure would have to make use of.

## The observer measure

What follows from this is a debt that every theory of observers owes, mine included. Call it the **observer measure**. A theory that wants to say anything about what observers should expect to find must specify three things: what implements an observer, so that one can tell whether a given structure contains one; which histories occur, so that one knows what there is to count; and how occurrences are weighted, so that counting yields probabilities. "Everything occurs somewhere" is not yet a probability model. It lists possibilities without saying how much of each there is, and without that no observation can confirm or disconfirm it, because every observation occurs somewhere.

The debt has already appeared twice under other names. In Chapter 12, the claim that any large enough grid produces our world by chance failed for lack of a measure over rules and initial configurations. In many-worlds, the Born rule problem is the problem of weighting branches, and the preferred-basis problem is the problem of saying what the branches are. The canonical Boltzmann argument has a measure, supplied by thermal physics, and that is exactly why it can be argued against; the broader proposal has none, and that is exactly why it cannot yet be argued for.

My own proposal contains the beginning of a measure without stating it as one. It says that every conscious observer may be instantiated in the same way, as a thought of the larger mechanism. That is a uniformity claim, and uniformity is a weighting: it says that no observer is privileged over another in how it comes to occur. Taken seriously, it has consequences. If every instantiated observer counts equally, and the mechanism produces brief, isolated thoughts more often than long, coherent ones because they cost it less, then most observers should find themselves in brief, isolated episodes, and the coherent extended history I appear to inhabit would be anomalous. If the mechanism instead produces observers only as parts of coherent worlds, the proposal predicts what I find, and it has become a claim about the generator's structure that can be stated and checked. Either way, the uniformity clause turns the proposal from a possibility into something that makes a prediction once the generator's costs are specified.

The cosmology paper owes the debt as well, and does not pay it. A finite-but-unbounded substrate on a cycle, which returns to its compressed phase in every period (Chapter 12), or a cyclic cosmology in which each cycle decompresses the boundary of the last (Gruber, 2026b, §5.4), raises at once the question of how many observers with my evidence occur per cycle, in which phases, and with what weight. The paper does not answer it. A version of the model that did would have to say what in the substrate counts as an observer — the Four-Model Theory offers a candidate architecture, self-referential closure between differentiated world- and self-models, which it does not claim to have confirmed — and how instances of that architecture are counted across a block that can be read as timeless (Gruber, 2026b, §8.4). Until then, the model is in the same position as my broader Boltzmann proposal: consistent with what I find, and unable to say how probable what I find should be.

The measure cannot be read off from the first person, for the reason already given. It has to be supplied by a theory of the generator and checked by what that theory predicts about the content observers find. That is the only route by which hypotheses about the apparatus make contact with evidence: not through the presence of experience, which every hypothesis grants, but through the distribution of what experience contains, which different measures predict differently. A theory that predicts we should see far less order than we do has been tested by the order we see. A theory that predicts nothing about the distribution has not been tested by anything.

## Other minds

The same poverty governs other people. I have no outside view of anyone else's qualia. Every judgement I make that another being experiences anything rests on what it reports and how it behaves, and reports and behaviour are content in my simulation, generated by an apparatus — theirs — of which my simulation holds at most a model, as it holds of mine.

The Four-Model Theory states the consequence for artificial systems in a form that applies to every case (Gruber, 2026a, §3.4.3). The doubt that a system might report experience while feeling nothing is the general problem of other minds. It applies with identical force to every human but oneself. To credit a human's report while denying that of an architecturally matched artifact demands an independent reason — that biological substrate does phenomenal work no functional duplicate could do — and that reason has to be argued, since it does not follow from anything the first person can observe. The theory does not claim that any particular artificial system is conscious. It claims that the evidential situation is the same for every mind one is not.

Solipsism is the limiting case of this symmetry: if no report certifies an inside, then no inside but one's own is certified. One distinction keeps the position from being as bleak as that sounds. Reports carry relational structure — which experiences resemble which, which follow which, what can be told apart — and that structure can be shared and checked between observers, as the similarity structure of a colour space can be compared across brains (Gruber, 2026a, §3.4.2). What a report cannot carry is the inside of the perspective that issues it. Two observers can confirm to each other everything about the structure of their experience and nothing about whether the other has one. That limit holds for creatures in any world they share, whatever generates them, including a world laid out statically on a disk (Chapter 14).

What first-person experience leaves me with, then, is one certainty and one obligation. The certainty is that something is experienced. The obligation falls on anyone, including me, who proposes a mechanism that generates observers: to say what an observer is in that mechanism, how many occur, and with what weight, so that the order I actually see can count for or against it.

# Chapter 14 — The Worm on the Disk

## The thought experiment

Take the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans*: about a millimetre long, a few hundred neurons, the best-mapped nervous system there is. Suppose its whole life were reproduced in silico, at whatever resolution the reproduction needs: body, nervous system, the agar it crawls on, the bacteria it eats. Run the simulation once, or a thousand times. Run it forward, or from its last state back to its first. Or do not run it at all: compute every time step once and lay the steps out side by side on a storage medium, one long static file.

My claim is conditional. If the simulated worm has consciousness at all, then interrogating it at any point yields what interrogating me yields: an experience organized in space and time, with a before and an after, had from the inside. The experience does not consist in dynamics in the host. Rule-governed dependency, the dependency of each state on its predecessors under a rule, might be all that is needed.

Nothing here claims that a simulated worm is conscious, or that a worm is. The Four-Model Theory grades consciousness, and where a nematode falls on that scale, if anywhere, is not decided here. The worm is chosen because its life is small enough to imagine copying in full. Any observer would serve.

## Five operations that look alike

"Run it backwards" and "lay it out on a disk" sound like variations on one theme. They are five different operations.

The first is a live implementation. A machine computes state *t*+1 from state *t* by the rule, and the simulated worm's internal records, its traces of where it has been and what it has met, take part in computing what comes next. This is the case nobody disputes, granted the condition.

The second is a change of schedule: faster, slower, paused for a year between two steps, split across machines. If every dependency the rule specifies is preserved, so that each state is computed from exactly the states it depends on, nothing inside any state can register the schedule. A pause between step 4,000 and step 4,001 leaves no trace in step 4,001, because step 4,001 is a function of step 4,000 and of nothing on the host's clock. This is an invariance claim about content. It proves nothing about experience, but on any account that grants the live case it removes host timing from the list of things the experience could depend on.

The third is reverse enumeration: read the stored states from last to first. The reading order is ours. Each state carries its content wherever in our traversal it appears. A state in which the worm's nervous system holds a trace of having touched the plate edge, and now registers food, holds that order inside itself. "I remember A and now see B" is a fact about one state and the states it depends on, and it does not reverse when we read the file from the end, any more than a novel's plot reverses when someone reads it from the last page.

The fourth is an actually inverted computation, in which the host computes each state's predecessor from its successor. For a reversible rule this can be done, and it generates the same states with the same dependencies in the opposite host order, which returns us to the third case. For an irreversible rule, and a dissipative simulation of a living animal will have one, the successor does not fix the predecessor: many states lead to the same next state. Bennett (1973) showed that running a computation backwards is itself a computation, uncomputation, whose steps are coordinated by the complete record of the forward run. SB-HC4A makes the same observation about watching an irreducible automaton's history backwards: played in reverse it looks globally choreographed, because what singles out each predecessor is the whole realized history (Gruber, 2026b, §8.4). So the inverted case either reduces to enumeration or presupposes the record.

The fifth is a static encoding. Every state is on the disk, and nothing is computed while the file sits there. This is the case that matters. It raises a question the first four do not raise: what relation between bits on a medium and the structure they encode makes the structure present at all?

"Interrogating" needs the same care. Reading a stored answer means retrieving the state at step 4,001 and decoding what the worm's nervous system holds there. Executing a continuation means loading a stored state and computing step 4,002 onward, which creates states that were never on the disk. Perturbing an implementation means touching the worm with a probe it did not meet in the recorded run, and only a running or continued implementation can be perturbed. Of the three, only the first applies to a static file without adding anything to it, and my claim concerns the first: at any stored state, what the state holds, as a member of its trajectory, is a moment of an experience organized in time, if the worm has experience.

## The implementation problem

The static case runs straight into an old difficulty. Putnam (1988) argued that under a suitably gerrymandered mapping from physical states to computational states, any ordinary open physical system implements any finite-state automaton. The same point is often made with a wall whose molecular motions, under some reading, implement a word processor. If a mapping is all implementation requires, implementation is trivial, and so is every computational account of mind: every rock runs every program, and every mind is everywhere.

Chalmers (1996b) answered by tightening the requirement. A physical system implements a computation when its causal structure mirrors the computation's formal structure, counterfactual transitions included: had the system been in a different state, it would have moved to the state the automaton prescribes from there. A rock's sequence of states can be mapped onto one run of an automaton, but the rock supports none of the counterfactuals; from states it never visited it would not have gone where the automaton goes. The criterion blocks triviality, and it places the mind in causal organization.

Applied to the disk, Chalmers's criterion seems to decide against my claim. The file supports no counterfactuals. It is one recorded run, and nothing in it would have gone anywhere. Egan's *Permutation City* (1994) took the opposite view as its premise. Its dust theory holds that a mind needs no causal connection between its states in the host: any scattering of states that can be assembled into a coherent history is that history, experienced from within, and a simulated man whose states are computed out of order notices nothing. Tegmark (2008) goes further: every mathematical structure exists, and self-aware substructures within one experience their world whether or not anything computes it. Chalmers stands at one end of the dispute and Egan and Tegmark at the other. The static disk sits between them.

## The position: instantiation is not needed

My position is close to the far end, without the claim that every structure, or every scattering of states, has an inside. Instantiation in a host is not needed.

The Four-Model Theory is a theory on our plane of existence, not a fundamental one. It describes the organization a system must have, within a world, to have an inside: implicit and explicit models of world and self, the self-model contained in the world-model, the two closed on each other's update (Gruber, 2026a, §§3.2–3.4). It says nothing about how that world must exist relative to another world that stores or computes it. When the theory says experience lives in the running process, "running" is our word for what that organization does within the world we share with brains.

Now look at the disk from both sides. From our point of view it is not conscious. It is a sequence of bits, and nothing about it is experienced by anyone on our plane. Suppose the file encodes more than one creature, a small world in which several of them meet whatever an inside requires. On their plane, any interaction between two of them ends with both confirming to each other that they are conscious and that they experience time. Their memories, their exchanges and the succession of events they live through are ordered internally, by the dependency of each state on the states before it. That order is the same whether anyone reads the disk, whether it is read forwards or backwards, once or never.

Calling the file "merely a description" assumes the ontological distinction under dispute. "Description" names a relation between a structure and a reader for whom the structure is about something else. The creatures are not reading the file. For them nothing on it is about anything; it is what there is.

If the relational structure is what suffices, the disk is unnecessary too. The disk encodes the world for us; it is how we hold it. It does not bring the creatures' consciousness into existence, as a second copy of the file does not add a second world. On this view consciousness could belong to a structured world intrinsically, and execution would be one way such a world appears from a level where someone holds the encoding and steps through it.

The creatures' mutual confirmation does not prove that either of them has experience. That is no special weakness of their situation. It is the problem of other minds, which every observer already has: I credit your report and you credit mine on the grounds the creatures have for crediting each other's. FMT states the burden: crediting one report while denying an architecturally matched other needs an independent reason that applies to one and not the other (Gruber, 2026a, §3.4.3). Host execution is the candidate reason my position rejects, and it is the only one the disk case offers.

## The fourth limit is perspectival

The fourth of the limits on any embedded description (Chapter 5) was constitutive: a description of a process, however complete, does not give its reader an instance of it. My two papers state the limit without the reader in it. FMT holds description and instance apart even at the limit of completeness: "even a complete, legible description in hand would still be a description and not an instance"; the code could be used to instantiate the experience on another substrate, but reading it is not having the experience (Gruber, 2026a, §3.4.5). SB-HC4A gives the same limit as one of the reasons why "the only complete description of the universe is the universe" (Gruber, 2026b, §6.4). The worm on the disk shows where the limit comes from, and the revision FMT's sentence needs follows.

*Description* and *instance* are relations. A rule generates a sequence of states. An observer who holds the rule and the states as content — who can store them, read them in any order, copy and edit them — is a reader, and for a reader the structure is a description. An observer whose frame the structure is, whose memories and perceptions are among the states, stands inside, and for that observer the structure is the world. One structure is a description from one side and an instance from the other. Nothing in the bits decides which; the relation in which an observer stands to them does.

FMT's sentence "reading the code is not having the experience" (§3.4.5) is then true, and it is true of the reader. Reading is what a reader does to content. The sentence says nothing about the creature for whom the code is not read but lived. The constitutive limit is real: no reader becomes the inside by reading, however complete the code. It is also perspectival: no perspective can have another perspective's inside as content. This is not derived from the principle of the absent generator. It is that principle's mirror image, and both rest on one fact: for any one perspective a structure is either its frame or its content, never both at once. A perspective's own frame is therefore not among its contents, which is the absent generator; and another perspective's frame, held as content, is not thereby had as frame, which is the constitutive limit.

This is a cut in the sense of Chapter 8: a separation an embedded mind introduces so that it can hold a domain at all, with a residue where the separated parts meet, contradicted somewhere by the theory that uses it. Description versus instance is drawn from the reader's side, and the place where it fails can be named: the limit of completeness. FMT keeps the two apart there, as a statement about descriptions as such. SB-HC4A's sentence admits a reading relative to the reader that the paper does not draw: nothing a proper part holds is the complete description, and the one object complete in that sense is the universe itself, which is had only from inside. Read so, the sentence says that at completeness description and instance are one object, against the cut the same paper uses everywhere else. On that reformulation the cut holds wherever descriptions are partial, which for embedded observers is everywhere they actually work, and it fails at the one point embedded observers never reach. Using it is correct. Treating "description" as a property bits have in themselves is the reification error of Chapter 1, committed against the cut instead of against a mathematical object.

## Which structures have an inside

The position carries a burden, and the burden is an open problem: if relational structure suffices, which structures have an inside?

The triviality objection arrives at once. If any structure, under some reading, has an inside, then any bit string under a gerrymandered reading contains every mind. Take a file of random bits as long as the worm file. Some mapping sends its blocks, one by one, to the worm's states. Putnam's argument returns in static form, and with it the dust theory's worst consequence: if readings are free, every mind is in every string, and saying that the worm file has an inside says nothing about the worm file.

I propose a criterion. It has the standing of a proposal, untested. An inside belongs to a region of a structure only if that region carries its own organization: only if the shortest description that exhibits the region's internal relations is short relative to the region. For an observer the region is its world, as far as its own states depend on it.

The criterion ranks structured regions, not media; for the worm the region is the whole trajectory. For the worm trajectory the condition holds. The trajectory is long, but its shortest description is short: the rule plus the first state. Each state follows from its predecessor under the rule, and the rule is a small fraction of the whole. The same accounting settles when a medium carries such a structure: when some decoding recovers the structure from the medium with a description shorter than the structure's own shortest description. The worm file passes: reading its states in order is shorter than the rule and the first state, and the organization that makes state 4,001 the successor of state 4,000 is in the bits. A file of random bits fails: a mapping that sends its blocks to worm states has to list, block by block, which state each block stands for, so it is as long as the worm trajectory, and a decoding that ignores the file and runs the rule is no shorter than the rule. Under an arbitrary reading the mapping is as long as the content, and the mind is in the map, not in the bits. The random file contributes nothing the mapping did not supply, and whatever structure there is belongs to whatever holds the mapping.

One consequence follows at once. Stated over structured regions, the criterion is met by the rule and the first state alone, unexecuted and unrecorded: they fix a trajectory whose organization is in it. If that trajectory also contains what FMT requires, the position says it has an inside whether or not anything computes or stores it, and the disk is one carrier among many, none of them needed. That is close to Tegmark (2008). The distance from him does not lie in compressibility, which many structures without insides share, but in the second condition below.

The measure is description length (Kolmogorov, 1965; Chaitin, 1966). The criterion is kin to SB-HC4A's observation that writing the rule into the state costs capacity (Gruber, 2026b, §6.3). There, a law carried inside the state is no free redescription of a law imposed from outside: it occupies part of a bounded budget. Here the accounting runs the other way. An organization supplied to a structure by a reader is no property of the structure, and its cost appears as the length of the reading.

The criterion is also kin to Chalmers's counterfactual condition. The shortest description of the worm trajectory is the rule and a first state, and the rule specifies what follows from states the recorded run never visited. A trajectory whose best compression is a rule therefore carries its counterfactual structure implicitly, in the very fact that makes it compressible. The criterion needs no host-level dynamics: nothing has to run for a structure to be compressible.

The criterion rules out gerrymandered insides, since a structure whose apparent organization lives in the reading has no inside of its own. It does not show that compressibility suffices. A string of identical bits is maximally compressible and has no inside on any account, and neither has a crystal. Compressibility is a condition on where an inside could be. FMT's architecture is the candidate second condition, for what else is required: among the internal relations a short reading exhibits, a self-model and a world-model closed on each other (Gruber, 2026a, §3.4.3). Whether the two conditions together suffice, I do not know. Nor do I know whether "short relative to the structure" can be made exact without an arbitrary threshold. Description length depends on the language of description up to an additive constant; for a world-sized structure the constant is negligible against the file, and for a small one it may not be.

## What changes in FMT

FMT v19 says the properties that make up phenomenality are "constitutively processual — they exist in the doing, not in the blueprint", and that they "exist when and only when the computation is actually running at self-referential closure" (Gruber, 2026a, §3.4.5), and it holds that a complete description would still be a description and not an instance. The position taken here revises that. On it, "process" and "running" mean the internal dependency structure of the world in which the doing is done: each state fixed by its predecessors under the rule, the self-model's states depending on the world-model's and the world-model's on the self-model's. They do not mean execution on a host. The blueprint in FMT's sentence is a description in the reader's sense, held as content by someone outside the structure. The doing is the dependency structure as it holds for those inside it. FMT's denial that a complete description is an instance survives as a statement about readers and is dropped as a statement about structures.

That reading is compatible with the block of SB-HC4A. There the closure condition Φ(U) = U is read two ways: as a timeless fixed-point condition on the whole four-dimensional block, and as a process when the block is described from within. Irreducibility explains why the timeless condition is lived as becoming: an embedded observer has no shortcut and can apprehend the block only by living through the computation (Gruber, 2026b, §8.4). The worm file is a small block. Its creatures, if it has any, live through it in exactly this sense, and nobody on our plane needs to step through the file for them to do so.

The phenomenality bridge is unchanged. FMT's single bridging commitment is that closure is phenomenality, with the asymmetry between inside and outside constitutive and not merely epistemic (§3.4.3). Nothing in the worm argument touches it. What changes is what "running" means: a property of a structure's internal dependencies, where it had read as a property of a host's activity.

On our plane the difference between a running brain and a stored record stays real for every practical purpose, and the empirical programme FMT proposes is untouched by it. A scan of a brain at one instant is one state. It is no trajectory; its relations to the states it would lead to are not in it, and nothing on our plane computes them until something runs. The worm file is a different object: a whole trajectory, its dependencies recoverable by a short reading. Which objects of that second kind, besides the ones we already are, have an inside is the question still open.

## The order that needs explaining

The position incurs Chapter 13's debt in a larger form. Nothing singled out the worm trajectory except two properties: its organization is in it, and by hypothesis it contains the closure FMT requires. If a static structure with those properties has an inside, every structure with them has one, whether or not anything computes, stores or reads it. The claim is narrower than Tegmark's (2008), since it does not grant an inside to every mathematical structure, and it inherits his difficulty. Structures with insides are then whatever meets two conditions, and there are a great many ways to meet them.

Most of those ways do not look like our world. A structure needs order only where its observers need it: enough regularity, over enough of the structure, for a self-model and a world-model to close on each other for a while. Around that region it can be anything. The description-length criterion admits them, because it is met by the island itself: an island of order is as compressible as its observers require, and what surrounds it enters no relation the island's states depend on. Structures that are lawful throughout, from a compressed first state across billions of years of coherent history, are a small family among them. Among structures up to any given size, those in which an observer's order is a local island in noise far outnumber those in which it is the visible part of a lawful whole, and the margin grows with every bit of surrounding noise. Counted so, the order we find is not what an inhabitant should expect. This is Boltzmann's problem moved from fluctuations in a thermal bath to the space of structures, and the move removes what made the thermal version arguable: a measure supplied by physics. Nothing but the choice to count uniformly supplies one here, and that choice is what the thermal version did not have to make.

The static-structure position therefore owes an observer measure and does not pay it, and this is the largest open problem it leaves. Chapter 13 asked of every theory of observers what implements an observer, which histories occur and how occurrences are weighted. The worm argument answers the first with FMT's closure condition and the second with every structure that meets the two conditions. It says nothing about the third, and without the third the order we see neither confirms nor disconfirms it.

A payment would need a weighting over structures with insides under which lawful structures dominate. Description length suggests one direction, applied this time not to the region that carries the inside but to the whole structure in which it sits. A structure ordered only locally must have its surrounding noise written out bit by bit, so its shortest description is long; a lawful structure given by a rule and a first state is short. A weight that falls with description length would put most of the measure on structures whose order is global, and the order we see would become the expected case. One quantity would then do two jobs: decide where an inside can be, and weigh how much of each there is.

The direction is unproven, and three things stand between it and a result. Nothing yet argues that description length should weigh existence at all; it is a reader's measure, taken in a language, and the position has just argued that what holds for a reader need not hold for an inhabitant. The weighting inherits the dependence of description length on the language of description, which is not negligible for small structures. And a lawful structure as large as ours may contain more brief fluctuation observers than coherent ones, so a weighting over structures still leaves the weighting of observers within each structure, which is Chapter 13's question unchanged.

# Chapter 15 — What Travels Between Perspectives

## One limit

The limits on embedded description are one limit met in different places: the generator never appears completely in what it generates. Parts of it can appear, as a formal system's proofs appear in its own arithmetic (Chapter 5); the whole cannot. Either the product loses consistency, or the frame that appeared whole as content needs a further frame to hold it, and that further frame is then the generator. It holds for any perspective that is part of what it describes, runs on fewer resources than the whole, and lacks its own generating apparatus, as a whole, as content, and it takes a different form at each place where such a perspective tries to close the gap.

A formalism cannot certify its own ontological reading (Chapter 1, the reading limit). The reading, the decision about which of its structures belong to the world and which to the representation, is made by the modeler, and the modeler is not among the formalism's objects. Patches and surplus structure are sorted only by conceptual analysis or experiment, and both are done from a perspective.

Knowledge is information assigned to a reference system, and a reference system cannot be its own content (Chapter 4). The regress ends in a layer never assigned by interpretation, grown by plasticity and selection. Logic's explicit rules are serializations of competences that were never derived, which is why logic has no logical ground and no observer has its own ground, as a whole, as content.

A proper part cannot store a complete description of an incompressible whole; where no shortcut exists, a subsystem holding the exact rule obtains the future only by running it, more slowly than the whole runs itself; and a formal system expressive enough for arithmetic cannot prove all truths about itself or its own consistency (Kolmogorov, 1965; Chaitin, 1966; Wolfram, 2002; Gödel, 1931; Turing, 1936). These are the capacity, irreducibility and incompleteness limits of Chapter 5. Each is the generator, whether the whole, the full computation or the system's own derivations, failing to fit inside the part that would describe it. The same limit reaches the act of describing itself. Whether a mental model can be serialized can only be judged by trying, and success answers the question while failure does not: we cannot decide in advance whether we will be able to explain what we think (Gruber, 2015). Mathematics is the most disciplined serialization there is, and it inherits that condition along with the others.

The explicit self-model cannot represent, as a whole and as cause, the apparatus that generates it (Chapter 7; Gruber, 2026a, §4.2.2). A choice whose cause cannot appear is assigned to the self, and felt freedom is what an origin feels like from inside. The extension proposed there gives felt logical necessity the same structure: the competence that performs an inference is absent from the scene in which the conclusion appears, so the conclusion presents as compelled by its content.

Every theory an embedded mind builds rests on cuts it cannot fully sustain (Chapter 8): time and space, boundary and projection, implicit and explicit. A theory without cuts would need the whole as content.

The success of mathematics cannot certify that the world is mathematical (Chapter 3). If observers are Class 4 systems, a world above their ceiling that had Class 4 regions would appear to observers in those regions compressible to it (Gruber, 2026b, §9.5).

No observer can certify, from inside, its independence from the system it is part of, and none can tell ontic randomness from the effective randomness of an irreducible deterministic substrate (Chapter 10). The condition `P(λ|x,y) = P(λ)` is assumed in every Bell-type argument and certified in none.

The update cannot appear as an object, only as order (Chapter 11). Becoming is irreducibility seen from inside: a block that satisfies a timeless condition is lived through, because no embedded observer has a shortcut through it (Gruber, 2026b, §8.4).

The only infinity an embedded observer can have is quasi-infinity (Chapter 12): effectively unbounded from any point inside, finite in the information accessible from each.

The first person cannot weigh hypotheses about its own generation (Chapter 13). Embodied history, simulation, fluctuation and timeless instantiation can each be stipulated to contain this experience, and the generator that would decide between them is, as a whole, the one thing the self-model cannot represent.

And no perspective can have another perspective's inside as content (Chapter 14). Description and instance are drawn from the two relations an observer can stand in to a structure: holding it as content, or having it as frame.

## What travels

Against all of this, some things pass between perspectives intact, and they are enough to work with.

The first is falsity (Chapter 6). An inconsistency between a reference system and its own admitted input is decidable within that system, and every successor that keeps the input inherits the ruling. Absolute falsehood does not require absolute truth. In 1644 Mersenne listed 2^67 − 1 among the primes. Lucas showed it composite in 1876 without exhibiting a factor; in 1903 Frank Nelson Cole produced the factors. The refutation required no agreement about what numbers are, no shared ontology and no standpoint outside arithmetic; it required that a reader be able to multiply. Every perspective that can do so receives the same verdict, and every later system that keeps multiplication keeps the verdict.

This is the part of logic that travels. Negation and inconsistency can be checked by anyone holding the system and the input; whether a model is the world can be checked by no one. The asymmetry is also a gradient. A refutation is pinned by one contradiction, while a positive claim is exposed to every competitor that fits the input better, so falsity rulings tend to outlast the frameworks that issued them. They also accumulate a justificatory apparatus of their own, which is why overturning a mistaken refutation costs more than overturning a mistaken positive claim, and why the cheaply rejected ideas are the ones that come back.

The second is relational structure. FMT draws the distinction for qualia: the similarity relations among colour experiences are shareable and recoverable across brains, while which state is *my* red is per-brain and untransferable (Gruber, 2026a, §3.4.2). Mathematics is the same split applied to world-models as a whole. What a formalism captures is relational structure, and that is what different observers can share, check and build on. What the symbols are, the ontology behind the structure, stays with each perspective. Mathematics is the most shareable part of a world-model because it carries structure and drops encoding. The complex structure of quantum theory shows the split at work (Chapter 2). Real-vector-space formulations with a modified composition rule reproduce the predictions of complex quantum theory (Hoffreumon & Woods, 2025), and they carry an operator J with J² = −I, multiplication by *i* under another name. The notation changed and the structure did not: whatever generates rotation in a two-dimensional state plane went across intact, and the question of whether it is fundamental or emerges from discrete oscillators stayed exactly where it was, with each perspective that holds the formalism. This is also why two observers can agree on a formalism while disagreeing about what it describes, as physicists have done about the wavefunction for a century, without the agreement settling the disagreement.

The third is compression within scope (Chapter 9). A law that holds at our level is compressed data, valid within a scope and silent beyond it. Its validity within the scope travels: anyone at that level can test it and obtain the same result. Newton's law of gravitation lost its standing as a fundamental law a century ago and still computes the trajectories of spacecraft, and every observer who uses it inside its scope gets the same numbers. What did not travel from Newton to his successors was the ontology of an instantaneous force acting at a distance; what travelled was the compression and the scope inside which it holds, now stated with its edges. A substrate theory that discarded every such regularity would have nothing left to reproduce. Level-relativity makes a regularity local; it does not make it a fiction.

The fourth is the cuts themselves, used knowingly (Chapter 8). A cut is shareable in the way a tool is shareable: another observer can take it up, apply it, and find the same places where it fails. FMT's implicit/explicit distinction, physics' separation of time and space and the boundary/projection split of SB-HC4A each carry a statement of where they contradict themselves, and that statement travels with them. A cut handed on with its failure points marked is a better instrument than one handed on as a fact about the world.

## What does not travel

Truth from no perspective does not travel, because there is no such perspective for it to start from. Every positive claim is held by some reference system and exposed to every competitor that fits the input better. Successor frameworks inherit refutations; they do not inherit the positive content of what they replace, as the flat earth was refuted and has stayed refuted while "roughly a ball" is already exposed to whatever comes next.

The inside of another perspective does not travel: structure and reports pass between brains, machines and creatures on a disk alike, and the inside stays where it is (Chapters 13–14). The disk makes the point in both directions. We can hold the creatures' entire world, every state and every dependency, and we hold all of it as structure and none of it as an inside. If the creatures ever came to model whatever holds their world, they would hold it as content in the same way, a hypothesis about their generator subject to every limit of Chapter 13. Full access to the structure buys nothing here, because the inside was never in the structure as something to be accessed; it is the structure as it holds for one side.

The generator does not travel, because it never arrives whole. No observer has its own generator, as a whole, as content, so none can hand it to another. What observers exchange about generators are models of them, which are content and fall under every limit above. Two observers can agree on a model of their common origin, a cosmology or a theory of brains, and the agreement is agreement about structure. Whether the model is the generator is the one question their agreement cannot reach, because answering it would require holding the generator as content beside the model and comparing the two.

## The same limits, applied to the theories that state them

The limits apply to the Four-Model Theory, to SB-HC4A and to the claim that no perspective is outside, on the same terms as to anything else. Each is stated from inside what it describes and rests on cuts whose failure points are already marked. FMT's implicit/explicit boundary fails at its permeability, and its open question whether all four models are virtual marks the place; its predictions do not depend on the levels being absolute (Chapter 8). SB-HC4A's boundary/projection split meets its limit in the cognitive-ceiling objection, which Chapter 3 leaves open: a Class 4 universe is what Class 4 observers would find whether or not it is what there is (Gruber, 2026b, §9.5).

The criterion proposed for which structures have an inside is drawn from our side as well (Chapter 14). Description length is measured by a reader, in a language, and it is fixed only up to a constant that depends on the language chosen. That constant is the residue of the cut between description and instance, showing up in the very criterion meant to manage it. For structures large against the constant the residue is negligible; for small ones it is not, and no choice of language removes it, because choosing a language is choosing a side.

A theory that states where its cuts fail can be corrected at those places, and a theory that takes its cuts for the world cannot be corrected anywhere.

## Mathematics and logic, placed

Mathematics and logic are the best tools embedded observers have for two jobs: sharing structure and detecting contradiction. They are languages with built-in checkers, and the checkers are what make their results portable. A proof can be checked by anyone who accepts the rules, and a contradiction found by one reader is found by every reader who follows the steps.

They cannot certify three things. They cannot certify their own ontological reading, since no formalism tells which of its objects are world and which are costume. They cannot certify their own completeness, since a system expressive enough for arithmetic cannot prove all truths about itself or its own consistency. And they cannot certify that the world is mathematical, since their success is equally what a compressible-to-the-ceiling world would produce for observers built at the ceiling.

None of these is a human weakness, to be fixed by a larger brain or a better machine. A larger brain is still a part of what it describes, still runs on fewer resources than the whole, and still lacks its own generator, as a whole, as content. A machine in our plane is in the same position, and so is a creature on a disk. Every limit mathematics and logic have is a limit for every perspective, because no perspective is outside.

## The limits as refutations

That last claim is made for every perspective, and so are all the limits before it. Yet on the account of what travels, a positive claim passes between perspectives only as structure and as validity within a scope, never as truth from no perspective. If the limit claims were positive truths about all perspectives, held from none, they would be exactly what that account says no one holds.

They have a different form. Each limit is an impossibility: no perspective that is a proper part of what it describes holds the whole as content — not its state where the whole is incompressible, not its future where the whole is irreducible, and not its own current generation ever — and none holds its own generator, as a whole, as content. The theorems of Chapter 5 have the same form and carry their own proofs. Support for a claim of that form comes from showing that its opposite fails, and here the opposite fails from inside every perspective that might assert it. A perspective that claims to hold the whole as content contradicts what it admits about itself. If it claims the whole's state, it claims more degrees of freedom than a proper part has, and the claim fails by counting wherever the whole is incompressible (Chapter 5). If it claims the whole's generator, holding the generator gives it the whole's future only by running it, behind the whole, and a shortcut through a whole that contains the shortcut would decide its own output ahead of itself (Chapter 5). A perspective that claims to hold its own generator as content contradicts its own frame, since the frame holding that content would be one more item in it and would need a further frame to hold it (Chapter 4). In each case the contradiction lies between the claim and input the claimant cannot drop without ceasing to be the kind of observer that makes claims: being embedded, being smaller than the whole, having a frame.

That is the structure Chapter 6 gave falsity. An inconsistency between a reference system and its own admitted input is decidable inside that system and inherited by every successor that keeps the input. The limits travel the same way. They do not reach another perspective as a positive account of what it is. They reach it as a refutation it can check for itself, using only what it already admits about its position, of any claim it might make to completeness or to its own generator. A perspective that denied being part of what it describes would be beyond the refutation's reach; such a perspective would be an outside, and nothing argued from inside can exclude it. What can be said is that no perspective that admits being embedded is in a position to make the claims refuted. The limits carry the resistance Chapter 6 gives a refutation, and not immunity: the input they rest on — being embedded, having a frame — is what a perspective would have to withdraw to escape them, and a perspective that does not treat contradiction as failure is outside their reach as it is outside every refutation's.

This covers the limits and nothing else. The cuts, the proposals and the positive pictures in these chapters are not impossibility results. FMT's levels, SB-HC4A's automaton, the description-length criterion of Chapter 14 and the reading of becoming as irreducibility are positive claims, each held from a perspective and exposed to every competitor that fits the input better. They are offered for their explanatory power. Some of them are cuts in the sense of Chapter 8; none makes a claim to absolute truth. Nothing about them travels the way the limits do. The thesis that there is no outside splits in the same way. As a refutation it travels: any perspective that claims to be outside while admitting it is part of the world contradicts itself. As a positive picture of how things are, a world of embedded perspectives with nothing beyond them, it is one more claim held from inside.

## A working arrangement

Observers who cannot share their insides or reach their generators can still check each other's derivations and refute each other's claims, and the refutations hold for all of them that keep the input.

The arrangement has a method, which is the rule of Chapter 1 with one addition: every mathematical object brought into a theory either predicts something new or is labelled bookkeeping, and every theory of observers states its observer measure — what implements observers, which histories occur and how occurrences are weighted — since "everything occurs somewhere" assigns no probability to anything.

Nothing in the method requires a standpoint outside. It requires only that each perspective keep track of what it has introduced, and that it accept a refutation from any other perspective that holds the same input.

Cole's factorization of 2^67 − 1 into 193,707,721 × 761,838,257,287 refutes, for every reader who can multiply, the claim that the number is prime, and it says nothing about what a number is.
