# Part III — Physics From Inside

# Chapter 9 — Compressed Data Is Not an Assumption

## 9.1 The suspicion

Every physical law we have was found by observers at one level of one world, with instruments made of the things the law describes. General relativity predicts its own breakdown: it produces singularities at which its equations stop returning values, and it says nothing about the regime in which quantum effects and gravity are both strong. Quantum mechanics returns probabilities for the records a measurement will produce, given a preparation, and is silent about what a measurement is, what exists between two of them, and why the records are the ones they are. Both theories have been tested to high precision within their scopes. Neither says fully where its scope ends; that is learned from the other theory, or from an experiment neither predicted.

My suspicion is this. The laws we have — relativity, quantum mechanics, locality, reversibility, the conservation of probability — describe our level and only our level. General relativity is incomplete by its own admission. Quantum mechanics matches reality closely within its scope, and only for that scope; and since it supplies no account of what is there, it is a theory of what observers will record, a probability calculus that is silent about the substrate producing the records. The reconstructions of quantum theory from information-theoretic axioms (Hardy, 2001) and the QBist reading of the quantum state as an agent's degrees of belief make this explicit from inside physics: the formalism can be rebuilt from postulates about information and probability in which no physical object is named. A calculus matched to a scope tells us nothing about how wrong it is beyond the scope. Newtonian gravity agreed with every planetary observation to the limit of measurement for two centuries, except for forty-three seconds of arc per century in the perihelion of Mercury; the Uranus patch that turned out to be Neptune (Chapter 1) gave no sign that the next anomaly would not yield to the same remedy. Its mechanism, a force acting instantly across empty space, is absent from the theory that replaced it. The precision was real. The ontology was not. Nothing inside Newtonian gravity could have told its users which of its objects were the world and which were the representation — Chapter 1's reading limit. The same holds of every current theory until a deeper one shows where its scope ends, and that deeper theory will be in the same position relative to the one after it.

## 9.2 Where the suspicion overreaches

Pushed one step further, the suspicion overreaches. The step is an inference: X holds only at our level; therefore X is an assumption; therefore X should be dropped. Applied to locality, to reversibility, to probability conservation, to the metric signature, it makes each of them a candidate for deletion. The inference fails at both steps.

The first step confuses three statuses a statement can have. A statement can be an axiom of a theory, assumed in order to derive the rest. It can be a regularity in data, measured and recorded. And it can be a claim about what exists. The conservation of probability is a consequence of the quantum formalism's unitary evolution, a regularity in every experiment ever recorded, and an ontological claim only if someone makes it one. "Holds at our level" is a statement about the second status: the regularity has been measured here and not elsewhere. It says nothing about whether the theory that contains it treats it as an axiom, and nothing about whether anyone has reified it. A law that holds at our level is compressed data. It summarizes a very large number of measurements in a short statement that reproduces them. Being valid within a scope is what a compression is: a compression is a regularity in some data, and the data have an extent.

The second step treats "assumption" as a synonym for "false". An assumption is a statement used without being derived. Most assumptions in physics are compressions promoted to postulates because they have never failed within the domain being formalized; the promotion is a bookkeeping decision about where to start, and it says nothing about truth either way. The fallacy identified in Chapter 1 was reification: taking the compression for the fundamental thing. Using a compression inside its scope is not reification. Taking it to be the substrate is.

The consequence of the overreach is the decisive objection to it. Suppose every regularity that holds only at our level is discarded as an artefact. Then a theory of the substrate has nothing left to reproduce. Its success condition has been emptied: there is no observation it must recover and no observation that could refute it, and it becomes exactly as untestable as any interpretation of quantum mechanics that predicts no deviation from the formalism (Chapter 12). The regularities at our level are the only data any substrate theory will ever be tested against.

## 9.3 Precision about quantum mechanics

Two claims about quantum mechanics need to be put exactly before the programme can use them.

Quantum mechanics is sometimes charged with inconsistency on account of its infinities. The charge is wrong. Non-relativistic quantum mechanics — a Hilbert space, a unitary evolution, the Born rule — is a mathematically consistent structure; nothing in it derives a contradiction. The infinities belong to quantum field theory, the relativistic extension, where they appear in perturbative calculations and are removed by renormalization. Renormalization is a legitimate target for Chapter 1's suspicion about patches, and the physicists who built it said as much; a patch is not an inconsistency, and a theory that needs one is not thereby contradictory. The defect quantum mechanics does have is incompleteness: it does not contain gravity, and general relativity does not contain it. Incompleteness and inconsistency are different defects. A consistent theory can be arbitrarily incomplete; an inconsistent theory proves everything and is complete in the worst possible way.

The second claim is the one in my suspicion, and it holds in one reading only. Quantum mechanics is a theory of what observers will record. It takes a preparation and a measurement procedure as inputs and returns a distribution over records; it does not say what the system is doing between preparation and record, and every attempt to add such an account — pilot waves, branching worlds, spontaneous collapse, deterministic automata — adds something the formalism does not require and predicts, in most cases, nothing the formalism does not predict. That is what it means to call it a probability calculus without an ontology, and the information-theoretic reconstructions show that the calculus can be derived from postulates in which no physical object appears (Hardy, 2001). None of this makes the calculus false, approximate, or unphysical within its scope; its records are records of physical events, made by physical apparatus. It makes it silent about the substrate. A weather forecast that gives a seventy per cent chance of rain is a physical statement about tomorrow and says nothing about how clouds form; quantum mechanics is in that position, with the difference that no deeper theory of its clouds is yet known.

## 9.4 Compression and its boundary

A compression carries more than the data it summarizes. It carries, at the edges of its scope, the first information about the level below.

The ideal gas law is the plain case. Pressure times volume is proportional to temperature: one line, and it reproduces the behaviour of every dilute gas ever measured. It is a compression, and nothing about it is an assumption about the nature of gases; it was found in the data before kinetic theory was accepted. It fails at high density and low temperature, and the manner of its failure — gases that occupy more volume than the law allows, and that attract one another into liquids — is the signature of molecules with a finite size and a short-range force between them. The deeper theory, statistical mechanics, reproduces the law exactly where it held and explains the failure exactly where it failed. The law was never the substrate. It was the substrate's most visible regularity at the level where dilute gases are observed, and the boundary of its scope was among the first measurements of what lay underneath.

Reification would have been to conclude that each molecule obeys the gas law, and to ask each molecule for its temperature. Nobody did. The molecular hypothesis came from chemistry, and kinetic theory derived the law itself; but the manner of the law's failure became one of the first measurements of molecules, since van der Waals read their size and their attraction off the deviations. Deletion would have been to discard the gas law as a level-bound artefact before statistical mechanics existed. Had that been done, there would have been no failure to explain and no boundary to measure, and kinetic theory would have had nothing to reproduce. The gas law's scope-boundedness was no reason to distrust it. It was one of the places the next level showed through.

The pattern generalizes. A regularity that holds only at our level is a compression whose scope is our level, and its scope boundary — the regime in which it fails — is the only place where the structure of the level below becomes observable from here. Quantum mechanics and general relativity each mark where the other's scope ends, and their constants combined mark the Planck scale, where both are expected to fail and neither has been tested. Those boundaries are the only data about the substrate any observer at our level has. A substrate theory is obliged to reproduce the regularities inside the boundaries and is tested by what it predicts at them.

A law written in mathematics is a world-model's compression written down. Its validity within its scope is the whole of its content. Whether its symbols — the wavefunction, the metric, the pressure — name things is a further question, the ontological reading, and no formalism answers it (Chapter 1). The history of heat shows how far apart the two can lie. For half a century heat was treated as a fluid, caloric, that flowed from hot bodies to cold ones and was conserved. The picture worked. Carnot, reasoning about caloric falling from a hot reservoir to a cold one as water falls through a mill wheel, derived that the maximum efficiency of a heat engine depends only on the temperatures of the two reservoirs, and the limit still stands. Caloric does not exist. Heat turned out to be the motion of molecules, and Carnot's limit survived the loss of the fluid it had been derived for. The result was a sound compression of what engines can do; the fluid was a reading of the symbols, and nothing in the derivation could have told its users which was which.

## 9.5 Four regularities, read as compressions

The regularities my suspicion singled out can each be read this way, and reading them so fixes what has to be decided about each.

Locality. No influence has been measured to outrun light, and no operation on one system changes the statistics observed at another. That is the compression. Its reification is the claim that the substrate is a set of separate things at separate places. Its scope boundary is already visible: entangled systems violate the Bell inequalities, so one of the premises of any local account of their correlations fails somewhere, and which one fails is the question Bell's theorem (Bell, 1964) leaves open.

Reversibility. The microscopic dynamics, wherever they have been measured, can be run backwards — unitarity in the quantum formalism, invertible equations of motion in the classical one — while every macroscopic process runs one way. That is a pair of compressions, one for each scale, and the relation between them is understood: the one-way behaviour comes from a boundary condition and a coarse-graining, the two-way behaviour from the law. The reification is the claim that the substrate is reversible because the formalism is. The scope boundary is wherever the formalism stops, which nobody has found. Experiments that look for spontaneous collapse — information leaking out of unitary evolution and showing up as noise — keep tightening their limits and have found nothing.

Probability conservation. In every experiment, something happens, and the probabilities of the things that can happen add to one. The compression is nearly empty, as a compression should be when the regularity is nearly universal. Its reification is the inference from "probabilities add to one" to "the evolution is reversible", which does not follow (Chapter 11).

The signature. One speed is the same for every observer, and the intervals observers agree on are those in which time and space enter with opposite signs. That is the compression. The reification would be the claim that the world is a four-dimensional manifold carrying that metric; the deflation would be the claim that the sign is an assumption bolted on to make general relativity work. Neither is what the data say. The data say there is an invariant speed.

In each case the same three questions apply: what has been measured, what has been inferred from it beyond the measurement, and where the measurement stops. The first is the compression. The second is where reification enters. The third is where the substrate shows.

## 9.6 The productive programme

The programme that replaces deletion has three steps. List the regularities observed at our level. Deny that any of them is fundamental. Find a substrate that reproduces them within their scopes and predicts where each scope ends.

This is the method of my cosmology paper (Gruber, 2026b). The paper assumes, and labels as an assumption, that the substrate is deterministic. It then takes what physics has recorded at our level as data: that the universe supports universal computation, that embedded systems perform computation with no predictive shortcut, that critical dynamics occur and sustain themselves, that static matter, periodic motion and fractal structure all exist. It asks which classes of deterministic rule could produce that data, and eliminates the ones that could not: a static or periodic rule cannot generate open-ended computation, a reducible rule with a closed form would make the universe predictable without being run, and lawless dynamics would make the success of physics an unexplained coincidence. What remains is the class at the edge of chaos, by elimination and conditional on the assumption (Gruber, 2026b, §3.2–3.3). At no point does the argument treat a measured regularity as false because it holds at our level. Every regularity is retained as a constraint the substrate must satisfy; what is denied is only that any of them is the substrate.

What the method obliges is as specific as what it forbids. A substrate theory must reproduce the quantum statistics within quantum mechanics' scope and the relativistic geometry within general relativity's. It may deny that either is fundamental. It may not deny that either is data, and it is refuted the moment it contradicts a measured regularity inside that regularity's scope. The asymmetry of Chapter 6 applies: a contradiction with admitted input is detectable, then permanent, and inherited by every successor that keeps the input; reproducing the input is not confirmation, since any number of substrates might reproduce it. A substrate theory is tested at the scope boundaries, where it predicts something the compressions do not. The automaton derivations of the free field equations give the form such a prediction takes: a Lorentz symmetry that is exact in the long-wavelength limit and deformed near the lattice scale, so that the dispersion of a free field at wavelengths approaching the lattice scale departs from the relativistic relation by an amount the compression puts at zero (D'Ariano & Perinotti, 2014). Sound in a crystal is the familiar case of the same thing. At long wavelengths sound travels at one speed whatever its pitch, and the crystal behaves as a continuous medium. As the wavelength shrinks towards the spacing of the atoms, the speed starts to depend on the wavelength, and no wave shorter than two atomic spacings exists at all. Where the continuum law starts to fail, the spacing of the atoms can be read off. Whether any such departure exists is an empirical question, and it is the right kind of question: it is asked at a scope boundary, and the compression and the substrate theory answer it differently.

## 9.7 Precision without ontology

Matching reality closely within a scope tells nothing about how wrong a theory is beyond the scope. Newtonian gravity was precise to the limit of measurement for two centuries and wrong about its own mechanism from the first day. Quantum mechanics is precise to many decimal places and silent about its mechanism, and the silence may hide an error of the same size as Newton's or none at all; the precision does not say.

Two inferences are blocked by this, and they are the same error with opposite signs. One cannot infer from the precision of quantum mechanics that its objects exist: that amplitudes rotate in a complex plane, that a wavefunction occupies configuration space, that the Hilbert space is the arena of the world. That inference is reification. One also cannot infer from its scope-boundedness that its objects do not exist, or that its regularities are artefacts to be dropped. That inference is deletion, and it discards the data. What remains between the two is a flat statement of what a compression's content is: its predictions within its scope. Its ontology stays open until a deeper theory shows where the scope ends — and the deeper theory settles the ontology only relative to its own scope, from inside which it was built. There is no theory of a level that was not made at some level. A description of physics is made by physical systems, and the making is not among what the description holds while it is being made (Chapter 5).

## 9.8 From laws to experiments

Laws are compressions; the compressions are data; the substrate is constrained by the data and tested at the boundaries. What remains is how an embedded observer obtains the data at all. Every regularity listed above was established by experiment, and an experiment is performed from inside. The apparatus is made of the matter whose laws are under test. The settings are chosen by processes that run on the same substrate as the system being measured. The experimenter who reads the record is a subprocess of the computation whose randomness, locality and independence the experiment is meant to probe.

# Chapter 10 — Randomness, Independence, and the Observer Inside the Experiment

## 10.1 Two kinds of randomness

A deterministic rule with a finite description, applied to a finite initial condition, cannot produce output more complex than the two together. The statement is Kolmogorov's and Chaitin's (Kolmogorov, 1965; Chaitin, 1966): the complexity of a string is the length of the shortest program that produces it, and the output of a rule applied to an initial condition is produced by the program "apply this rule to this initial condition for this many steps", whose length is fixed apart from the step count. Any prefix of the output of a cellular automaton is therefore compressible to the rule, the initial condition and a step counter; a sequence that no program shorter than itself can produce — a genuinely random one, in Kolmogorov's definition — is beyond any such rule. My cosmology paper calls randomness of that kind **ontic**, generated by no expressible rule, and reserves a class for it that no automaton occupies (Gruber, 2026b, §2.3–2.4).

The digits of π show how far appearance and complexity can come apart. Its first million decimal digits pass the standard statistical tests for randomness: each digit occurs about a tenth of the time, pairs and longer blocks occur at the expected rates, and no test finds a pattern that would let anyone predict the next digit from the ones before it. Yet the whole million is produced by a program a few lines long, so its complexity in Kolmogorov's sense is tiny. Someone handed the million digits without their source would find no pattern and could not exclude one; the shortest description is there, and nothing in the digits points to it.

What an automaton can produce is **effective** randomness: output that passes the statistical tests an observer applies, and that, as far as is known, cannot be predicted except by running the rule. Rule 30 (Chapter 5) is the standard case. The rules that behave like this — irreducible, and universal where universality has been proved (Cook, 2004, for Rule 110) — are the Class 4 rules, and effective randomness is their ordinary product.

An observer inside such a system cannot tell the two kinds apart, and the reason is structural. The observer is a subprocess of the computation it is trying to classify, with strictly fewer cells and a slower clock than the whole (Gruber, 2026b, §2.5). To certify that a sequence is ontically random it would have to show that no rule produces it, which is a claim about every program, and the quantity that would settle it — the sequence's Kolmogorov complexity — is not computable by any procedure. To certify that a sequence is effectively random it would have to exhibit a rule and initial data that reproduce it, and finding them is a search over programs that no procedure is guaranteed to finish, run with fewer resources than the computation has. Neither certification is available from inside.

The predicament has a general form, which Wolpert (2008) proved for any device that observes, predicts or recalls the universe containing it: such devices face impossibility results that hold whatever the physical laws are, with the halting problem as their model. Szangolies (2018) derives limits of this self-referential kind, which he calls epistemic horizons, from a fixed-point theorem, and proposes them as the origin of the informational limits of quantum theory. Quantum measurement outcomes, which pass every statistical test, can on this reading be effective randomness from a deterministic substrate, which is the position of 't Hooft's cellular automaton interpretation ('t Hooft, 2016) and the assumption my cosmology paper declares (Gruber, 2026b, §3.2). The limit applies in both directions: the assumption cannot be confirmed from inside either. What the observer measures is indistinguishability.

The limit applies to the certification of randomness by experiment as well. Protocols exist that certify randomness from the violation of a Bell inequality with no trust placed in the devices; they are the strongest randomness certification physics has. Each of them takes as an input that the settings fed to the devices were chosen independently of the devices' internal state, fully or to a stated degree. The certification cannot reach the assumption it stands on.

## 10.2 What Bell's theorem assumes

Bell's theorem is a constraint on correlations.

Two wings of an experiment, far apart. At each wing an observer chooses a setting — x on the left, y on the right — and obtains an outcome, a or b. Suppose there is some variable λ, the complete state of whatever the two wings share from their common past, such that three things hold. First, **factorization**: given λ and the local settings, the outcomes are independent, P(a,b|x,y,λ) = P(a|x,λ)·P(b|y,λ). This is Bell's local causality: once everything in the shared past is specified, what happens at one wing depends on that wing's setting and on nothing at the other. Second, **measurement independence**: the distribution of λ does not depend on the settings, P(λ|x,y) = P(λ). Third, each run has one outcome at each wing. From these premises the CHSH combination of correlations is bounded, S ≤ 2. Quantum mechanics predicts S up to 2√2 for suitable entangled states and settings, and experiments measure values above 2, since 2015 with the detection and locality loopholes closed in the same experiment. The freedom-of-choice loophole, measurement independence itself, can only be narrowed (§10.3).

Three things are not among the premises. Determinism is not assumed; the outcomes may be stochastic given λ. Randomness is not assumed; nothing in the derivation requires that any process be ontically random. And the truth of quantum mechanics is not assumed; the bound is derived from the premises alone, and the violation is measured. Any theory of the substrate, an automaton included, must reproduce correlations with S > 2, and must therefore give up at least one premise.

The ways out correspond to the premises. Give up factorization, and the theory is nonlocal in Bell's sense: Bohmian mechanics does this, is fully deterministic, retains measurement independence, and reproduces the predictions of non-relativistic quantum mechanics exactly. Give up measurement independence, and the theory is superdeterministic, or retrocausal, which is a way of making λ depend on settings chosen later. Give up single outcomes, and the theory branches. Determinism appears on none of these routes because it is not a premise, and a deterministic world does not, by being deterministic, take any of them. Bohmian mechanics and the many-worlds interpretation are both deterministic and take different routes; quantum mechanics does not require ontic randomness.

I have held a stronger objection: that in a deterministic world everything with a shared past is correlated, so that measurement independence is unattainable in principle. It is wrong. Correlation requires more than a shared past; it requires that the dependency structure of the dynamics route information from the common past into both variables in a way that survives everything in between. Take the settings from the binary digits of π, computed by a machine in the laboratory, and the hidden state from the polarization of photon pairs emitted by a crystal. Both are determined. Both lie in the causal past of the measurement. Nothing in the dynamics correlates the digits of π with the state of the crystal, and a superdeterminist owes the mechanism that would. Determinism makes every variable a function of the initial state; it does not make every pair of variables statistically dependent, and chaotic mixing generically destroys whatever dependence the common past did seed. That is why randomized trials work in a world that may be deterministic throughout. Measurement independence is not a claim that the settings are uncaused. It is a claim about one joint distribution, P(λ, x, y), and determinism says nothing about which joint distribution obtains.

## 10.3 The observer who cannot step outside

A different claim does hold, and it does not depend on determinism at all.

Measurement independence is an assumption about the joint distribution of the hidden state and the settings. Testing it requires settings whose independence from the hidden state is known. The only way to know that is to generate the settings by a process whose independence from the source is assumed, which is the assumption under test. An experimenter may push the setting generator as far from the source as the universe allows — to a random number generator, to a human choice, to the light of a quasar — and at each step the question is the same: is this generator independent of λ? The cosmic Bell test did exactly this (Rauch et al., 2018). Settings were fixed by whether arriving photons from two quasars were redder or bluer than a threshold wavelength, photons that had travelled for billions of years, 7.8 billion in the case of the nearer source, a long way to come for one bit; under the stated assumptions — that the setting was fixed when the light was emitted and that no influence outruns light — any local common cause of settings and hidden state must have acted before that light left, which the authors compute as the exclusion of such a mechanism from about 96 per cent of the space-time volume of the experiment's past light cone. That is a constraint on a specified class of explanations under specified assumptions. It is not a demonstration that the settings and the hidden state have no common dependence, and the authors do not claim one; the remaining few per cent include the big bang, and the assumptions include the locality whose denial is one of Bell's exits.

The limit is a first-person limit, and first-person means any embedded perspective. No observer can certify, from inside, its independence from the system it is part of. The process that generates its choices runs on the same substrate as the process that generates the outcomes it will measure; whether the two are independent is a fact about the substrate's joint distribution, and that distribution is what the observer has no access to except through experiments whose settings it must choose. The setting generator can be measured like anything else. Its independence from λ cannot, because measuring that would need a further generator whose independence is assumed, and in the experiment the independence appears only as an assumption. Breuer (1995) proved a neighbouring limit for any observer, classical or quantum, deterministic or not: an observer cannot distinguish all present states of a system that contains it, and, as a corollary, cannot measure the quantum correlations between itself and a system outside it. Measurement independence is a claim about a correlation of another kind, between the observer's choices and the source, namely that it is zero, and the regress through setting generators puts it out of reach by a different route. Wheeler's suggestion that there is only one electron, threading backwards and forwards through time, is the extreme picture of total coupling: Feynman's Nobel lecture records the suggestion, and records his own objection, that the world does not contain as many positrons as the picture requires (Feynman, 1965). It is a historical possibility and not a measured coupling, and it is the limiting case of a question that has no inside answer: how tightly is this apparatus coupled to that quasar?

The inability to certify independence is not evidence of coupling. By the asymmetry of Chapter 6, a refutation requires a specific contradiction with admitted input; "it cannot be excluded" is not an input. And a theory that is permitted to fit an arbitrary dependence between settings and hidden state predicts nothing: for any observed correlation there is a dependence that produces it, so no observation refutes the theory and no observation confirms it. The reflexive dismissal of superdeterminism as a conspiracy is poorly argued, and Hossenfelder and Palmer (2020) are right that most of the standard objections rest on intuitions about linear classical systems; the charge that holds is the narrower one, that a superdeterministic theory owes a specific dependence and a specific prediction that follows from it. Hossenfelder and Palmer also say where such a theory could be tested — in regimes where its specific dependence predicts departures from quantum statistics. That is the shape a testable superdeterminism has. A merely permitted one has no shape at all.

## 10.4 Locality and its price

The second premise, factorization, is the one my cosmology paper gives up.

Factorization presupposes that the two wings are two systems in two regions, separated by a spacelike gap, whose joint state decomposes into local states once the shared past is specified. If spacetime is an emergent projection of a lower-dimensional computation, that presupposition is a fact about the projection and need not hold at the substrate. Cells adjacent in the substrate need not be adjacent in the emergent spacetime, and two points at opposite ends of the emergent space may be one locus in the substrate's encoding. Locality at our level — no measured influence outruns light, and no operation on one system changes the statistics at another — is then a regularity of the projection, compressed data in the sense of Chapter 9, and no evidence of locality at the bottom. The cosmology paper takes exactly this route: an entangled pair is one configuration on the holographic boundary that appears as two separated particles only in the decompressed interior; the correlations are two interior reflections of one locus, and no influence crosses the gap because at the substrate there is no gap (Gruber, 2026b, §6.5). This is Bell's nonlocality option, the oldest consistent one, and Bohmian mechanics is the existence proof that the corner of theory space it occupies — deterministic, nonlocal, measurement independence retained — is coherent.

Economy follows. A theory that gives up factorization has already escaped the bound; it does not also need to give up measurement independence. Superdeterminism and nonlocal projection are two payments for one debt, and a theory that makes both has overpaid. Physics has no office for refunds. My cosmology paper rejects factorization and keeps measurement independence. It keeps it as every Bell-type argument does, by assumption: the paper's remark that the apparatus settings are not entangled with the pair before the measurement gives a reason to expect the assumption to hold and does not derive it, since nothing inside a world certifies that its setting generators are independent of what they measure. The choice is the economical one.

The price of the choice is specific, and the paper names it (Gruber, 2026b, §6.5). Giving up factorization dissolves the Bell bound too thoroughly: the correlations that no-signalling alone permits extend to S = 4, while quantum mechanics and nature stop at Tsirelson's bound of 2√2. The extreme case can be described as a pair of hypothetical boxes, one at each wing. Each takes a setting, 0 or 1, and returns a bit. The two bits always agree, except when both settings are 1, and then they always disagree. Three of the four correlations in the CHSH combination are then +1 and the fourth, which enters with a minus sign, is −1, so S = 4. Yet neither party can send anything with the boxes: each output, on its own, is a fair coin whatever either party does, and the pattern shows only when the two records are brought together. Nothing in no-signalling forbids such boxes. Nature has never been seen to supply them. A nonlocal projection therefore owes an account of why its correlations stop exactly there, and reproducing the quantum value is a near-empty achievement until it does, since any number of nonlocal mechanisms can be tuned to emit 2√2. The paper offers a conditional derivation and names the condition; the debt is open.

## 10.5 No-signalling and the fork

The regularity that disciplines every nonlocal proposal is no-signalling: the statistics at one wing do not depend on the setting chosen at the other, so the correlations cannot be used to send a message. Within quantum mechanics this is a theorem. In nature it is a measured regularity, confirmed every time an entanglement experiment is run and never violated. It is compressed data in the strict sense of the previous chapter: without a theory of the substrate, nobody can know that information transfer at our level is impossible, only that it has not been observed within the scope of the theories we have. A lattice substrate, moreover, has something relativity lacks: a preferred frame, the lattice itself. The paradoxes of faster-than-light signalling — messages that arrive before they are sent — arise from combining superluminal transfer with Lorentz invariance, which lets a boost turn a fast signal into a backwards one. With a preferred frame there is no such boost. A substrate could carry influences faster than light without any causal loop; at our level the frame would show as a violation of Lorentz invariance at its scale, and, by the theorem below, possibly as signalling.

There is a theorem that turns this into a test. Bancal and colleagues (Bancal et al., 2012) consider a specific class of models: Bell correlations are produced by hidden influences that propagate at some finite speed v, faster than light but finite, in a preferred frame, and the model reproduces the quantum predictions whenever its influences arrive in time. They show that for any such v there are four-party arrangements in which the model must do one of two things: predict correlations that differ from quantum mechanics, or produce correlations that themselves allow signalling faster than light — not hidden signalling in the substrate, but signalling available to the parties at our level. The result constrains that class and only that class: finite propagation speed, preferred frame, influences that arrive or do not. A projection in which nothing propagates because the two wings are one locus is not a member of the class, and the theorem says nothing against it. Scoped this way, the result is an asset to the substrate view, because it produces a fork with a different prediction on each branch. If the substrate's nonlocality is carried by influences moving at a finite speed through the lattice, then either the quantum predictions fail somewhere or signalling exists in principle, and multi-party experiments can look for either. If the projection is genuinely nonlocal — one locus, nothing moving — there is no signalling at any speed. My cosmology paper asserts the second branch (Gruber, 2026b, §6.5): correlations, never signals. It is a commitment, and the fork states what would refute it: any finite speed at which influence travels inside the lattice.

## 10.6 What the automata have done

Cellular-automaton physics has produced specific results, each with a specific scope.

D'Ariano and Perinotti (2014) derive the Dirac equation from a quantum cellular automaton — a lattice of cells each carrying a finite-dimensional quantum system, updated by a unitary step — under the principles of unitarity, locality, homogeneity and a discrete isotropy, with the Dirac dynamics recovered in a limit of wavelengths long compared with the lattice scale. Lorentz symmetry emerges approximately in that limit and is deformed near the lattice scale, which is in principle a prediction. Two scopes attach to the result. The automaton is already quantum: it does not derive quantum mechanics from a classical rule, and its cells carry amplitudes from the start. And the fields are free: no interaction, no gravity. 't Hooft's programme ('t Hooft, 2016) starts further down, with classical states — ontological states, in his term — that evolve by permutations, and treats the quantum formalism as a tool for describing their statistics; by his own account no model has yet been found that reproduces known particle physics. Wolfram's (2002) programme has produced a vast catalogue of simple rules with complex behaviour and the Principle of Computational Equivalence as a conjecture; it has not produced a quantitative prediction. Free fields from automata exist. Interacting fields with gravity from an automaton exist in no programme.

The argument from universality adds less than it seems to. It runs: a Class 4 automaton is Turing complete, so it should be easy to produce in it uncountably many systems that behave, on the projected plane, exactly as the Bell experiments require. Two things are wrong with it. The rules are countable: a rule is a finite description, and there are only countably many finite descriptions; what is uncountable is the set of infinite configurations, and the two are different objects to count. And universality guarantees existence, not abundance. A universal automaton can simulate the quantum formalism, so an automaton exists whose emergent description contains Bell-violating correlations; that is cheap, and it says nothing about how many rules do it, how large a rule it takes, or whether a random rule applied to random data will. The simulation that universality guarantees stores amplitudes globally — which is the nonlocal projection again, at exponential cost. The problem that remains is the one no programme has solved: the minimal natural rule from which the quantum statistics, the relativistic geometry and gravity all emerge without being written in by hand. That the behaviour exists somewhere in rule space was never in doubt. Where, and how rarely, is the question.

## 10.7 What an embedded experimenter establishes

An observer inside the computation can measure correlations. It can refute specific models — local ones, finite-speed ones, superdeterministic ones that commit to a dependence — by finding correlations they forbid. It can place the substrate on a branch of Bancal's fork by looking for signalling in multi-party experiments. It cannot certify that any sequence is ontically random, cannot certify its own independence from the system it measures, and cannot read off from locality at its level whether the substrate is local. Those three are not failures of apparatus. They are the condition of being a subprocess of what one measures. Every experiment is also ordered in time, and the clock by which it is ordered is inside too.

# Chapter 11 — Time Without a Clock

## 11.1 The suspicion

Relativity made time a coordinate on a four-dimensional manifold and then distinguished it from the other three by a sign. The textbook gloss is that all four dimensions are on an equal footing and that we happen to move through one of them at the speed of light: the whole observable universe does, which is an asymmetry strange enough to make one suspect that it belongs to the observer rather than to the world. The suspicion has three parts. Time is not obviously a dimension. The metric signature, the minus sign that separates time from space, is an assumption needed to make general relativity work, not a trace of reality. And dynamics are not fundamental; they emerge as a point of view, after the fact, from a structure that does not itself change.

The third part stands, and Chapter 14 shows on which physics it depends. The first two rest, as stated, on formal artefacts.

## 11.2 Three replies

The first concerns the sentence "we all move through spacetime at the speed of light." It is true, and it is empty. The four-velocity of a body is the derivative of its position in spacetime with respect to its own proper time, and proper time is defined so that the four-velocity has magnitude c — the normalization is written into the definition. The statement that everything moves at c through spacetime is the statement that the four-velocity has the magnitude the four-velocity was defined to have. It is a tautology of the bookkeeping, and it is exactly the kind of artefact Chapter 1 warned about: a formal consequence of a notation, read as a fact about the world. It also fails for light, which has no proper time, and so no four-velocity in this sense at all. On this reading the speed of light is the speed of everything except light. What the bookkeeping does record is that there is an invariant speed, the same in every frame; that is a fact, and the four-velocity's magnitude is one of its costumes.

The second concerns the signature. It does not come from general relativity, and it is not an assumption added to make general relativity work. It is in Maxwell's equations, published fifty years before general relativity: the wave operator that governs light, ∂²/∂t² − c²∇², carries the minus sign between the time derivative and the space derivatives; every wave equation since d'Alembert's carries it, and what light added is that the speed in it is the same in every frame, which makes that sign the Lorentzian signature before anyone had named it. Minkowski's geometry of 1908 made the sign into the structure of a space, with light cones as its null directions (Minkowski, 1908/1909). The signature is the geometric form of an invariant speed: if there is one speed that every observer measures the same, the intervals that observers agree on are the ones in which time and space enter with opposite signs. General relativity inherited the signature; it did not introduce it.

The signature is also, in a definite sense, what my own view predicts, and the sense is narrower than the reply usually given. A substrate that updates locally, one cell influencing its neighbours per step, has a bounded propagation speed, and therefore a **dependency cone**: the set of cells whose state can affect a given cell at a given step. A dependency cone is not Lorentz symmetry. A lattice has a preferred frame and a discrete symmetry group; the cone is a diamond, or a cube, with corners; nothing about it is invariant under boosts. Lorentz symmetry, if it emerges, emerges in a limit and under further conditions, and D'Ariano and Perinotti (2014) needed unitarity, homogeneity and isotropy as well as locality, and a limit of long wavelengths, to recover it from their automaton — approximately, with deformations at the lattice scale. What the dependency cone gives by itself is weaker and correct: a bounded information speed, which at our level appears as an invariant speed, which the signature encodes. The signature is a trace of bounded information speed at our level. That is consistent with the substrate view and is not an assumption against it; by the asymmetry of Chapter 6, reproducing it is no confirmation either.

The third concerns the number of time dimensions. If all dimensions were equal, nothing would single out one of them as time, and the number of time-like directions would be a free parameter. It is not free. Tegmark (1997) showed that with more than one time dimension the field equations of physics become ultrahyperbolic: the initial-value problem is ill-posed, data on a surface do not determine the solution, and an observer could predict nothing. With one time dimension the equations are hyperbolic and prediction is possible. The argument is the anthropic argument of Chapter 3 applied to time: brains that survive by predicting likely futures can, on that analysis, exist only where there is exactly one time dimension. That the whole observable universe shares exactly one time dimension is then no egocentric accident; it is a condition on there being anyone to observe it. The asymmetry is real, and it is in the world, in the sense that the world contains observers only where the asymmetry holds.

## 11.3 Time is the update

In a computation, time is not a dimension.

A cellular automaton has a lattice and a rule. The lattice is space: an index set of cells that exist simultaneously, with an adjacency relation among them. The rule is the update: given a configuration of the lattice, it produces the next. Time is the update, the successor relation between configurations. It is not a fourth axis of the lattice, and no cell lies along it. The familiar picture of a one-dimensional automaton — rows stacked downwards, each the successor of the one above, forming a two-dimensional pattern — is a representation that lays the update out as if it were a spatial axis. It is useful, and it is a choice. The rows exist in sequence, generated one from the other; the picture exists all at once, because somebody drew it.

![Time is the update. Each row is one configuration of a one-dimensional automaton (Rule 30), and the rule produces the next row from it. The shaded region holds every earlier cell the marked cell depends on. The update lies between rows and appears in none of them.](figures/no-outside/ch11-time-is-the-update.pdf){width=85%}


Treating time as a fourth coordinate of spacetime is the same choice made at the level of physics. It is the representation under which the update appears as a coordinate, and it is productive: it makes the symmetries of the equations visible as symmetries of a space. What remains of time's difference from space inside the representation is the minus sign. Within a spacelike slice, the relations are simultaneous constraints among the cells of one configuration. Across slices, along any timelike direction, the relations are dependencies between configurations, one generating the next. The signature is where the cut shows through — the cut between time and space that Chapter 8 names as one every physical theory makes in order to be thinkable, and that the theory then contradicts by treating the two as coordinates of one manifold. The residue of the cut is the sign. It does not mark one of the four coordinates as the time axis; relativity has no such axis, since any timelike direction serves some observer as one. It records that one class of directions is unlike the others: the directions along which configurations generate configurations. The substrate's update picks a single axis in that class, the lattice's own; our level sees only the class, as it sees no preferred frame (Chapter 10).

The update is the generating relation of the computation, and the principle of Chapter 5 holds of it: the update as such never appears complete in any configuration as content; what appears is its trace, the order of configurations. An observer inside the computation is a pattern in configurations. It can represent, as content, the states of cells and the relations among them in a configuration, the order of configurations as recorded in its own memory, and the rule table itself, if it has found it; the generator's contents are not barred. What it cannot represent is the step that is producing the configuration it is representing in, because that step is in no configuration. It is between them. What the observer has of that step is order: that this configuration follows that one. Time is what an embedded observer never has complete as content, only as order, and that is the first reason time is not obviously a dimension.

## 11.4 The inherited arrow

A reversible update has no direction of its own. It can be run from either end, and nothing in the rule says which end is the past. The direction has to come from somewhere, and in both of my theories it comes from the same place.

My cosmology paper takes a reversible substrate as a declared choice: no two distinct substrate states evolve to the same state, and total information is conserved (Gruber, 2026b, §8.4). The arrow of time inside is emergent, and it comes from two things together. One is a boundary condition: the compressed phase from which each cycle of the interior decompresses, the counterpart in that architecture of the Past Hypothesis — the low-entropy early universe that on the standard analysis is the source of the thermodynamic arrow (Albert, 2000). The other is coarse-graining: an interior observer tracks the decompressed projection and cannot see behind horizons, where the information needed to invert the dynamics is sequestered. From inside, the dynamics look like Rule 30: one-way, entropy-producing, illegible. At the substrate, they are a reversible computation. A reversible law does not erase the arrow; the arrow was never a property of the law. It is a property of a boundary condition seen through a coarse-graining, and the observer sits inside the coarse-graining.

The Four-Model Theory makes the matching claim about the observer (Gruber, 2026a, §3.4.4). The explicit models that constitute experience are implemented in the coarse-grained regime in which the thermodynamic arrow is defined. Records, traces and memory formation are entropy-gradient phenomena; a memory is a low-entropy configuration that correlates with a past state and not with a future one, because that is what the gradient permits. The cognitive arrow — the direction in which a mind remembers and anticipates — is therefore inherited from the thermodynamic one. There are not two arrows that happen to align. There is one arrow, and the modeler is inside the layer that has it. Neither theory assumes a direction of time. Both derive the experienced direction from where the experiencer sits.

## 11.5 Felt order is constructed

The direction is inherited. The order of events within the direction is something else again, and here the Four-Model Theory makes a stronger claim: felt temporal order is constructed at the explicit layer and is not read off the order in which signals reach the substrate (Gruber, 2026a, §3.4.4).

The evidence is the postdiction literature. Two differently coloured flashes shown in sequence produce apparent motion, and observers report the colour changing abruptly midway along the path — content assigned to a moment before the second flash, which could not have been specified until the second flash had occurred (Kolers & von Grünau, 1976). Taps delivered to widely separated points on the skin are felt as a regular progression hopping along the arm, with taps at places never touched, which is why the effect goes by the name of the cutaneous rabbit (Geldard & Sherrick, 1972). In the flash-lag effect the position attributed to a flash depends on what happens in the eighty milliseconds after it (Eagleman & Sejnowski, 2000). Dennett and Kinsbourne (1992) drew the conclusion: for such revisions there is often no fact of the matter whether the content was altered before or after it "entered consciousness", because the question presupposes a finish line in the substrate at which content becomes conscious, and there is none. On the theory's reading this is expected. Each frame of the simulation assigns an order to its contents as part of constructing them, and the assignment answers to coherence and not to arrival time.

The "now" of experience is a computational construct, one frame of a recursive simulation, and it is not a physical instant; physics has no now. The flow of time as experienced is simulation output, produced by a system that assigns order to its content after the content has arrived. "Dynamics emerge as a point of view after the fact" is, at the level of felt time, literally true. What the construction does not supply is its own direction, which is inherited as above. Order is made; direction is given; and neither is a property of the substrate's clock, because the substrate has none that the observer can read.

## 11.6 Becoming is irreducibility seen from inside

If the order is constructed and the direction inherited, what is left of becoming — the sense that the future is open and the present is being produced? The cosmology paper gives an answer (Gruber, 2026b, §8.4).

The whole block — the complete history of the substrate — can satisfy a timeless condition: it is the configuration that encodes itself, a fixed point, a reading no embedded observer occupies. The same block, described from inside, is a process. The two readings do not contradict each other; they are the same condition at two levels. What makes the inside reading unavoidable for an embedded observer is irreducibility. Class 4 dynamics admit no predictive shortcut, and an observer inside such dynamics cannot obtain the next configuration except by being part of its computation. It cannot see the block, because seeing it would be a shortcut. It lives through the computation because that is the only access a subprocess has to the output of the whole. Becoming is what irreducible computation is like from a position inside it.

Barbour (1999) is the nearest neighbour to this position and differs from it at one point. Barbour removes time from fundamental physics and keeps a space of configurations, some of which — time capsules — contain records that imply a history; experienced time, for him, is the content of such records. The present account agrees that the substrate needs no clock and that the records are inside the configurations. It locates the sense of passage in the irreducibility that prevents any embedded observer from having the block as content, where Barbour locates it in the records. A record is static; a mind reading it is a computation, and a computation inside an irreducible whole proceeds step by step because no other mode is available to it. That is the difference between a timeless world with memories in it and a timeless world that is lived. Whether that is all becoming is, or whether the substrate also has a becoming that a block lacks, is the question on which the split of Chapter 14 turns, and the account here does not settle it.

## 11.7 Reversibility, probability, and the operator J

Reversibility and the conservation of probability are the two regularities of the quantum formalism most exposed to the suspicion, and the operator that encodes them together — J, with J² = −I, the real-valued name of multiplication by i — is its natural target.

Normalization does not imply reversibility. That probabilities sum to one says that something happens at every step; it is bookkeeping. A deterministic map that sends two distinct states to one output preserves total probability — all of it ends up in the output — and loses the information about which input was there. A shredder conserves paper on the same terms. Unitarity asserts much more: that the evolution is invertible, that no two states merge, that total information is conserved. Normalization alone does not give reversibility. In quantum mechanics it does so only together with linearity: a linear evolution that preserves the norm of every state of a finite-dimensional Hilbert space is unitary, and so invertible. Without that premise it fails. A decay into an environment preserves total probability and is irreversible, and in infinite dimensions a norm-preserving shift need not be invertible.

For a finite deterministic system a weaker statement does hold, and it should be given its exact scope. If the total state is finite and the rule autonomous, every trajectory is eventually periodic — there are only finitely many states, so some state recurs — and on the cycle the update is bijective: each state on it has exactly one predecessor on it. A system without beginning has been on its cycle forever. So a finite deterministic substrate is reversible on its attractor, even if its rule, written out, is many-to-one. This is a fact about the total state. It says nothing about the variables an observer inside the system can track. The coarse-grained variables of an interior observer can be irreversible — many microstates to one macrostate, information leaving the observer's description at every step — while the total state cycles reversibly underneath. Rule 30 run on a finite ring is eventually periodic and bijective on its cycle; it still looks, from inside any row, like one-way decay of structure. Reversibility of the whole and irreversibility of the observer's level coexist, and the reply that my own framework produces reversibility is true of the first and silent about the second.

The cycle constrains the arrow. A substrate that is finite and reversible, and that passes through a compressed phase, returns to it: on a bijective cycle every state recurs, the compressed one included. The perfect riffle shuffle of Chapter 12 shows the pattern on a small scale. The interior arrow therefore cannot run one way over the whole cycle. Between one visit to the compressed phase and the next, coarse-grained entropy rises as the interior decompresses; by the return it is low again, so somewhere on the cycle the direction reverses, or the trajectory passes through a transition at which it is reset. My cosmology paper's candidate for the turn is the CPT alternation of its cyclic renewal (Gruber, 2026b, §5.4): a saturated boundary decompresses into a successor whose signature under the combined inversion of charge, parity and time is flipped, so that each cycle has an arrow and consecutive arrows are not the same — a possibility the paper offers and does not derive. I do not claim that this settles what happens at the turn. What the recurrence does settle is where the question of Chapter 13 becomes unavoidable. A cycle that passes through every state on it again and again, the compressed phase and the disordered stretches alike, is a setting in which observers can arise from the disorder as fluctuations as well as from the decompression. Counting those observers across every cycle, and asking which kind is typical, is a count no inhabitant makes, and Chapter 13 puts the debt of a measure on such counts only. Held from inside, the model is tested as any reference system is, by whether what its observers find contradicts what it predicts they find, and the arrow by itself settles neither question.

J itself divides in two. In a real representation of quantum mechanics, J is the operator on pairs of real numbers that sends (u, v) to (−v, u); J² = −I, and J is multiplication by i under another name. As ontology — the claim that there is a plane in the state space of the world in which things fundamentally rotate — J can be declined, and I decline it: nothing in the formalism tells whether that rotation is a feature of the world or of the representation, which is the costume problem of Chapter 2 in the clothing of time evolution. As compression, J is data. Interference, the stable phase relations between the components of a superposition, and the proportionality of energy to frequency, E = hf, are measured facts, and J is the shortest way yet found to write them. To reject J as data is to reject the measurements. To reject J as ontology is to decline an inference the measurements do not support. Those are different acts, and only the second is open.

Stone's theorem is the result most often cited as if it derived J from first principles, and it does no such thing. The theorem (Stone, 1932) says that a strongly continuous one-parameter group of unitary operators on a Hilbert space has a unique self-adjoint generator: if U(t) is such a group, then $U(t) = e^{-iHt}$ for a self-adjoint $H$. Each clause of the hypothesis is an assumption. The group is parametrized by a real number t, assumed in advance to exist and to vary continuously; the operators are assumed unitary, which is reversibility assumed; the continuity is assumed. It does not produce time: t is its input. It does not produce reversibility: unitarity is the hypothesis. What it says is that if there is a continuous, reversible evolution labelled by a real parameter, then that evolution has the exponential form, with J, in the guise of −i, in the exponent. The theorem is conditional on exactly what was in question. Nobody who doubts that time is a continuous parameter is answered by a theorem that begins by assuming it.

Reversibility and time-reversal symmetry are different properties, and doubting one does not license doubting the other. A dynamics is **reversible** if its update is invertible: from the present state the past state can be recovered. It is **time-reversal symmetric** if the inverted dynamics has the same form as the forward one — if running the film backwards shows a process the same laws allow. A rule can be reversible without being time-symmetric; the weak interaction is the physical case, where the evolution is unitary and therefore reversible, while the processes it governs violate time-reversal symmetry, as the observed violation of CP symmetry together with the CPT theorem requires. Doubting time-reversal symmetry at the substrate is defensible. Doubting effective reversibility at our level fights the data: apparent, local reversibility at our level is a measured regularity, compressed data in the sense of Chapter 9, and what remains open is only what the substrate does, which the regularity does not determine. My cosmology paper takes a reversible substrate as a declared architectural choice; the regularity at our level is consistent with that choice and does not force it, and nothing else available at our level decides it. The CPT theorem, which is sometimes offered as a proof that the laws are reversible, is a theorem about a symmetry: it says that a Lorentz-invariant, local, unitary field theory is invariant under the combined operation of charge conjugation, parity and time reversal. Unitarity — reversibility — is among its hypotheses. It does not prove reversibility; it assumes it, and proves a symmetry.

## 11.8 Three properties no observer can check

Whether the substrate is reversible reduces to limits already reached. Reversibility cannot be read off directly: it is a property of the total state, and no part can certify its description of the total state complete (Chapter 5), and the local reversibility measured at our level is consistent with it without implying it. Nor does it follow from anything the inside can establish: reversibility on its attractor follows from determinism and finiteness together — if the substrate is deterministic and finite, every trajectory ends on a cycle and the update is bijective there — and neither of those can be settled from inside. Determinism cannot be told apart from ontic randomness by an observer that is a subprocess of what it classifies, because certifying either kind would need a claim about every program or a run of the whole with fewer resources than the whole has (Chapter 10). Finiteness cannot be told apart from infinity, because both present as quasi-infinite from inside: a finite world whose bounds recede, shield or terminate and an infinite one predict the same finite access from every point (Chapter 12). So reversibility and determinism, and the finiteness the first rests on, cannot be checked from inside. Measurement independence is the third property the cosmology paper declares: it is assumed in every Bell-type argument and certified in none, since testing it needs setting generators whose independence from the source is already known (Chapter 10).

My cosmology paper (Gruber, 2026b) keeps all three — reversibility, determinism, measurement independence — as declared architectural choices, and justifies each by what it buys. Reversibility conserves information, which the holographic bookkeeping between boundary and interior relies on. On a finite substrate it also forces recurrence: every state returns, the compressed phase included, and the paper's cyclic renewal, which it describes as a transition and not an exact return (Gruber, 2026b, §5.4), has to be squared with that. Determinism is what lets the paper ask which classes of rule could produce our data (Chapter 9). Measurement independence lets nonlocal projection pay the Bell debt once (Chapter 10). These are reasons to adopt the choices, and none of them is a finding. No observation inside the model could promote any of the three to a finding; an observation can refute the architecture only where it commits to a consequence, as the signalling fork of Chapter 10 does.

## 11.9 What time is for an embedded observer

Time, for an observer inside a computation, is four things, and none of them is a clock. It is the update, the generating relation the observer never has complete as content and has only as order. It is a direction inherited from the coarse-grained layer in which the observer's records are made. It is an order the observer constructs, frame by frame, to make its contents cohere. And it is a becoming that is irreducibility felt from inside: the step-by-step access that is the only access a part has to a whole that admits no shortcut. What the substrate does — whether its update is reversible, whether it has a clock at all, whether the block is static — is settled by none of them, and its reversibility by nothing an observer inside can check. A creature whose entire world is laid out, step by step, on a storage medium has all four as structure; whether it has them from inside is the split Chapter 14 takes up.
