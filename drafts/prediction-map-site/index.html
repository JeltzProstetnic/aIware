FMT Prediction Map

The Four-Model Theory of consciousness as a network of claims and predictions, compared with five other theories. Map v1, built from FMT v19, 2026-10.

This map sets out the Four-Model Theory of consciousness (FMT) as a network of claims. Each box is one claim. Each arrow says that the claim it points to holds in virtue of the claim it starts from. The three principles of the theory form the core, in the left column. Every column to the right specifies the one before it, and the last two columns hold the theory's empirical predictions: neuroscientific ones at level 4, cognitive and behavioural ones at level 5. Select a box or an arrow to read it.

The page also carries the maps that Negro, Elbaz, Fischer, Schreiber and Mudrik (2026) drew for four other theories: the Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (GNWT), Integrated Information Theory (IIT), Perceptual Reality Monitoring (PRM) and Recurrent Processing Theory (RPT), and a map of Predictive Processing (PP) that we drew ourselves from its proponents' publications, with the same method. About the format

The format is that of the prediction maps of Negro, Elbaz, Fischer, Schreiber and Mudrik (2026), who mapped the major theories of consciousness as claim networks and measured which claims carry the most weight in each. FMT's principles describe a computational substrate, not the brain, so the map uses the layout they give for substrate-general theories such as Integrated Information Theory: levels 1 and 2 hold mostly substrate-general claims, level 3 brain-specific claims and extrapolations to animals and machines, level 4 neuroscientific predictions and level 5 cognitive predictions. Their maps of GNWT, PRM and RPT use the layout for cognitive theories, in which level 3 holds cognitive predictions, level 4 the neural implementation and level 5 neuroscientific predictions.

How to read it

  • Colour gives the claim class: cyan for claims about cognitive functions, green for claims about a physical substrate in general, pink for claims about the brain, yellow for claims about consciousness beyond healthy adult humans. A thick border marks the core. On the FMT map a dashed purple border marks a proposed prediction (E15): added in October 2026 for binding (C9), on the map only and not in the FMT paper. The maps of the other theories keep the colours of the authors' files, except GNWT, whose file has none: there the colours are transcribed from the preprint's Figure 3.
  • Solid arrows join claims at different levels; dashed arrows join claims at the same level.
  • Select a box to read the full claim and its centrality. On the FMT map the panel also gives the section of the FMT paper the claim comes from, its status (derived, corroborated, untested, proposed and so on), whether other theories make the same prediction, and its references. Select an arrow to read why the one claim follows from the other.
  • The measure selector sizes the boxes by one of four centrality measures. They are computed in this page from the arrows alone, with the definitions Negro et al. use:
    • LRC (local reaching centrality): the share of the other claims that depend on this one, directly or through a chain of arrows.
    • HC (harmonic centrality): the same count, with each dependent claim weighted by 1/distance.
    • BC (betweenness centrality): the share of shortest paths between other claims that pass through this one.
    • PR (PageRank, damping 0.85): weight inherited from the claims above. Negro et al. use it to find high-stakes predictions, because a failed prediction with high PageRank reflects on influential claims upstream.

What the measures show

  • Reach. After the core, the claim with the widest reach is B1, the architecture of two kinds of model (world and self) on two levels (stored and generated): 77% of the other claims depend on it. Next come B2, the split between stored implicit models and generated explicit ones (46%), and B4, self-referential closure (41%). The architecture claim reaches further than closure does.
  • Bridges. B2 has the highest betweenness (0.016). It connects the principles to the cluster of anaesthesia and working-memory predictions and to the cluster about how permeable the explicit models are to the implicit ones. Evidence against the implicit/explicit dissociation (E4, E6, F4) would therefore cut more paths through the theory than evidence against any other single claim.
  • Highest PageRank: E3, lucid-dream onset (0.031). E3 predicts that verified onset of lucidity shows phase-transition dynamics that begin in self-model networks. It ranks first because it rests on D7 (REM sleep approaches criticality; lucid onset is a crossing), which has three parents, one of them C12, the criticality signature. A null result on lucid onset refutes the criticality signature (C12 and D7), not the theory. In FMT, near-criticality is a signature that free modelling usually leaves in neural tissue, neither necessary nor sufficient for consciousness, so criticality data cannot disconfirm the core principle A1 either way. PageRank counts parents and cannot represent that distinction.
  • Next predictions by PageRank (D7, an intermediate claim, sits between them at 0.025): F2, the qualia-structure dissociation (0.028); F1, the content of psychedelic ego dissolution (0.025); E10, the white-box trace (0.025); E2, the gradient between alters in dissociative identity disorder (0.024).
  • A low rank that does not mean low stakes. F6, the novel-input probe, tests principle A1 directly, yet ranks 46th of 62 by PageRank. A1 is a root, so it passes on only the base PageRank share, split over its five children.
  • Claims with no empirical consequence on the map. C6 (substrate independence) leads only to extrapolations to machines and animals (D8, D9, D11), from which no prediction follows. B3, virtual qualia, reaches a single prediction (F2, through C10). Negro et al. report the same pattern in the map of Integrated Information Theory.

Which claims count as predictions
The level of a claim decides it, as in Negro et al. (preprint p. 7): levels 4 and 5 in the layout for substrate-general theories (FMT, IIT), levels 3 and 5 in the layout for cognitive theories (GNWT, PRM, RPT). Every other claim, the core included, is a theoretical claim. Arrows point from a claim to the claim that holds in virtue of it, so the claims above a prediction are the ones a failed prediction puts at risk. One rule overrides the levels in every map: a claim of the extrapolation class (alone or in a two-class box) counts as neither theoretical claim nor prediction and is listed in the last column as one of the extrapolations (excluded), because extrapolations to animals or machines are neither part of the theory's derivation base nor currently testable predictions in either layout. Without the rule the layouts would treat them unequally: FMT's extrapolations sit at level 3, which its layout counts as theory, while each other map's level-3 extrapolation would count as a prediction. Arrows still run through an excluded claim when the measures follow a chain. Scope still counts the extrapolation class.
Reach
The share of theoretical claims from which a chain of arrows reaches at least one prediction; the others are listed as idle claims. A claim with no route to a prediction cannot be tested through the map; these are the claims Negro et al. call "not particularly productive ... in terms of empirical consequences" (pp. 18–19).
Fruitfulness
The number of distinct predictions reachable from a theoretical claim, as mean / median over all theoretical claims, and averaged over the core claims (FMT has three, the other maps one). Kuhn counted fruitfulness, a theory's yield of new consequences, among the criteria for choosing between theories; Negro et al. read their maps for "which claims yield more downstream claims" (p. 27).
Exposure
For each prediction, the share of the map's theoretical claims among the claims it holds in virtue of, directly or through a chain, given as mean / median over the predictions; and the share of predictions that rest on a core claim. Popper made exposure to refutation the mark of an empirical theory. Lakatos described how auxiliary claims absorb a refutation and shield the core (preprint p. 3); exposure counts how much of the theory stands behind each test.
Robustness
The share of predictions with two or more parent claims, and the share reached from the core along two or more paths that share no arrow (counted as a maximum flow). Negro et al. note whether a claim is "supported by a single or multiple 'parent' claims" (p. 27). A prediction derived along two independent routes does not hang on a single auxiliary claim, so a failed test is harder to blame on that one claim alone.
Scope
The share of substrate-general claims, and of cognitive / brain-specific / extrapolation claims, among the claims outside the core, in the four classes of Negro et al. (p. 7). A claim drawn in two classes (their "hybrid claims", p. 24) counts half in each; the core is left out because the authors' files draw it grey, without a class. Kuhn's scope is the range of phenomena a theory speaks to; the substrate-general share says how much of a theory is stated without assuming a brain.
Experimental record in the ConTraSt database

ConTraSt (Yaron, Melloni, Pitts & Mudrik, 2022, Nature Human Behaviour 6, 593–604, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-021-01284-5) collects experiments whose authors interpreted their findings in light of a theory of consciousness, and records whether they read the findings as supporting or challenging it. ConTraSt groups the theories into four families. Counts of experiments per family, read from the database's public interface at https://contrastdb.tau.ac.il/, retrieved 2026-10-19 (the 2022 paper covered 412 experiments; the database has grown since):

ConTraSt does not list PRM or RPT separately: PRM belongs to the higher-order family (Negro et al., preprint p. 5), RPT to the first-order family, so their rows count every theory in the family. An experiment interpreted in light of several theories counts once for each. The counts record how authors read their own results, most of them post hoc rather than as tests of critical predictions (Yaron et al., 2022), and they are not weighted by how central the tested prediction is to the theory.
