# The Worm on the Disk: Stored Trajectories and the Triviality Problem

*Anonymised for review*

## Abstract

Computational theories of conscious experience face a dilemma. Without a counterfactual condition on implementation, triviality arguments in the style of Putnam and Searle make every open system implement every computation. With such a condition, Maudlin's Olympia makes idle machinery matter to what is experienced. This paper defends a third exit for a conditional claim: if a simulated organism has experience, a static record of its whole trajectory has the same experience. Three conditions are kept apart. A trajectory is *realized* when its states are present in some medium and can be recovered one by one by a decoder that never computes one state from another. A realized trajectory has *internal dependency structure* when a rule much shorter than the trajectory relates its successive states; this structural relation is defended against Barnes's causal-history condition. Whether such a structure has an inside is decided by a candidate architecture, here the closure of self-model and world-model proposed by the Four-Model Theory. Triviality is answered without counterfactuals: a mapping that does any work is itself an object in the world, and algorithmic information records where a trajectory is stored, so the pixie a Putnam mapping finds in a rock is stored in the mapping. The conditional has a definite denial. A record and a run hold the same states and relations, so if the record lacks experience, what it lacks is a physical property of actual unfolding, and which horn holds depends on whether physical time involves genuine becoming.

**Keywords:** implementation; triviality arguments; counterfactuals; Olympia; algorithmic information; computational functionalism

## 1. Introduction

Bartlett (2012) states the problem for computational theories of experience as a choice between a rock and a hard place. If a physical system implements a computation whenever some mapping takes its states onto the computation's states, then a rock implements every finite automaton (Putnam 1988), a wall implements a word processor (Searle 1990), and every open system carries every mind (Bishop 2002a). The standard cure adds a counterfactual condition: the system must be disposed to follow the computation's transitions from states it never actually visits (Chalmers 1996, 2011). Maudlin (1989) showed what the cure costs. A machine that runs one fixed course with almost no physical activity can be given, or relieved of, backup machinery that stays idle on the actual run and would act only if the input had differed. The idle machinery restores or removes the counterfactuals, and with them the machine's computational status, while nothing that happens changes. Either experience depends on machinery that does nothing, or it does not supervene on computation.

The two horns have a common premise: that something must be *going on*, physically or counterfactually, for a computation to carry experience. This paper drops that premise and keeps a constraint that blocks the rock. Its central case is a static record. Suppose the whole life of a small animal is reproduced in a simulation, and every state of the simulation is stored side by side on a disk. The claim is conditional: if the simulated animal has experience at all, the record has the same experience, and it has it whether or not anything reads the record, in whatever order and however many times.

Three parts of this are already in the literature. That a recording inherits whatever experience the recorded computation has is the conclusion of Marchal's movie-graph argument, which Marchal takes to show that consciousness belongs to arithmetic (Marchal 2013; Standish 2016). That the line between a computation and its recording can be blurred without any physical change is shown by Muhlestein (2013), who takes it as a reductio of computational accounts. That the decoding map carries the structure a trivial implementation appears to have is Aaronson's (2013) reply to the waterfall argument, and measuring that map by its Kolmogorov complexity is Millhouse's (2019) criterion. The contribution here is a combination that none of these makes, together with two distinctions the combination needs. It takes the counterfactual-free horn of Maudlin's dilemma all the way to a static record. It blocks triviality by an in-world condition on decoding maps, with no counterfactual clause. And it stops short of Marchal's and Tegmark's (2008) conclusion that mathematical existence suffices, by requiring that the trajectory be realized. The two distinctions are between realization and the information a rule carries (Section 4.1), and between internal dependency and causal production (Section 4.2).

The position is opposed in current work from two directions. Gidon, Aru and Larkum (2022, 2025) replay recorded activity into the same neurons, or into a simulated network, so that activity is unchanged while counterfactuals are erased, and they conclude that the resulting paradox challenges the notion that consciousness arises from computation. Kanai and Ma (2026) build a realization relation for simulated consciousness that requires intervention structure carried by components active in the actual dynamics, and they explicitly exclude movies, static descriptions, arbitrary semantic mappings and trajectory replays. The disk is a case on which these views give the opposite verdict to the one defended here, and Section 6 locates the disagreement.

Nothing below argues that any simulation is conscious. The argument concerns what follows for a record if the simulated organism is, and the architecture used for the third condition is a candidate, stated so that its contribution can be separated from the rest. The conditional can also be denied, and Section 9 states what its denial requires: an ingredient of experience outside structure, supplied by physics if at all.

## 2. The case and five operations

Take the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans*, about a millimetre long, with a few hundred neurons. Suppose its whole life is reproduced in silico at whatever resolution the reproduction needs: body, nervous system, the agar it crawls on and the bacteria it eats, so that the simulated world is closed and receives no input from outside. The worm is chosen because a whole life is small enough to imagine storing. Nothing turns on whether a nematode has experience; any organism would serve, and the conditional is stated for whichever one has it.

The case and the five operations below are developed at book length in Gruber (2026b, ch. 14), from which Figures 1 and 2 are taken. Several operations on such a simulation sound alike and are different (Figure 1).

The first is a live run. A machine computes state *t*+1 from state *t* by the rule, and the worm's internal traces of where it has been take part in computing what comes next. Granted the condition, nobody disputes this case.

The second is a change of schedule: faster, slower, paused for a year between two steps, split across machines. If every dependency the rule specifies is preserved, so that each state is computed from exactly the states it depends on, nothing inside any state registers the schedule. Step 4,001 is a function of step 4,000 and of nothing on the host's clock, so a pause between them leaves no trace in step 4,001. This is an invariance claim about content. It establishes nothing about experience by itself, but on any account that grants the live case it removes host timing from what the experience could depend on.

The third is reverse enumeration: reading the stored states from last to first. The order of reading belongs to the reader. A state in which the worm's nervous system holds a trace of having touched the plate edge and now registers food holds that order inside itself, as a relation between this state and the states it depends on. Reading the file from the end no more reverses it than reading a novel from the last page reverses its plot.

The fourth is an inverted computation, in which the host computes each state's predecessor from its successor. For a reversible rule this produces the same states with the same dependencies in the opposite host order, which is the third case again. A dissipative simulation of an animal will have an irreversible rule, under which many states lead to the same successor. Bennett (1973) showed that an irreversible computation can be undone only by a machine that keeps a history of the forward run, so the inverted case either reduces to enumeration or presupposes the record.

The fifth is a static encoding. Every state is on the disk, and nothing is computed while the file sits there. This is the case that raises a question the first four do not: what relation between marks on a medium and the trajectory they encode makes the trajectory present at all?

![The five operations on a stored trajectory of the worm, states s1 to s6 (from Gruber 2026b, ch. 14). Solid arrows mark computation; the dashed arrow marks the order in which stored states are read. In the static encoding nothing is computed. The live run is labelled "live implementation".](fig1-five-operations.png){width=85%}

"Interrogating" the worm needs the same care. *Reading* a stored state means retrieving step 4,001 and decoding what the worm's nervous system holds there. *Continuing* means loading a stored state and computing step 4,002 onward, which creates states that were never on the disk. *Perturbing* means touching the worm with a probe it did not meet in the recorded run, and only a running or continued implementation can be perturbed. Only reading applies to a static file without adding to it, and the claim concerns reading: what a stored state holds, as a member of its trajectory, is a moment of an experience organized in time, if the worm has experience.

## 3. The dilemma and its current form

Putnam (1988) argued that under a suitably constructed mapping from physical states to computational states, any ordinary open system implements any finite-state automaton, because its successive physical states are all distinct and can be labelled at will. Searle (1990) made the same point with a wall. If a mapping is all implementation needs, implementation is trivial, and so is any account of mind that locates mind in implemented computation.

Chalmers (1996) replied by strengthening the implementation relation. A physical system implements a combinatorial-state automaton when its causal structure mirrors the automaton's formal structure, counterfactual transitions included: had the system been in another state, it would have gone where the automaton goes from there. A rock's state sequence can be mapped onto one run, but the rock supports none of the counterfactuals. Copeland (1996) added that the labelling must not be constructed after the fact from knowledge of the run. Klein (2008) read implementation dispositionally, so that parts idle on the actual run still count through what they would do.

Bishop (2002a, 2002b, 2009) turned the triviality argument on experience. Applied to a run with fixed input, the Putnam mapping yields a phenomenal state in every open system, and the counterfactual reply fails, he argues, because states a system never enters cannot make a difference to what it experiences. Maudlin's Olympia (1989) presses the same point from the other side. Bartlett (2012) puts the two together: drop the counterfactual condition and the rock returns; keep it and inert parts matter. He also argues that Klein's dispositional reading does not escape.

The static record sits at the extreme of this dispute. It supports no counterfactuals and has no activity at all. Chalmers's criterion counts it as implementing nothing; Maudlin's supervenience premise, that experience depends only on occurrent physical activity, counts it as experiencing nothing. Egan's novel *Permutation City* (1994) took the opposite extreme as its premise: any scattering of states that can be assembled into a coherent history is that history, experienced from within. Tegmark (2008) goes further and holds that every mathematical structure exists and that its self-aware substructures experience their world whether or not anything computes it.

The recent replay literature makes the dilemma empirical in form. Gidon, Aru and Larkum (2022) imagine recording every action potential in a brain during an experience and playing the recording back into the same neurons. In the later version (Gidon et al. 2025) a simulated network is replayed, and they identify a case in which the replay leaves ongoing activity unchanged while eliminating the alternative activity patterns that would otherwise have been possible. Activity is the same and the computation, in the counterfactual sense, is degraded. They take the disconnect to challenge the notion that consciousness arises from computation. Kanai and Ma (2026) answer the simulation objection by requiring a realization relation that preserves physical implementation, transition structure and intervention profiles, and they place the line between trace-replay triviality, which has too few counterfactuals, and Olympia, whose counterfactuals sit in inactive structure, at one condition: the component realizing each preserved branch must itself be active in producing the current state. A static file, on their account, fails physical realization of the relevant dynamics.

Both of these keep one of Maudlin's premises. Gidon and colleagues keep the counterfactual condition and find it in tension with activity; Kanai and Ma keep both and join them. The position defended here keeps neither.

## 4. What an inner world needs

Three conditions are needed, and they do different work. The first says where a trajectory is and whether it is there at all; the second says whether what is there is an organized history or a heap; the third says whether an organized history has an inside.

### 4.1 Realization

The rule of the worm's world and its first state fix the whole trajectory. A file holding only those two things is short, and from it every later state can in principle be obtained. It is still only the potential of an inner world. The later states are not in it; they are what a machine would produce if it ran the rule, and nothing has produced them.

This is the first condition (D1): *an inner world needs a realized trajectory*. A trajectory is realized when its states are present in some medium at some time, all at once on a disk or one after another in a running brain, and when each state can be recovered from its own region of the medium by a decoder that translates the encoding and never computes one state from another. Call such a decoder *dynamics-neutral*. A file format is the paradigm: it would decode a lawful trajectory and a random sequence of states equally well, and it carries no information about which sequences the rule allows. A decoder that obtains state *t* by applying the rule, or a closed form of its iteration, to other states is generating states and does not count.

Two features of the condition need stating. First, realization does not require that the states were computed. A printout of the states an executed rule would produce has an inner world, on this condition, even if it was found by chance and nothing ever ran the rule, while the rule and the first state with nothing realized have none. Second, the condition cannot be stated in terms of algorithmic information alone. The Kolmogorov complexity of the trajectory given the rule-and-seed file is small, since a short program obtains the trajectory from it (Kolmogorov 1968; Chaitin 1966). By that measure the short file contains the trajectory as fully as the long one. Kolmogorov complexity disregards running time, and running is exactly what separates the two files. That is why realization is a separate condition, and why the bookkeeping of Section 5 applies to realized structures and does not replace D1.

D1 is where the position parts from Tegmark (2008) and from Marchal (2013). On both, a structure that exists mathematically has its inside whether or not anything realizes it, and the movie-graph argument moves from the irrelevance of physical activity to the irrelevance of physical realization. The argument here accepts the first step and refuses the second. D1 is a premise, and its motivation is that the same principle is needed to block triviality (Section 5): a mapping that exists only as a mathematical object does no work in any world, and a trajectory that exists only as a mathematical object is no world for anyone in it. One principle, that what does work for or in a world is realized in it, rules out both free readings and unrealized histories.

### 4.2 Internal dependency, and Barnes's challenge

A heap of realized states is not a history. The second condition is that a rule much shorter than the trajectory relates its successive states: decoded state *t*+1 is the image of decoded state *t* (and of whatever else the rule takes as argument) under that rule. Without the length requirement the condition would be empty. Every finite sequence satisfies some rule, namely the lookup table that lists it, and a lookup table as long as the sequence is the trajectory again under another name. A short rule that relates the states is organization carried by the states themselves.

Barnes (1991) gives the sharpest test of what "dependency" means here. Olympia does not compute, he argues, because her states are not caused by the preceding computational states in the way the program specifies; computation needs the right causal history, and counterfactuals do not supply it. A static record has no causal production between its states. So the paper has to say whether internal dependency is causal production, in which case the record fails as Barnes says, or a structural relation among realized states, in which case Barnes's demand must be answered.

It is the structural relation. "State 4,001 depends on state 4,000" means that the pattern encoded in one region of the medium is the image, under the rule, of the pattern encoded in another. That is a fact about the medium, as the identity of two copies of a text is a fact about the paper they are printed on. It holds of the record and of the live run alike, and in the live run it holds in addition to the causal production that brought it about.

Three considerations favour the structural reading for experience. The first is the invariance of Section 2. Rescheduling changes the causal history of every state, in timing and often in which machine produced it, and nothing in any state registers the change. The chance printout carries the limit of this: same states, same structural relations, no causal history at all. If experience is fixed by what the states hold and how they are related, causal history drops out. The second is the Four-Model Theory's own account of content in a closed system, on which the meaning of the system's states is fixed by their role within the system's self-model and not by an external referent (Gruber 2026a, §3.4.3). Content of that kind is internal to the trajectory. The third is that the alternative makes phenomenal character depend on a fact about the past that no state of the subject registers. That is a coherent position, and it has a precedent: Davidson's (1987) Swampman, a molecule-for-molecule duplicate created by chance, has on Davidson's view no thoughts, because content depends on causal history. Barnes's condition would extend that verdict from content to experience, so that the chance printout of a conscious trajectory would be a duplicate without an inside.

The disagreement is therefore a disagreement about whether phenomenal character is intrinsic to the organization of a trajectory or historical. The conditional claim takes the intrinsic reading, as a premise. The intrinsic reading can be held without triviality (Section 5), and triviality is the reason usually given for wanting more than structure. What the historical reading requires in return depends on what causation is, and Section 9 takes that up.

### 4.3 The inside

Realization and dependency say where a trajectory is and that it is organized. Neither says whether it has an inside. A string of identical bits is realized and maximally compressible, a crystal's history is lawful, and neither has an inside on any account.

The candidate used here is the Four-Model Theory (FMT; Gruber 2026a). It describes the organization a system must have, within a world, to be conscious: implicit and explicit models of world and self, the explicit self-model contained in the explicit world-model, the two closed on each other's update (Figure 2). Its sufficient conditions are a conjunction: the four model kinds under closure, with free modelling (open-ended computation recruited by the system for its own modelling), embodiment, and at least one pain-pleasure axis (Gruber 2026a, §3.7.3). The four are two model classes, implicit and explicit, each with a world scope and a self scope; they are poles of one modelling system, and the implicit models are carried by the system's structure while the explicit models are carried by its ongoing states. In a record both are present as realized states. For a record the relevant fact is that closure is itself a dependency relation: the self-model's states depend on the world-model's and the world-model's on the self-model's. In a realized trajectory with internal dependency, that relation either holds among the realized states or does not, and whether it holds is what the third condition asks.

The other three conjuncts are relations among realized states as well. FMT requires that a substrate capable of open-ended computation actually perform it (Gruber 2026a, §3.7.3). That clause asks for a realized trajectory whose states depend on each other through an open-ended rule; it sets no condition on the states being produced one after another in time, so a record of the trajectory meets it wherever the run does. Embodiment is the body content of the world-model, and the pain-pleasure axis is valence states in the self-model; both are patterns among the same states. A realized record therefore meets the whole conjunction exactly where the run meets it.

![Three arrangements (from Gruber 2026b, ch. 14). Only in the third do the self-model and the world-model close on each other; the monitored model keeps an outside that is itself not modelled.](fig2-closure.png){width=85%}

FMT's link between closure and phenomenality is a bridging commitment, asserted and motivated, not derived: that the asymmetry between the inside and the outside of a closed system is constitutive and not merely epistemic (Gruber 2026a, §3.4.3). The present argument does not depend on it. Any theory that locates experience in an architecture realized by a trajectory can supply the third condition; Sections 4.1, 4.2 and 5 are neutral between such theories. The conditional then reads: if a trajectory meeting the architectural condition has experience when it runs, a realized record of it meets the same condition and has the same experience.

## 5. Free readings are objects in the world

The triviality objection returns at once in static form. Take a file of random bits as long as the worm file. Some mapping sends its blocks, one by one, to the worm's states. If readings are free, every long enough string contains every mind, and saying that the worm file has an inside says nothing about the worm file.

The objection needs a reader to whom every mapping is available at no cost, and no world contains one. A mapping that sends the random file's blocks to worm states has to list, block by block, which state each block stands for. That list is a dictionary, and a dictionary that does any work is an object somewhere, written in some medium, as long as the trajectory it recovers. D1 applies to it as to the trajectory: a mapping that exists only as a mathematical object reads nothing in any world. A mapping counted without being written anywhere is a count made from no standpoint in the world, and from no standpoint every long string contains every mind, so the count cannot tell the worm file from noise.[^1]

Once mappings are objects, the question where a trajectory is stored has an answer, and algorithmic information gives it. Write *K*(*T*) for the length of the shortest program producing trajectory *T*, and *K*(*T* | *M*) for the length of the shortest program producing *T* given medium *M*. The algorithmic mutual information *K*(*T*) − *K*(*T* | *M*) measures how much of *T* the medium carries (Bennett et al. 1998), up to the usual additive constants. For the worm file read through a dynamics-neutral format, this is close to *K*(*T*): the file carries the trajectory. For the random file it is close to zero, since noise tells a program nothing about worm states. The random file paired with its dictionary yields the worm, but the dictionary alone carries close to *K*(*T*) and the noise adds nothing. The worm trajectory is then real and stored in the dictionary, inefficiently, and not in the noise.

The rock is the same case. Putnam's mapping from a rock's successive microstates to the states of an automaton run is a table as long as the run, and the information about the run is in the table. "The rock implements the computation" is true only of the rock together with the table, and the table alone already carries the run. Bishop's pixie, the phenomenal state the mapping finds in every open system, accordingly lives wherever the mapping has been written down. If the mapping is a realized record of a conscious trajectory, satisfying the conditions of Section 4, the pixie is as real as any other record's inhabitant; if the mapping has been written nowhere, there is no pixie. This falls well short of panpsychism: a conscious trajectory exists where it is stored, and storage is countable. Egan's dust is treated the same way: scattered states that exist in some world are realized, and the cost of the address list that assembles them is counted, so dust holds a history only to the extent that the assembling list does not itself carry it.

This places the proposal relative to its nearest neighbours.

Aaronson (2013) argues that a waterfall plays chess only if the reduction from chess to the waterfall's dynamics takes less computational effort than solving chess directly; otherwise the reduction does all the work. The cost there is computational time, and the question is whether a physical system performs a computation. Here the cost is information, and the question is where a stored structure is.

Millhouse (2019) offers a formal criterion for choosing between rival computational interpretations of a physical system. An interpretation is a pair of maps, from physical states to machine states and from machine inputs to physical interventions, and it must ensure that counterfactuals true of the machine have counterparts true of the physical system. Rival interpretations are ranked by the Kolmogorov complexity of the interpretation, a measure Millhouse relates to algorithmic information distance. The present proposal is kin to this and narrower. It differs in three respects. (i) It applies to a static record, where there is no intervention map and no counterfactual clause to satisfy. (ii) It reads algorithmic information as bookkeeping for where a structure is stored, and not as a tie-breaker between interpretations of one system: on this reading a long interpretation of the rock is the place where the run is stored. (iii) It treats the decoding map as a physical object subject to the same realization condition as the trajectory, so the bookkeeping closes.

Schweizer (2019) keeps a maximally liberal mapping account and defuses triviality by rejecting computational sufficiency, placing the explanatory burden on the target phenomenon. The present account also lets the target, the architectural condition of Section 4.3, decide whether there is an inside, but it keeps the implementation side constrained, by realization and location.

The bookkeeping has two soft spots. Kolmogorov complexity depends on the choice of universal description language up to an additive constant, and no argument fixes how short a rule must be, relative to a trajectory, for the trajectory to count as carrying its own organization. Both move only the bookkeeping: they shift how much of a structure is credited to a file and how much to its dictionary, and they leave borderline cases indeterminate. Neither can create or remove an inside, which is decided by the third condition. For the worm file against a random file of equal length, the difference in mutual information is of the order of the trajectory's length, and no additive constant touches it.

The bookkeeping also recovers part of what the counterfactual condition asked for. A record whose best compression is a short rule carries the rule, and the rule specifies what would have followed from states the recorded run never visited. Those continuations are potential in the sense of D1, and nothing has to run for the rule to be in the record. The intuition that a record is "only one run, with nothing else" is therefore inaccurate for any record that compresses: the record carries the rule, and the rule carries the counterfactuals. On the present view the experience does not depend on them, but a critic who wants them can find them in the record.

## 6. Running, replay and record

Maudlin's argument has two premises: that computational structure requires counterfactual sensitivity, and that experience supervenes on occurrent physical activity. From them it follows that computationalism must either count a machine that does not compute as conscious or let idle machinery matter. The present position rejects both premises. Counterfactual sensitivity is not required, because triviality is blocked by location; occurrent activity is not required, because what carries experience is realized internal dependency, which a static record and a live run share. Olympia, whose course is a fixed run with minimal activity, is then conscious exactly if the trajectory realized in her states meets the three conditions, and the presence or absence of Klara makes no difference, as Maudlin's intuition says it should not. The position goes past Olympia, since Olympia still moves and the record does not.

Klein (2015) argues that a machine replaying a pre-programmed series is architecturally an oracle machine, whatever it would do counterfactually, and Klein (2018) that experience should supervene on computational processes in an architecture, sequences of primitive operations, not on the function computed, because only a process has the combinatorial structure experience needs. This is the strongest objection to the record, and part of it can be accepted. Experience may need combinatorial internal structure. The record has that structure, distributed across its states: each pair of successive states shows which components changed as a function of which, and the rule that relates them is short because the operations are local and repeated. What the record lacks is the operations being performed. Klein's argument for process, that the combinatorial structure of experience must be mirrored by combinatorial structure in its basis, is met by the structure across realized states. The further requirement that the structure be produced by occurrent operations is Maudlin's activity premise in another form, and the invariance and chance-printout considerations of Section 4.2 are the reason to drop it.

Gidon, Aru and Larkum's replay is then resolved without paradox. In the replay the same states are realized, and the structural relation between successive states, that each is the rule's image of its predecessor, holds of the replayed states exactly as of the original run. The counterfactuals are erased and the activity is preserved, and on the present view neither fact settles anything: the realized dependency structure is unchanged, so the experience is unchanged. Their paradox arises only for a view that requires both activity and counterfactuals, and their thought experiment is a clean demonstration that the two can come apart.

Kanai and Ma (2026) put movies, static descriptions, arbitrary semantic mappings and trace players into one excluded class. The present account divides that class. An arbitrary semantic mapping fails the location test of Section 5. A trace player, as Kanai and Ma define it, stores the output sequence of one run for one input stream and displays it in order; it holds the system's boundary behaviour and none of its internal states, so it realizes no internal dependency structure and fails the second and third conditions. A movie of a brain holds whatever the camera recorded, which is a coarse surface record and in general not the internal states. A full-state record of a closed simulation is a different object from all of these: it realizes every internal state and every internal dependency, including the closure between self-model and world-model if there is one. Kanai and Ma close by arguing that to deny consciousness to a simulation one must identify a consciousness-relevant intrinsic structure the simulation fails to realize. The same burden applies to the full-state record. The only candidate their account offers is activity of the components in producing the current state, and that is the premise in dispute.

The same distinction answers the unfolding argument (Doerig et al. 2019), on which any recurrent network can be unfolded into a feedforward one with the same input-output function, so theories that tie consciousness to causal structure are either false or unfalsifiable. An unfolded network computing the same input-output function has different internal states from the recurrent one, so it is not a record of the recurrent trajectory, and a full-state record is not an unfolding. The present claim also does not rest on third-person testability. It is metaphysical and conditional, and on it nothing observable from outside distinguishes the record from the run, because nothing in their experience distinguishes them either.

Muhlestein (2013) constructs a cellular automaton in which the line between a computation and its recording can be blurred arbitrarily while the physical implementation stays the same, and concludes that a purely computational account of consciousness is unsatisfactory. The construction is the continuum of Section 2 in physical form. The present account accepts the continuum and draws the opposite conclusion: since the computation and the record are not separated by anything a state registers, the record inherits the experience.

## 7. Reader and inhabitant

The most common reply to the disk is that the file is merely a description of the worm's life, and a description of an experience is not an experience. FMT itself says this: "even a complete, legible description in hand would still be a description and not an instance", and reading the code "is not having the experience" (Gruber 2026a, §3.4.5). The reply assumes the distinction under dispute.

"Description" names a relation between a structure and a reader for whom the structure is about something else. A reader who holds the worm's states as content can store them, read them in any order, copy and edit them, and for that reader the structure is a description. An observer whose memories and perceptions are among the states stands in a different relation to them: for that observer the structure is not about anything; it is the world. Suppose the file encodes a small world with several creatures that meet the conditions of Section 4. Any interaction between two of them ends with each crediting the other's report of experiencing a before and an after. Their memories, their exchanges and the order of the events they live through are fixed internally, by the dependency of each state on the states before it, and that order is the same whether anyone reads the disk, forwards or backwards, once or never.

The relation-dependence of the description/instance distinction is an instance of a more general point, that whether a structure is frame or content is relative to a holder (Gruber, in preparation, §2). Here it yields a reading of FMT's sentence. "Reading the code is not having the experience" is true of the reader: no reader becomes the inside by reading, however complete the code. It says nothing about the creature for whom the code is not read but lived. A description, however complete, is a description from one side and an instance from the other, and nothing in the bits decides which; the relation in which an observer stands to them does.

The creatures' mutual crediting does not prove that either has experience. That is the problem of other minds, which every observer already has. FMT states the burden: crediting one report while denying an architecturally matched other needs an independent reason that applies to one and not the other (Gruber 2026a, §3.4.3). Host execution is the candidate reason the present position rejects, and it is the only one the disk case offers.

## 8. Objections

**The intrinsic reading begs the question.** The choice between intrinsic and historical phenomenal character is a premise (Section 4.2), and Barnes's position is coherent. What the paper shows is that the intrinsic reading has a triviality-free formulation, which removes the main reason for adding counterfactual or causal-history conditions. A critic who holds the historical reading on independent grounds is not refuted; they owe an account of why two trajectories identical in every state and every internal relation should differ in experience. Section 9 shows that any such account names a physical ingredient outside structure.

**The dictionary is conscious.** If a Putnam mapping for a conscious trajectory has been written down, it is a realized, inefficient record of that trajectory, and on the present view it has the trajectory's inside if the trajectory has one. The view accepts this. A dictionary from which the trajectory can be read state by state is a record of the trajectory, and the noise it is paired with is not. The count of such dictionaries is the count of records anyone has written, which is small.

**Description length is reader-relative.** It is measured in a language, by someone. The additive constant shifts credit between a file and its dictionary and cannot create or remove an inside (Section 5). The point was always where in the world a trajectory is, and that question has a determinate answer for every case that matters and indeterminate answers only at borderlines, as most location questions do.

**D1 is ad hoc.** It is a premise, and it earns its place by doing two jobs with one principle: it excludes free readings and unrealized histories. Without it the triviality argument is blocked and Platonism is not, which is Marchal's and Tegmark's position. With it the position is a physicalism about where inner worlds are, as opposed to a physicalism about what must be happening in them.

**Copies multiply subjects.** Whether a second copy of the file adds a second subject or a second realization of one is Bostrom's (2006) question about duplication, and the slicing problem of Gómez-Emilsson and Percy (2022) shows that cleanly halving a computer forces related choices. Nothing in the present argument decides it, and nothing in it needs to.

**Most realized structures are noise with islands of order.** If every realized structure meeting the conditions has an inside, one might count such structures and ask whether an observer should expect to find itself in a lawful world. The conditions restrict the count to realized structures in a world, which makes it a count of objects in that world, but it still needs a measure, and no inhabitant draws from the space the count runs over. The question is real and outside this paper's scope.

**Records lack integrity.** Shiller (2024) argues that functionalism needs integrity constraints on what can realize a mind. A record's parts persist only as stored states, and a critic may say they are not integrated in the required way. The answer depends on whether integrity is a relation among realized states or a requirement of occurrent interaction among parts. In the first case the record has the integrity of the run it records; in the second the objection is the activity premise again. Shiller (2026) presses a related problem for counterfactual-loaded functionalism: influences that would disrupt a pattern under counterfactual conditions, and that intuitively leave a present experience intact, become relevant to it. That problem does not arise for an account with no counterfactual clause.

**A brain scan would then be conscious.** It would not. A scan at one instant is one state. Together with the rule it is potential, like the worm's first state: the states it would lead to are not in it, and nothing realizes them until something runs. On our side, the difference between a running brain and a stored scan remains real for every practical purpose, and the empirical programme of FMT is untouched by the argument. A whole realized trajectory is a different object from a single state.

## 9. If the record lacks experience: a split for physics

The conditional of Sections 4 to 8 can be denied, and its denial has a definite shape. A full-state record and a live run of the same trajectory hold the same states and the same relations among them: every state, every dependency of one state on another, and the closure between self-model and world-model if there is one. A difference in experience between them therefore cannot be a difference in structure. Any candidate difference either lies among the states and their relations, which the record shares, or lies outside them. So the options reduce to two.

On the first horn, the run has an ingredient that the record lacks and that no structural description captures: a physical property of actual unfolding, present when states come about one after another and absent when they lie side by side in a medium. Whatever it is, it belongs to physical process as such, below any architecture, and so to whatever underlies the physics of the substrate. That is a light form of panpsychism. It needs no micro-subjects and no experience in particles, only a contribution from physical becoming that closure alone does not supply. On this horn closure is still what makes a subject, and a closed architecture in a medium without the ingredient has no inside. On the second horn there is no such ingredient, and a human life laid out on a disk, state by state, is conscious exactly as the living person was. The argument of Sections 4 to 8 develops the second horn: D1, the dynamics-neutral decoder and the chance-found printout say what a structural account of experience needs, and Section 5 shows that it can be had without triviality. None of this excludes the first horn.

Barnes's causal-history condition (Section 4.2) falls into one horn or the other, depending on what causation is. On a Humean view, causal facts supervene on the pattern of events: regularities in Hume's sense, or counterfactual dependence fixed by the whole arrangement of particular facts (Lewis 1973, 1994). In a closed simulated world the relevant regularity is the rule, and a record that compresses carries the rule (Section 5). "State 4,000 caused state 4,001" is then a fact about the pattern, the record holds the pattern, and the causal-history condition is met by the record. Barnes read this way lands on the second horn. On a non-Humean view, causation is primitive production: an earlier state brings about a later one, and the laws that govern this are themselves basic and productive, over and above any pattern (Maudlin 2007). The record's states were not produced by one another in the medium, and the chance printout's were produced by nothing that ran the rule. Production of this kind is an extra-structural property of actual unfolding, and Barnes read this way lands on the first horn. The choice Section 4.2 makes as a premise is in this sense a choice between the horns.

Which horn has room depends on the physics of time. If spacetime is a four-dimensional whole in which all events are equally real, as Putnam (1967) argued special relativity requires, nothing comes into being. A brain's history is then itself a set of states laid out along a time direction and related by the dynamics, with no further happening, and what separates it from the disk is how its states are arranged in spacetime. On a block universe the first horn has no natural candidate for its ingredient, unless that arrangement itself, states succeeding one another along a timelike direction, is made the ingredient; otherwise the second horn is what remains. If time involves genuine becoming, the first horn has room. A growing or crystallizing block, on which the indeterminate future becomes a determinate past through quantum events (Ellis 2006; Ellis and Rothman 2010), and objective-collapse dynamics, on which state reduction is a physical process absent from unitary evolution (Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber 1986; Bassi et al. 2013), each supply a kind of physical event that occurs in a running system and has no counterpart in a stored record. Room is all they supply. That becoming exists would not show that experience depends on it.

The two horns are behaviourally identical. The reports of a recorded or running inhabitant are fixed by its states, which are the same on both horns, and an outside observer of a running system sees the same behaviour whichever horn holds. No experiment on a conscious system, natural or artificial, decides between them, so the split is a dependency on physics that no test of FMT can settle. Physics bears on it asymmetrically. Evidence that time is a block and causation is pattern would remove the first horn's candidates and leave the second. Evidence for becoming or for primitive production would keep the first horn open without establishing it.

FMT does not decide the split. It specifies the architecture a conscious system needs, and on both horns that architecture is required. What it predicts is that the question of the record has exactly these two answers, and that physics, not consciousness research, is placed to narrow them. The first horn is an open option and is neither this paper's claim nor FMT's.

## 10. Consequences for the theory, and conclusion

FMT says that the properties constituting phenomenality are "constitutively processual — they exist in the doing, not in the blueprint", and that they "exist when and only when the computation is actually running at self-referential closure" (Gruber 2026a, §3.4.5). After Section 9, "running" in that sentence has two readings, one for each horn. On the first, it names actual physical unfolding, an ingredient a record lacks. On the second, the structural horn developed here, "process" and "running" mean the internal dependency structure of a realized trajectory: each state present and related to its predecessors by the rule, the self-model's states depending on the world-model's and the world-model's on the self-model's. On that reading they do not mean execution on a host. The sentence fits the theory only when read as stating the split, since the architecture it describes is the same on both horns. On the structural horn, the contrast between blueprint and doing survives in two places. The rule and the first state are a blueprint in the sense of D1: they fix the trajectory and are not it. Any description, however complete, is a blueprint in the reader's sense, held as content by someone outside the structure. FMT's denial that a complete description is an instance holds as a statement about readers. The bridging commitment, that closure is phenomenality with the inside/outside asymmetry constitutive, is unaffected.

The general result is a third exit from Bartlett's dilemma. Counterfactual conditions on implementation were introduced to stop the rock from implementing everything. The rock can be stopped instead by counting the decoding map as an object in the world, subject to the same realization condition as the trajectory, and by reading algorithmic information as a record of where a trajectory is stored. With that, counterfactuals can be dropped without accepting pixies in every open system, and activity can be dropped without accepting that every mathematical structure has an inside. What an inner world needs, on this account, is a realized trajectory, organized by a short rule, with the architecture a theory of consciousness specifies. Execution on a host is one way such a trajectory comes to be realized. A file on a disk is another. If the file nonetheless lacks what the run has, the missing ingredient is physical and not computational, and whether there is one is a question for the physics of time and causation.
