# What a Rejection Cost: Conversion Cost and the Reversibility of Scientific Falsity Rulings

*Anonymised for review*

---

## Abstract

Scientific communities rule claims false, and some of those rulings are later reversed. Continental drift and the bacterial cause of peptic ulcers were rejected and then accepted; geocentrism and spontaneous generation have stayed rejected. This article states and operationalises a hypothesis about which rulings return: the resistance of a falsity ruling to reversal scales with the cost of the conversion that established it. Stated loosely, the hypothesis is circular, because a ruling that never came back can always be described afterwards as having been expensive. I define conversion cost from five components that are dated no later than the ruling itself (prior tenure of the rejected claim, duration of the controversy, breadth of participation, institutional acts, and the theoretical reorganisation the ruling required), and I define resistance only for rulings that met a qualifying later challenge, as the time from that challenge to re-acceptance. The test samples rulings from reference works at fixed baseline dates, so that the sample does not depend on which rulings were later reversed; codes cost blind to outcome; controls for the strength of the original refuting evidence and for cohort turnover; and states in advance which results would refute the hypothesis. The relevant prior work includes Kuhn, Lakatos and Laudan on theory change, epistemic entrenchment in belief revision, Planck's principle, and the bibliometrics of delayed recognition. None of it states a cost-scaling law for negative verdicts or measures conversion cost independently of the outcome it is meant to predict.

**Keywords:** theory change; scientific rejection; entrenchment; reversal; continental drift; *Helicobacter pylori*; history of science methodology

## 1. Introduction

Some rejected claims return. Alfred Wegener's proposal that the continents have moved large distances relative to one another was rejected by most geologists in the 1920s and accepted, in the form of plate tectonics, by the end of the 1960s (Oreskes 1999; Le Grand 1988). The proposal that a bacterium living in the stomach causes most peptic ulcers was treated as settled against for three decades after a negative biopsy study in 1954, revived in 1983, and endorsed by a United States National Institutes of Health consensus panel in 1994 (Palmer 1954; Warren and Marshall 1983; NIH Consensus Development Panel 1994; Kidd and Modlin 1998; Thagard 1998a). Reports of stones falling from the sky were dismissed by much of learned Europe in the eighteenth century and accepted within a decade of Chladni's 1794 treatise, after the fall at L'Aigle in 1803 (Marvin 1996).

Other rejected claims have not returned, and nobody expects them to. Geocentrism is the standard example. The rejection of the Ptolemaic arrangement took about a century and a half from Copernicus to general acceptance of a moving earth, and its last direct observational supports arrived long after: stellar aberration, communicated to the Royal Society by Bradley in 1729, and annual parallax, announced by Bessel in 1838 (Kuhn 1957; Bradley 1728/9; Bessel 1839).

The difference between the two groups invites a hypothesis about falsity rulings, the verdicts by which a community comes to hold that a claim is false:

> **(G)** The resistance of a falsity ruling to reversal increases with the cost of the conversion that established it.[^nooutside]

A ruling established expensively, across generations, against institutional resistance and at the price of reorganising neighbouring theory, should be hard to reverse. A ruling established cheaply, on a background assumption, on authority or on a single observation, should be comparatively easy to reverse once evidence for the rejected claim appears.

(G) is easy to make unfalsifiable. If "cost" is judged after the fact, a ruling that stayed put will be described as having been expensive and a ruling that collapsed will be described as having been cheap, and the hypothesis will fit every case. The examples above were chosen with their outcomes known, so they do not count as evidence for (G). Their role is to show that the variation (G) addresses exists.

The contribution here has four parts. Section 2 fixes what a falsity ruling is and what counts as its reversal, and separates the ruling from the logical relation of inconsistency that sometimes underlies it. Section 3 states the mechanisms that would produce (G) and the rival hypotheses that predict the same raw correlation for other reasons. Section 4 places (G) among existing work on theory change. Sections 5 to 7 give the operational content: a cost measure dated no later than the ruling, a resistance measure conditional on a qualifying challenge, a sampling rule that does not depend on outcomes, a coding scheme, an analysis plan and the results that would refute (G). Section 8 discusses the limits of the design.

## 2. Rulings, inconsistency and reversal

### 2.1 Two things called refutation

"Refutation" names two different things, and (G) concerns only one of them.

The first is a logical relation. A set of claims together with the observations a system admits can be inconsistent, and once a derivation of the contradiction has been written down, anyone who accepts the rules of derivation can check it. That relation does not change while the observations are kept. The flat earth contradicted the round shadow of the earth on the moon before anyone noticed, and it still does. What the relation does not settle is which member of the inconsistent set is false. Duhem and Quine made that point, and Quine (1951) generalised it: any statement can be held true come what may if enough adjustments are made elsewhere.

The second is a community verdict: the judgement, recorded in textbooks, review articles and professional practice, that a particular claim P is false. A ruling selects P from the inconsistent set as the member to give up, and it often goes beyond what any exhibited inconsistency licensed. The continental drift ruling rested largely on methodological objections and on conflicts with accepted geophysical theory (Oreskes 1999). The ruling against gastric bacteria rested on one negative study and on the background assumption that stomach acid keeps the stomach sterile (Palmer 1954; Kidd and Modlin 1998; Thagard 1998a).

Falsity rulings in the second sense can be reversed, and (G) is a hypothesis about them. A reversal does not undo an inconsistency. It happens in one of three ways: the observation that generated the inconsistency is withdrawn or reinterpreted; it turns out that P was not the member of the set that had to go; or no inconsistency involving P was ever exhibited, and the ruling rested on something weaker.

### 2.2 Definitions

A **falsity ruling** is a triple (P, K, *t*~R~): a proposition P, a community K (a discipline or a defined subfield), and a date *t*~R~ at which K's reference literature comes to state that P is false, refuted, discredited or abandoned. Section 5 gives the dating rule.

A **conversion** is the transition by which K comes to hold not-P. Conversions differ in their starting points. In a **reversal ruling**, P was the community's accepted position for some period before it was challenged and ruled false; geocentrism and spontaneous generation are examples. In a **non-adoption ruling**, P was proposed, considered and rejected without ever having been the accepted position; continental drift and the bacterial theory of ulcers are examples. The distinction is coded, because it is the most direct difference in what a conversion required: a reversal ruling turns a community away from something it held, while a non-adoption ruling only confirms the status quo.

A **reversal of a ruling** occurs when K's reference literature comes to state P, or a proposition that entails P at the grain at which P was ruled false, as accepted. The grain clause matters. What came back in the 1960s was the claim that continents have moved large horizontal distances relative to one another. Wegener's proposed mechanism, continents ploughing through the ocean floor, did not come back. If the ruled-out proposition is recorded as "continents have moved laterally by thousands of kilometres", the plate-tectonic consensus reverses it. If it is recorded as Wegener's mechanism, it does not. Section 5 fixes the recorded proposition from the ruling-era literature, before anything about the later history is known.

**Resistance** is defined only relative to a challenge. A ruling that has never met credible evidence for P has not resisted anything. Its persistence is consistent with any degree of resistance and is uninformative about (G). This is the first reason geocentrism cannot serve as evidence for (G): no qualifying challenge to its rejection has appeared in four centuries, so its stability says nothing about how hard it would be to reverse.

## 3. Why conversion cost might matter, and what else might explain the pattern

### 3.1 Three candidate mechanisms

**Evidential pinning.** A ruling resting on a specific contradiction can be overturned only by overturning that observation or by showing it was misapplied to P. That is a narrow target, and a costly controversy tends to produce rulings pinned to many observations, each of which would have to be overturned. A positive claim, by contrast, is underdetermined by any finite body of evidence and is displaced whenever a better-fitting competitor appears. On this mechanism, cost predicts resistance only because expensive conversions accumulate more pinning evidence. The cost variable would then be a proxy for evidential strength.

**The social ratchet.** A ruling, once made, acquires an apparatus organised around the falsity of P: textbook explanations, teaching examples, standard objections, instrument and training choices, and reputations earned in the controversy. Kuhn (1962/2012, ch. XI) described how post-revolutionary textbooks rewrite a field's history so that the defeated view looks like an error made by predecessors. Reversing a ruling means dismantling that apparatus, which is a real forward-looking cost. It is not a sunk cost in the sense of Arkes and Blumer (1985), who studied continued investment justified by money already spent; continuing to hold a ruling avoids the work of rewriting the curriculum and conceding the controversy. The apparatus also grows with the cost of the original conversion: a community that fought for a generation to reach not-P teaches the fight, and a community that dismissed P in a symposium has little to teach. On this mechanism, cost predicts resistance beyond what the evidence explains.

**Consolidation.** A ruling used repeatedly stops being held as an explicit claim and becomes a habit of rejection. Most people who know the earth is not flat cannot reconstruct the argument. They have the verdict without the reasons, and it arrives as an immediate dismissal before any reasoning starts. I have developed a version of this in a model-based account of cognition, on which content used repeatedly in a running explicit model is written back into implicit structure that is not itself open to explicit revision ([Author] 2026a). Nothing in the test below depends on that account. At the community level, consolidation shows up as rulings taught without their evidence, which Section 5 codes as a mediator.

### 3.2 Rival explanations of the same correlation

A raw correlation between conversion cost and stability would not establish (G). Four alternatives predict it.

**Truth.** Rulings that are correct are not reversed, however cheaply they were made. If expensive conversions are more often correct, cost will correlate with stability because of truth alone. Truth is not available to the investigator as a variable. The design restricts the main analysis to rulings that met a qualifying challenge (Section 6), and Section 7.2 adds an analysis restricted to rulings that were in fact reversed, for which the truth confound runs in a known direction.

**Evidential strength.** If the evidential mechanism is the whole story, the stability of a ruling depends on the strength of the evidence behind it, and cost has no effect once that is controlled for. The design measures the strength of the refuting evidence at *t*~R~ separately from cost (Section 5.3) and treats the residual effect of cost as the test of the social ratchet.

**Cohort turnover.** Planck's principle says that new views win when their opponents die. Hull, Tessner and Diamond (1978) tested this on the reception of Darwin's theory in Britain and found that the received views were less than accurate: conversion was neither as fast nor as strongly graded by age as supposed. Azoulay, Fons-Rosen and Graff Zivin (2019) found that after the premature death of an eminent life scientist, contributions to the field from non-collaborators rise and are disproportionately highly cited. If resistance comes from the people who established a ruling, it should fade as they leave the field. If it comes from the apparatus, it should outlast them. The design separates the two by the interval between *t*~R~ and the challenge (Section 7.3).

**Neglect.** Kitcher (1990) argued that a community does better when its members distribute their effort over rival approaches, including ones that currently look worse. A ruling reached through a long conversion may leave nobody working on P, while a quick dismissal may leave a minority who keep working on it, as continental drift kept Arthur Holmes and Alexander du Toit (Le Grand 1988; Oreskes 1999). That would produce (G) through the survival of a dissenting minority, not through the defence of a ruling. It is a candidate mediator of (G), and Section 5.4 codes it.

## 4. Prior work

**Kuhn.** Kuhn (1962/2012) gave the classic account of resistance to new paradigms and of how textbooks render revolutions invisible. His concern was the resistance of an accepted paradigm to anomalies and to its successor. (G) concerns the other direction: the resistance of an accepted rejection to the return of what it rejected. Kuhn also gave no measure that could rank two rejections by how hard they would be to undo.

**Lakatos and Laudan.** Lakatos (1970/1978) held that a degenerating research programme may recover, so that elimination is never final and there is no instant rationality. Laudan (1977) likewise allowed that abandoned research traditions are sometimes revived. Both accept that reversals happen. Neither says which rejections are more likely to be reversed. Laudan's (1981) pessimistic induction from the history of discarded theories concerns positive claims: successful theories have often turned out false. That is the complementary half of an asymmetry whose negative half (G) addresses. Laudan, Donovan, Laudan and colleagues (1986; Donovan, Laudan and Laudan 1988) proposed testing the claims of theories of scientific change against historical cases, extracted theses from Kuhn, Lakatos, Feyerabend and others, and organised case studies to test them. The design below belongs to that programme, with two changes: the cases are sampled by an outcome-blind rule, and the variables are coded by people who do not know the outcome.

**Thagard.** Thagard (1992) modelled conceptual revolutions, the acceptance of plate tectonics among them, as shifts in explanatory coherence. His two studies of the ulcer case (Thagard 1998a, 1998b) are the most detailed philosophical treatment of one of the returning rulings used here and attend to the role of instruments, experiments and social interaction in the acceptance of the bacterial theory. Explanatory coherence is an evidential account: acceptance follows the balance of explanation. It corresponds to the evidential-strength alternative of Section 3.2 and predicts no residual effect of conversion cost.

**Entrenchment.** In the theory of belief revision, epistemic entrenchment orders beliefs by how readily they are given up when consistency must be restored (Alchourrón, Gärdenfors and Makinson 1985; Gärdenfors 1988). The formal theory takes the ordering as given and says nothing about where it comes from. Quine (1951) put the core of the web of belief beyond easy revision and spoke of the tendency to disturb the total system as little as possible. (G) can be read as an empirical hypothesis about the source of entrenchment for one class of beliefs, negative verdicts: their entrenchment is set by what it cost to adopt them, and that cost can be measured from the historical record.

**Resistance, prematurity and delayed recognition.** Barber (1961) catalogued sources of scientists' resistance to discovery: substantive and methodological commitments, professional standing, specialisation and others. Stent (1972) called a discovery premature when its implications could not be connected by simple logical steps to the knowledge of its time, and the volume edited by Hook (2002) extended the discussion. These accounts concern the reception of new claims and offer categories of resistance, not a measure of it. Bibliometric work on "sleeping beauties", papers recognised long after publication, has made delayed recognition measurable: van Raan (2004) defined the phenomenon by thresholds, and Ke, Ferrara, Radicchi and Flammini (2015) introduced a parameter-free measure, applied it to 22 million papers, and found a continuous spectrum of delayed recognition. Citation dynamics can supply part of the outcome measure in Section 6. They do not supply a predictor.

**Reversal rates for positive claims.** Medicine has quantified how often accepted positive claims fail. Ioannidis (2005) followed highly cited clinical studies and found that about a sixth of those claiming an effect were contradicted by later research. Prasad and colleagues (2013) found that of 363 articles in a leading journal that tested an established practice, 146 reversed it. These are measured reversal rates for positive claims, with an outcome-independent sampling rule. Nothing comparable exists for negative rulings, and no study I have found relates the reversal of either kind of claim to the cost of the conversion that produced it.

**What is new.** Three things. The unit of analysis is the negative verdict, not the theory or the paradigm. The predictor is a conversion cost fixed no later than the verdict. The hypothesis is a scaling claim conditional on challenge, with stated refutation conditions. I have not found (G), or a quantitative statement of the same relation, in the literatures above.

## 5. Measuring conversion cost before the outcome

### 5.1 The non-circularity condition

Every cost component must be computable from records dated no later than *t*~R~, by coders who do not know whether or when the ruling was reversed. Nothing that responds to a later challenge, and no judgement of how surprising a reversal was, may enter the cost measure. Anything measured after *t*~R~ enters only as a mediator, and only if it is dated before the qualifying challenge.

### 5.2 Fixing P and *t*~R~

For each ruling, P is recorded verbatim as stated in the earliest reference work in the sample that states it as false, together with a note on its grain: whether the ruling denied a phenomenon, a cause or a mechanism. That record fixes what a later reversal has to entail.

*t*~R~ is the first year in which at least two thirds of the sampled reference works of K published in a three-year window state that P is false, refuted, discredited or abandoned. Where the literature is too thin for this rule, as in cases before about 1800, *t*~R~ is taken from the dating in the secondary historiography and flagged; the flagged cases are excluded in a sensitivity analysis.

### 5.3 Cost components, and evidential strength kept apart

**Table 1. Conversion cost components (all from records dated ≤ *t*~R~)**

| Code | Component | Operational definition | Scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Prior tenure | Years during which P was the accepted position of K before the first published challenge to P. Zero for non-adoption rulings. | years |
| C2 | Controversy duration | Years from the first published challenge to P (or, for non-adoption rulings, the first publication of P) to *t*~R~. | years |
| C3 | Participation | Number of distinct authors publishing on the question of P between the start of C2 and *t*~R~. | log count |
| C4 | Institutional acts | Count of formal acts adopting not-P before *t*~R~: society resolutions or symposia issuing a verdict, official condemnations, consensus statements, prizes for the refutation, curricular decisions. | log (1 + count) |
| C5 | Reorganisation | 0: not-P required no change to accepted theory beyond P itself. 1: not-P required revising an adjacent accepted theory, method or instrument. 2: not-P required replacing a framework or founding new instruments, methods or subdisciplines. | ordinal |

The composite C is the mean of the standardised components, with weights fixed in the preregistration. Each component is also reported separately, and the main model is refitted with each component dropped in turn, so that no single component decides the result.

The strength of the evidence behind the ruling is coded separately, from the same pre-*t*~R~ records:

| Code | Variable | Operational definition |
|---|---|---|
| E1 | Independent lines | Number of independent evidential grounds against P cited in the reference works at *t*~R~ (observations, experiments or arguments that do not share a data source). |
| E2 | Kind of ground | 0: authority or background assumption only. 1: theoretical incompatibility, or absence of a mechanism. 2: one direct observational contradiction. 3: several independent direct observational contradictions. |

C and E will correlate, and the design does not need them to be uncorrelated. It needs enough variation in cost at a given level of evidence for the residual effect of C to be estimated. Section 7.1 states what follows if that variation is absent.

### 5.4 Mediators

Two variables are measured after *t*~R~ but before the qualifying challenge. They are used only to ask which mechanism carries an effect of C, never as part of C.

- **M1, apparatus:** the share of sampled textbooks of K in the decade after *t*~R~ that use the refutation of P as a teaching example, and the share that state the verdict without its evidence. The second share is the community-level trace of consolidation (Section 3.1).
- **M2, dissent:** the number of distinct authors publishing in favour of P in the decade after *t*~R~ (Section 3.2, neglect).

### 5.5 Coding procedure

Each case is assembled into a dossier of records dated no later than *t*~R~ by a team that takes no part in coding. That team removes any reference to later events. Two coders code every cost and evidence variable independently. Reliability is reported as Krippendorff's α (Hayes and Krippendorff 2007), and a variable enters the analysis only if α ≥ 0.67 in a pilot of at least twenty cases. Coders record whether they recognise the case. Well-known cases cannot be fully blinded, so the main model is refitted without recognised cases. A separate team codes the challenge and outcome variables of Section 6 without access to the cost codes.

### 5.6 The three motivating cases under the scheme

Three of the four cases of Section 1 show what the scheme does with real material and why they cannot test (G). The meteorite case would be coded in the same way and is left out for brevity.

*Geocentrism.* P: the earth is at rest at the centre of the cosmos. Reversal ruling. C1 is very large: the Ptolemaic system had been the accepted position since the second century. C2 runs from 1543 at least into the late seventeenth century. C4 includes the 1616 decree of the Congregation of the Index on Copernicus's book. C5 = 2: the conversion required a new physics. E2 rises over the controversy. The phases of Venus, observed by Galileo in 1610, contradicted the Ptolemaic arrangement while leaving the geoheliocentric system of Tycho Brahe standing, and the observations of the earth's motion came only in 1729 and 1838 (Kuhn 1957; Bradley 1728/9; Bessel 1839). No qualifying challenge has occurred, so the case is excluded from the main analysis.

*Continental drift.* P: the continents have moved large horizontal distances relative to one another. Wegener stated it in 1912 and developed it in a 1915 book (Wegener 1912, 1915). Non-adoption ruling, so C1 = 0. C2 is about fourteen years, to the symposium of the American Association of Petroleum Geologists held in New York in 1926 and published in 1928, which stands in the literature as the marker of rejection in the United States (Oreskes 1999). C4 is low. C5 = 0, since the ruling kept the accepted theory. E2 = 1: Oreskes (1999) argues that the American rejection rested on methodological norms and on conflicts with accepted theory, not on a contradicting observation. Palaeomagnetic results of the mid-1950s (Le Grand 1988), the seafloor-spreading hypothesis (Hess 1962) and the magnetic anomaly stripes over ocean ridges (Vine and Matthews 1963) mark the challenge. Re-acceptance came by the end of the 1960s.

*Bacteria and ulcers.* P: a bacterium resident in the human stomach is associated with gastritis and causes most peptic ulcers. Non-adoption ruling, C1 = 0. The ruling rested mainly on Palmer (1954; on its role, Kidd and Modlin 1998), who examined suction biopsies from more than a thousand patients and found no spiral organisms, together with the assumption that acid keeps the stomach free of bacteria (Thagard 1998a). C2, C3 and C4 are small, C5 = 0 and E2 = 2, but a single observation. The challenge came in 1983 (Warren and Marshall 1983), followed by Marshall and Warren (1984) and by Marshall's self-ingestion experiment (Marshall et al. 1985). The NIH panel recommended antibiotic treatment for ulcer patients infected with *H. pylori* in 1994 (NIH Consensus Development Panel 1994).

Both returning cases are cheap rulings with a latency of roughly a decade or a little more from challenge to re-acceptance. They show that cheap rulings can be reversed within about a decade of a credible challenge. They do not show that expensive rulings take longer, because the only expensive ruling among the three was never challenged. A test needs expensive rulings that were challenged, and it needs cases chosen without knowledge of their fate.

## 6. Measuring resistance, and sampling without knowing the outcome

### 6.1 Qualifying challenge

A **qualifying challenge** to ruling (P, K, *t*~R~) is the first publication after *t*~R~ that (a) reports new evidence or a new argument for P; (b) appears in a venue indexed for K or an adjacent field at the time; and (c) is written by an author with at least one earlier publication in K or an adjacent field. The rule refers only to properties of the publication and its author, not to how it was received. Its strength S is coded on the E2 scale by the outcome team. The year of the challenge is *t*~Q~.

### 6.2 Outcome

Re-acceptance is dated by the same two-thirds rule used for *t*~R~ (Section 5.2), applied to statements that P, or a proposition entailing P at the recorded grain, is accepted; that year is *t*~V~. **Latency** is L = *t*~V~ − *t*~Q~. Rulings not reversed by the end of follow-up are censored at that date. A secondary outcome uses citation dynamics: the beauty coefficient of Ke et al. (2015), computed for the challenge publication, measures how long it lay dormant before recognition.

The grain rule decides doubtful cases. A later consensus that shares a name or a theme with P but does not entail P as recorded is coded as no reversal and flagged. The return of "continental drift" as plate tectonics entails lateral displacement of continents, but not Wegener's mechanism. Claims about the transgenerational inheritance of acquired characteristics are the kind of case the flag exists for: whether a later finding entails the proposition the ruling denied depends on how that proposition was recorded, and it has to be recorded before the later finding is considered.

### 6.3 Sampling frame

Selecting famous episodes selects on outcome: reversed rulings are remembered because they were reversed, and rulings that held are remembered when the conversion behind them was dramatic. The frame is therefore drawn from reference works at fixed baseline dates.

1. Choose disciplines with long, well-indexed textbook traditions (for instance astronomy, geology, physiology and medicine, chemistry and general biology) and baseline years (for instance 1900 and 1950).
2. For each discipline and baseline, select reference works by a fixed rule, such as the most widely held textbooks and handbooks in union library catalogues published within five years before the baseline year.
3. Extract every statement that a claim is false, refuted, discredited or abandoned, using a written extraction protocol applied by two extractors, and reduce the extracted statements to distinct propositions.
4. Draw a random sample of rulings, stratified by discipline and baseline.

The frame contains rulings that were later reversed and rulings that were not, in proportions that the investigators do not control. The proportion of sampled rulings that ever meet a qualifying challenge is itself reported. It bounds how much of the stability of falsity rulings has ever been put to a test.

### 6.4 Sample size

The main analysis is a proportional-hazards model of re-acceptance after *t*~Q~ among challenged rulings. To detect a hazard ratio of 0.6 per standard deviation of C at two-sided α = 0.05 with 80 % power requires about 30 reversal events, and about 40 if C correlates with the evidence covariates at around 0.5. If roughly a third of challenged rulings are reversed within follow-up, that means about 120 to 130 challenged rulings. If about a third of sampled rulings are ever challenged, the frame sample has to be around 400 rulings. Both proportions are guesses to be replaced by pilot estimates; they are given here to show that the study is feasible for a team and out of reach for a single case study. That size is set by the effect (G) predicts. The refutation condition R1 of Section 7.1 needs more: if C has no effect, its confidence interval falls entirely above 0.8 in roughly a quarter of samples at 30 to 40 events, and 80 % power for R1 takes about 160 events.

## 7. Analysis and refutation conditions

### 7.1 Main test

The model is a stratified Cox regression of re-acceptance after *t*~Q~ on C, with E1, E2, the strength S of the challenge, the interval *t*~Q~ − *t*~R~ and the era of *t*~R~ as covariates, stratified by discipline. (G) predicts a hazard ratio for C below 1: the more expensive the conversion, the lower the yearly probability of re-acceptance once a challenge has appeared. The preregistered smallest effect of interest is a hazard ratio of 0.8 per standard deviation of C.

(G) is refuted if either of the following holds.

- **R1.** The 95 % confidence interval for the adjusted hazard ratio of C lies entirely above 0.8. Cost then has no effect large enough to matter, or an effect in the opposite direction.
- **R2.** The unadjusted effect of C meets the prediction, but the adjusted interval lies entirely above 0.8 once E1, E2 and S enter. The stability of rulings then follows the strength of the evidence behind them, cost is a proxy for that strength, and the social ratchet is refuted. (G) is refuted as a claim about cost, although the evidential account survives.

If the confidence interval includes both 0.8 and 1, the result is inconclusive and is reported as such. At the sample size of Section 6.4, a null result will usually be inconclusive in this sense. If C and the evidence variables are so closely correlated that the adjusted interval is too wide to decide R2, the data cannot distinguish the ratchet from the evidential account, and that is reported in place of a verdict.

Because (G) is a statistical claim, single cases are not scored as refutations. Two kinds are reported separately because they are the cases (G) most directly forbids: expensive rulings reversed within a few years of a qualifying challenge, and cheap rulings that survived a strong challenge (S = 3) for decades.

### 7.2 Reversed rulings only

The second analysis is restricted to rulings that were in fact reversed and asks whether latency increases with C. Every ruling in this subset turned out to be wrong by the community's current lights, so the confound that correct rulings never return (Section 3.2) does not operate within it. Restricting the sample to completed reversals introduces truncation: expensive rulings challenged recently, whose reversal is slow, are less likely to have been completed by now and drop out, which biases the estimate towards no effect. A positive result in this subset therefore survives a bias against it. A null result here, combined with a positive main result, would suggest that the main result is carried by the truth confound.

### 7.3 Mechanism

Three further analyses address mechanism. They do not decide whether (G) holds, and they are reported as secondary.

- **Cohort.** The effect of C is estimated separately for challenges arriving within 35 years of *t*~R~ and later. A cohort-turnover account predicts that the effect disappears in the later group; the ratchet predicts that it persists, because textbooks and curricula outlast their authors. If the effect is confined to the earlier group, the ratchet is refuted in favour of turnover, even though (G) as a correlation survives.
- **Apparatus and consolidation.** Mediation of the effect of C through M1.
- **Dissent.** Mediation of the effect of C through M2. If the effect of C runs through the size of the dissenting minority, the neglect account explains (G), and the ratchet adds nothing.

## 8. Limits

**Truth remains a confound.** Conditioning on a qualifying challenge removes rulings that have never been tested, but challenges to correct rulings fail for reasons that have nothing to do with cost. If expensive conversions more often produce correct rulings, part of any cost effect in the main analysis is an effect of truth. The analysis restricted to reversed rulings limits this, and the evidence covariates absorb part of it. What remains cannot be removed, because the design has no access to truth except through the later verdicts it studies. The result is therefore a finding about resistance to reversal, and it says nothing about resistance to correct reversal.

**Claim identity.** Many apparent returns are returns of a successor that shares a name with the rejected claim. The grain rule decides these cases by the record made at the time of the ruling, which keeps the coding independent of the outcome but will sometimes count a substantive return as a non-reversal because the original ruling denied something more specific. Flagged cases are reported, and the main model is refitted with flagged cases coded as reversals.

**Dating.** The two-thirds rule depends on which reference works the sample contains, and for older cases on the secondary historiography. Errors in *t*~R~ propagate into C2 and into the interval used for the cohort analysis. The flagged-case sensitivity analysis bounds the effect.

**Scope.** The frame covers disciplines with textbook traditions in the period after about 1800. Rulings in fields without such traditions, and rulings made outside institutional science, are out of scope. Whether (G) holds for them is a separate question.

**What cost means.** The five components are not the only defensible operationalisation of conversion cost. They are chosen because each can be dated before the ruling and coded from documents. A different operationalisation, fixed in advance and dated the same way, would test the same hypothesis. The one condition no operationalisation may relax is the dating condition of Section 5.1.

## 9. Conclusion

(G) says that how hard a scientific rejection is to undo depends on what it cost to make. The standard cases are compatible with it and cannot test it: the expensive ruling among them was never challenged, and the cheap ones were picked because they came back. Made testable, (G) becomes a claim about challenged rulings sampled from reference works without regard to their fate, with conversion cost coded from records dated no later than the ruling, evidential strength coded separately, and refutation conditions fixed in advance. If (G) survives, its main practical consequence is that cheaply established rejections deserve a lower threshold for re-examination than their place in the textbooks suggests. If it fails under R2, the history of reversals is explained by the strength of the original evidence, and the institutional history of a rejection is irrelevant to whether it will last. Neither result can be obtained from the famous cases alone.
