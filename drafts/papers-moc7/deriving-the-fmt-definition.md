## Abstract

The Four-Model Theory (FMT) defines consciousness as the ongoing process in which an entity, organism or machine, runs a model of itself, relates that model to itself and interacts with it; the capacity for that process is named separately. Two objections are raised against definitions of this kind. One is circularity: "self" and "relating to itself" seem to presuppose a subject. The other is a change of subject: the definition is said to capture a cognitive capacity and to leave phenomenal consciousness untouched. This article reconstructs the derivation of the definition from its first statement (Gruber, 2015). Every term of the definiens is grounded in primitives defined without consciousness: data as differentially registrable physical phenomena, information as reversibly codable data, knowledge as information assigned to a reference system, interpretation as the assigning, and a model as a non-trivial reference system traceable to a physical one. Applied to a test case, the definition yields two further requirements, a containing world-model and a functional boundary between self and world. One step is not derived: the identification of the defined process with the referent of the ordinary word. That identification has the logical form of "water is H₂O", and every theory of consciousness contains a step of this form; FMT states its own as an identity of phenomenality with self-referential closure. The phenomenological formulations offered as the accepted definition are reference-fixing devices, and they are applied to cases through a self-model, memory and report, which is the structure the FMT definition names. Separating the capacity (*Bewusstsein*) from the ongoing process (*Bewusstheit*) handles sleep without treating arousability as evidence of consciousness. The article states FMT's position on phenomenal content below reportability and on reportedly selfless states, and names the findings that would count against the definition. Scored against nine conditions on a formulation that decides contested cases, FMT meets the operational and discrimination conditions for systems whose structure and running dynamics can be inspected, and relies on behavioural evidence for the others.

**Keywords:** definition of consciousness; self-model; reference system; reference fixing; phenomenal consciousness; sleep

## 1. Introduction

The Four-Model Theory (Gruber, 2026) defines consciousness as "the ongoing process in which an entity — biological or artificial — runs a model of itself, relates that model to itself, and interacts with it", and names the capacity for that process separately, as the capacity for consciousness (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.1). The definition was first stated in German, as a capacity (Gruber, 2015, p. 58):

> Das Bewusstsein ist die Fähigkeit einer Entität (Lebewesen oder Maschine) ein Modell seiner selbst („Selbstmodell") zu erzeugen, es auf sich selbst zu beziehen und damit zu interagieren.
>
> *Consciousness is the capacity of an entity (organism or machine) to generate a model of itself ("self-model"), to relate it to itself, and to interact with it.*

Two objections recur. The first is circularity. A "model of itself" seems to need a self, and "relating it to itself" seems to need a subject who relates, so the definiens appears to contain what it defines. The second objection grants that the definition is well formed and denies that it is a definition of consciousness. What it picks out is a cognitive capacity, and whether that capacity is consciousness is the question at issue. Smith (2026) puts the second objection in print: explanatory constructs including "attention, access, prediction, information integration, global availability, and self-modelling should be evaluated according to how successfully they account for conscious experience rather than treated as independent definitions of consciousness".

This article answers both. Sections 2 to 4 reconstruct the derivation from the 2015 text and show that each term of the definiens is defined without consciousness. Section 5 separates the capacity from the process, a distinction the 2015 text introduced and then dropped. Section 6 locates the one step that is not derived, the identification of the process with the referent of the ordinary word, and compares it with the corresponding step in other theories. Section 7 examines the phenomenological formulations and shows what their application presupposes. Section 8 takes the strongest objections: that the account reaches access only, that selfless states exist, and that the definition admits trivial systems. A companion paper (Gruber, in preparation) surveys the formulations treated as the settled definition of consciousness, finds that their authors present them as pointers, and states nine conditions on a formulation that decides contested cases; Section 9 scores FMT against them.

## 2. What a definition of consciousness has to do

Searle (1998) offers a common-sense definition: "'Consciousness' refers to those states of sentience or awareness that typically begin when we wake from a dreamless sleep and continue through the day until we fall asleep again, die, go into a coma or otherwise become 'unconscious'." He states its purpose as "to identify the target … and not to provide an analysis", and adds that "analytic definitions typically come at the end, not at the beginning of a scientific investigation", with water as his example. The distinction behind the remark is the one Kripke (1980) and Putnam (1975) drew for natural-kind terms. A description such as "the clear liquid in rivers and lakes" fixes the reference of "water". The statement that water is H₂O says what the referent is, and it was found out.

Two kinds of item therefore go under the name "definition of consciousness". A reference-fixer picks out cases. A theoretical definition states what the cases have in common that makes them cases. Block (1995) places phenomenal consciousness in the first category: "Let me acknowledge at the outset that I cannot define P-consciousness in any remotely non-circular way. I don't consider this an embarrassment", and "really all one can do is point to the phenomenon".

The 2015 book sets out requirements for the second kind before it defines anything. Terms are to be defined consistently before any explanation is attempted, since otherwise every attempt "ist … von vorne herein zum Scheitern verurteilt" (is doomed from the start; Gruber, 2015, p. 57). Data, information and knowledge are to be describable independently of one another (p. 14). The ordinary word is kept and given a precise sense; coining a fresh technical term in its place is rejected because it abandons the task of explaining the phenomenon to someone who asks about it (p. 57). Substrate neutrality is built into the first primitive, whose reading device may be "einem Lebewesen oder einer Maschine" (an organism or a machine; p. 13). The closing summary of the definitions chapter states the standard reached: data, information, knowledge, intelligence and consciousness were defined "ohne Bezug zum Menschen (also nicht-zirkulär und nicht-rekursiv), nur anhand von sehr einfachen Grundbegriffen" (without reference to the human being, that is, non-circularly and non-recursively, using only very simple basic concepts; p. 65). The book also makes Searle's concession: "manchmal [kann] ein Begriff erst dann wirklich klar definiert werden …, wenn der zugrunde liegende Sachverhalt vollständig verstanden wurde" (a term can sometimes be clearly defined only once the underlying matter has been fully understood; p. 11).

The companion paper (Gruber, in preparation) states these requirements, and further ones, as conditions on a formulation that is meant to decide, for systems that do not report, whether they fall under the term (C1 to C5), and on a candidate explication of it (C6 to C9). They are used here verbatim:

- **C1. Non-circularity (diagnostic).** The definiens does not contain the definiendum or a term whose application presupposes it. Failing C1 does not disqualify a formulation. It marks the point at which the formulation will need report or a further theory in order to be applied.
- **C2. Grounding.** The terms in the definiens either are defined elsewhere without circularity or have application conditions that do not depend on the concept being defined.
- **C3. Explicit operationalisation.** The definition, together with a *stated* theory, yields a decision procedure for whether a given system at a given time falls under it, and the procedure does not rely solely on the system's own first-person report. A definition that can be applied only by the subject to itself fails C3 whatever theory is added.
- **C4. Substrate neutrality.** The definition can be applied to organisms and machines without building in the answer, in either direction.
- **C5. Discrimination of contested cases.** The definition sorts the cases on which researchers disagree (dreamless sleep, anaesthesia, disorders of consciousness, infants, non-human animals, artificial systems), or states what further fact would sort them and how that fact could be established for a system that does not report.
- **C6. A stated bridge.** The explicatum is connected to the pointer's referent by a premise, either an identity (as "water is H₂O" or "heat is mean molecular kinetic energy") or a nomological bridge law (as in the psychophysical laws Chalmers calls for). The premise is stated, together with what would count against it. […]
- **C7. Agreement on the uncontested cases.** The explicatum classifies the paradigm cases the way the pointer does: waking perception and pain fall inside; hormone release, dispositional knowledge and the regulation of blood pressure fall outside. An explicatum that fails here has changed the subject. […]
- **C8. An explanation of the pointer.** The explicatum explains why systems that satisfy it produce the reports and beliefs on which the pointer rests: why they say there is something it is like, why they find the explanatory gap compelling, why introspection describes experience as it does. […]
- **C9. Creature and state distinguished.** *Conscious* is said both of creatures and of states (Rosenthal, 1993, 2005; Bayne, 2007), and of capacities and of occurrences. […] An explicatum should say which of these it explicates, because the contested cases differ in which of them is in question.

Francken et al. (2022) found that "the definition of 'what' needs to be explained by consciousness science is not shared between all respondents" and recommended that researchers make their definition explicit as "a conceptual or computational definition", which supports C1 and C2. Reichert (2026) argues that "only theories that target functionally-defined aspects of consciousness can be scientific theories", which supports C3. Reichert draws the consequence that theories of phenomenal consciousness cannot be decided empirically; that consequence holds for theories that posit phenomenal facts over and above physical ones, and FMT does not (Section 8.1).

## 3. Primitives defined without consciousness

### 3.1 Data, coding and information

The chain begins with data:

> Daten sind physikalische Erscheinungen (wie zum Beispiel Zahlen auf einem Stück Papier), die von einer Lesevorrichtung (zum Beispiel einem Lebewesen oder einer Maschine) differenziert aufgenommen werden können. (Gruber, 2015, p. 13)
>
> *Data are physical phenomena (for example numbers on a piece of paper) that a reading device (for example an organism or a machine) can register differentially.*

Data are relative to the reading device. A white sheet of paper is data, but for an unaided human eye it contains no further data (p. 13).

Information requires a coding, and a coding is defined by reversibility:

> Informationen sind differenzierbare physikalische Erscheinungen, denen eine bestimmte Kodierung zugeordnet werden kann. … Die Kodierung einer Information ist ein Vorgang, bei welchem diese Information reversibel in einen anderen Zustand transformiert wird. (p. 14)
>
> *Information is differentiable physical phenomena to which a particular coding can be assigned. … Coding is a process by which information is reversibly transformed into another state.*

Where a state can be transformed into another and, at least in principle, back again, there is information; where it cannot, there are at most data (pp. 14–15). Nothing in the two definitions mentions a subject, an experience or a meaning.

### 3.2 Knowledge and interpretation

The book considers and rejects two candidate definitions of knowledge before giving its own. "Wissen ist Information, welche bereits bestehendem Wissen zugeordnet werden kann" (knowledge is information that can be assigned to existing knowledge) is called correct within the system but not clean, being recursive. "Knowledge is information that can be understood" is called plausible and correct, and set aside because "understanding" would itself need defining (p. 22). The definition adopted borrows a concept from physics:

> Wissen ist Information, die einem Bezugssystem zugeordnet ist. … Die Interpretation ist ein Vorgang, bei welchem Information einem Bezugssystem zugeordnet wird. (p. 22)
>
> *Knowledge is information that is assigned to a reference system. … Interpretation is a process by which information is assigned to a reference system.*

Interpretation is thus the process that turns information into knowledge. Truth is excluded from the definition by design: knowledge is defined "unter völligem Verzicht auf den Begriff der ‚Wahrheit'" (with complete renunciation of the concept of truth; p. 22), so false knowledge is knowledge. Consciousness is excluded as well. The reference system "muss … keinesfalls bewusst sein; darin unterscheidet sich implizites von explizitem Wissen" (need not be conscious at all; that is what distinguishes implicit from explicit knowledge; p. 22). The book adds that the implicit/explicit distinction is well defined only once consciousness has been defined (p. 23); knowledge itself is not deferred in this way. A machine with a reference system has knowledge.

### 3.3 Reference systems are models

The step that joins the epistemic vocabulary to the architecture of the later chapters is on the same page:

> Ein Bezugssystem ist in der Physik ein „raum-zeitliches Gebilde", an welchem der Physiker ein (in der Regel gedachtes) Koordinatensystem fixieren kann … Das heißt, dass Wissen eine Abbildung von Information ist. Somit können wir die Begriffe Bezugssystem und Modell zumindest für unsere Zwecke gleichsetzen. (p. 22)
>
> *In physics a reference system is a "spatio-temporal structure" to which the physicist can fix an (often imagined) coordinate system … This means that knowledge is a mapping of information. We can therefore equate reference system and model, at least for our purposes.*

The book widens the notion so that a model or a virtual system can serve as a reference system, and it grounds the widening at once: information in the brain can in principle be assigned unambiguously to "dem Bezugssystem Gehirn", and a model or virtual system "lässt sich letzten Endes wieder auf ein physikalisches Bezugssystem zurückführen" (can ultimately be traced back to a physical reference system; p. 22). A model, in the sense the consciousness definition uses, is "nur ein nicht-triviales Bezugssystem" (only a non-trivial reference system; p. 60).

This disposes of a suspected loop. If a model is defined by the system's ability to interpret its own representations, interpretation is assignment to a reference system, and a reference system is a model, the three definitions seem to chase one another. The chain is recursive with a base case, and the 2015 text supplies the base: a physical reference system, specified by registrable differences (C2) without the word "model". The FMT master paper gives a second base case for the same recursion (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.6). Each layer of knowledge is information assigned against a layer already in place, so the recursion must end in a layer that was never assigned at run time, a layer grown by plasticity and selection. That layer is the implicit one, and "the regress is consciousness-free throughout". A system whose representational content is all assigned at run time has no base case, which is a structural reason for the implicit models and does not rest on neurobiology.

## 4. From model to self-model

### 4.1 Grounding each term of the definiens

The 2015 definition is introduced as a deliberate simplification: "Ich halte es für besser, zuerst ein Modell aus möglichst einfachen Grundbegriffen heraus zu erarbeiten, daher wird auch die Definition für das Bewusstsein unerwartet simpel erscheinen … und [ich beschreibe] sie nicht als Phänomen, sondern so wie auch die Intelligenz als ‚Fähigkeit'" (I think it better first to work out a model from the simplest possible basic concepts; that is why the definition of consciousness will seem unexpectedly simple, and why I describe it as a capacity and not as a phenomenon, as with intelligence; p. 58). The next two pages tie each term back to Section 3.

*Entity.* The reading device of the data definition, organism or machine.

*Model.* A non-trivial reference system (p. 60).

*Model of itself.* "Das Selbstmodell ist ein Modell, mit welchem sich eine Entität selbst abbildet (‚Schreibzugriff'), und welches die Entität interpretieren kann (‚Lesezugriff')" (the self-model is a model by which an entity maps itself, write access, and which the entity can interpret, read access; p. 62). "Itself" is the reflexive pronoun for the entity. No experiential self is presupposed: the self-model is something the entity produces, and the book adds that it is not produced consciously, since "das wäre ein Zirkelschluss" (that would be a circular argument; p. 59).

*Interact.* "Mit ‚interagieren' ist hier im einfachsten Falle nur interpretieren (oder ‚beobachten') gemeint, also Informationen zu Wissen zu verarbeiten; dazu ist keinerlei Zauberei erforderlich" (in the simplest case "interact" means only interpret, that is, process information into knowledge; no magic is needed; p. 59). "Interpret" was avoided in the definition because it makes the sentence clumsier and would have excluded the extended forms defined afterwards (p. 59). This sentence is the direct link between the consciousness definition and the epistemic chain: interaction with the self-model includes assigning information about oneself to a reference system.

*Relate it to itself.* This term needs a construction, given in Section 4.2.

Consciousness on this definition is "die direkte Funktion des Selbstmodells" (the direct function of the self-model; p. 59). The book then claims C1 for it in so many words (p. 65), and the claim holds for the noun: no term of the definiens requires experience, feeling or perception for its own definition. The definitions that follow on p. 62, of the adjective "bewusst" and of the I-model as the self-model "welches dem Bewusstsein zur Verfügung steht" (which is available to consciousness), use the noun once it is defined; they are later in the order of definition and do not feed back into it.

### 4.2 What "relate it to itself" requires

The book builds the requirement through a test case (p. 60). A camera pointed at a mirror registers itself and is not conscious; it "betrachtet nur, sie beobachtet (interpretiert) nicht. Die Kamera arbeitet mit Information, nicht mit Wissen" (only looks, does not interpret; it works with information, not knowledge). An image-recognition system that decomposes what it sees into contents can assign them to a reference system, and with a mirror it could build a model of itself. That still falls short:

> zwei Elemente – und darunter das entscheidende – fehlen noch: Erstens: Das Selbstmodell muss laut obiger Definition interpretiert werden. Es muss mit dem System selbst in ein Bezugssystem gestellt werden, so dass eine Beziehung zwischen dem Modell und dem modellierten System entsteht. Es muss also implizit ein „Übermodell" geben, in welchem das Selbstmodell enthalten ist. Dieses Übermodell nennen wir ab sofort das Metamodell.
> Zweitens: Das System muss sich selbst mit seinem Selbstmodell in irgendeiner Form in Bezug stellen … Das heißt, Selbstmodell und Metamodell müssen so beschaffen sein, dass das System die Grenze zwischen „sich selbst und dem Rest der Welt" … erkennen kann. Diese Grenze muss aber nicht bewusst sein. Es genügt eine implizite Grenze, so dass das System auf Änderungen am Selbstmodell anders reagieren kann, als auf Änderungen am Metamodell. (p. 60)
>
> *Two elements are still missing, one of them decisive. First: the self-model must be interpreted. It must be placed, together with the system itself, in a reference system, so that a relation arises between the model and the modelled system. There must therefore be, implicitly, an "over-model" containing the self-model, which we call the meta-model. Second: the system must relate itself to its self-model in some form … self-model and meta-model must be such that the system can recognise the boundary between "itself and the rest of the world". This boundary need not be conscious. An implicit boundary suffices, such that the system responds differently to changes in the self-model than to changes in the meta-model.*

Two requirements follow from the definition. The self-model must be contained in a model of everything the entity can map and interpret, and the system must carry a functional self/world boundary, defined behaviourally by a difference in response. Neither requirement uses an experiential term. The first is the origin of the nesting that the current theory states as ISM ⊆ IWM (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.2), where the four model kinds are presented as the minimum that the theory's principles force; the 2015 meta-model, "eine Abbildung all dessen, was eine Entität abbilden und interpretieren kann" (a mapping of everything an entity can map and interpret; p. 62), is the implicit world-model, and the explicit pair, an I-model embedded in a world-model, follows on p. 61.

### 4.3 Two rival definitions and why they fail

The book tests two simpler candidates (pp. 59–60). "Consciousness is the capacity of a system to perceive itself" is rejected as circular, because "wahrnehmen" already implies consciousness, so the simpler definition is "zwar intuitiv, aber leider auch zirkulär" (intuitive, but unfortunately circular). "Consciousness is a system's knowledge about itself, which it can use to describe itself" is rejected because "ein solches System völlig mechanisch, also ohne Bewusstsein konstruiert werden kann" (such a system can be constructed entirely mechanically, that is, without consciousness).

The second rejection is made by appeal to a prior judgement about what lacks consciousness. The 2015 method therefore already used an antecedent grip on the referent as a check on candidate definitions, which is what a reference-fixer is for.

### 4.4 Scope and the ladder

The 2015 definition is offered for the simplest case. The book does not claim that it describes human consciousness exactly: "Wir erklären hier noch lange nicht das Bewusstsein des Menschen, wir definieren vorerst nur ein einfachst mögliches Konzept eines Bewusstseins" (we are far from explaining human consciousness; for now we define only the simplest possible concept of a consciousness; p. 61). Graded extensions follow (pp. 61–64). In simply extended consciousness the meta-model also maps the relation between itself and the self-model; in doubly extended consciousness it maps the system's own observation of the self-model; in triply extended consciousness it maps the system's interaction with the self-model. Extended consciousness in general can generate a model of its self-model, the I-model, and identifies with it (p. 64). The current theory carries the same ladder as basic, simply, doubly and triply extended consciousness (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.5). The book also notes that even non-extended consciousness "bereits ein minimales Ich-Modell [impliziert]" (already implies a minimal I-model; p. 62), which is the basic, non-recursive explicit self-model of the current theory.

The continuum has two dimensions in the 2015 text: the richness of the self-model, as between child and adult, and "die Bandbreite der Beobachtung des Selbstmodells (im Halbschlaf zum Beispiel sehr gering)" (the bandwidth of observation of the self-model, very low in half-sleep, for example; p. 64). Gradedness is part of the definition from the start.

### 4.5 What the current theory adds

Taken alone, the 2015 sentence admits too much. The book says so itself: "Ein minimales, künstliches Bewusstsein lässt sich nach dieser Definition daher recht einfach erstellen" (a minimal artificial consciousness can therefore be built fairly easily on this definition; p. 60), and the word that carries the threshold, "nicht-trivial", is never defined. The current theory supplies the threshold through two of its principles (Gruber, 2026, Section 3). The modelling must be free, meaning that open-ended computation is recruited by the system for its own modelling (Principle 1). And the modelling must close on itself: the self-model and the world-model each enter the other's update rule (Principle 3). On the master paper's analysis, a simulation with a monitoring module, a thermostat or an operating-system kernel has self-reference without closure and has no inside perspective (Section 3.4.3). The self model, finally, must be a model of the system itself, however simple: a realistic representation of the organism, its states, geometry and capabilities, in proportion to what the system has. A position estimate alone is not one (Section 3.4.3). The current theory also states what suffices: a system implementing the four model kinds under closure, with free compute, embodiment and at least one pain-pleasure axis, is conscious (Section 3.7.3). The axis may be engineered, since presetting pain as aversive and pleasure as appetitive shortcuts what selection does; embodiment is required for consciousness to emerge naturally, not for it to exist. The definition in current use is the 2015 *Bewusstheit* sentence (Section 5.1) read with these conditions.

### 4.6 What is derived and what is not

Every term of the definiens is grounded in the consciousness-free chain of Section 3, and the minimal architecture, a self-model inside a world-model with a functional boundary between them, follows from applying the definition. The choice of self-modelling as the definiens is not derived. The book presents it, defends it by eliminating two rivals and by its later explanatory use, and limits it to the simplest case. The question whether the defined process is what the ordinary word refers to is the subject of Section 6.

## 5. Capacity and process

### 5.1 A distinction introduced and dropped

The 2015 text gives a second definition beside the first:

> Die Bewusstheit ist die fortlaufende Interaktion mit einem Modell, welches eine Entität von sich selbst erzeugt und auf sich selbst bezieht. (p. 58)
>
> *Bewusstheit is the ongoing interaction with a model that an entity generates of itself and relates to itself.*

It then declares the two words interchangeable and strikes the second: "Daher können wir den Begriff ‚Bewusstheit' wieder aus unserem Gedächtnis streichen – er ist für unsere Zwecke völlig überflüssig" (we can strike "Bewusstheit" from memory again; it is completely superfluous for our purposes; p. 58). The book's own later usage requires the distinction. It says that consciousness "wird im Tiefschlaf zumindest für kurze Zeit unterbrochen" (is interrupted in deep sleep, at least briefly; p. 99), that it is fragmented even in waking (p. 101), and that it is interrupted "nur im Tiefschlaf und bei Narkose oder Ohnmacht" (only in deep sleep, under anaesthesia or in fainting; p. 264). A capacity is not interrupted by sleep; a process is. The current English definition keeps the distinction and puts the word on the process: consciousness is "the ongoing process of running a self-model, relating it to the self, and interacting with it", and the capacity for it is the capacity for consciousness (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.1 and Table 1).

The two terms read:

- **Bewusstsein (capacity for consciousness):** the standing ability of an entity to generate, relate and interact with a self-model. In the current architecture it is carried by the implicit models, the learned structure from which the explicit models are generated.
- **Bewusstheit (consciousness):** the ongoing interaction itself, the explicit models running in the closed loop.

### 5.2 Sleep without the arousal criterion

The common-sense anchor fixes the referent through sleep: consciousness is what "begin[s] when we wake from a dreamless sleep". That anchor is disputed. Windt, Nielsen and Thompson (2016) argue that unless dreamless sleep is defined as unconscious from the outset, there is reason to think that experience occurs across sleep stages. On the master paper's state table, consciousness in deep NREM sleep is "probably absent (unconsciousness)", its brief up-states do not sustain the explicit models, and NREM dreaming belongs to non-deep NREM (Gruber, 2026, Section 5.3).

With the distinction in place, the sleeping human retains the capacity in full and runs the process intermittently and at reduced depth. What sleep removes first is the extended self-model, the rungs on which a self with a past and a future is represented. Non-deep sleep is the reportable state closest to the floor of minimal consciousness (Section 8.2); deep sleep is probably unconsciousness, with the capacity intact and the process absent. This is testable with the serial-awakening paradigm (Siclari et al., 2017), which separates reports of experience with content, experience without recall of content, and no experience. If deep sleep is unconsciousness, awakenings from deep NREM sleep should yield no experience reports beyond chance, while awakenings from non-deep sleep should yield experience, with experience without recalled content most frequent where the arousal threshold is highest. Serial awakening cannot separate the floor from absence, because the floor is unreportable; in a system whose connectome and running activity can be inspected, a signal traced to the closed self and world models can (Section 9).

Arousability cannot carry this claim. A thermostat responds to a change in temperature and a smoke detector to smoke; if responsiveness to a stimulus indicated consciousness, both would qualify. What distinguishes the sleeper is what the stimulus restarts. Within seconds of waking, a self-model returns that places the sleeper in a remembered past and an anticipated future and can model its own modelling. The capacity is measured by what the standing structure can generate when it runs, and responsiveness says nothing about that.

This also orders cases that the reference-fixer cannot order. A sleeping human has a greater capacity for consciousness than a waking insect. The current theory allows insects at most minimal consciousness, "if present" (Gruber, 2026, Section 6.4). Even on that generous reading, an insect's structure can generate at most the present moment with a primordial pain or a nothingness: no modelled past or future, and no model of its own modelling. A sleeping human's structure can generate all the rungs, and at a given moment its process may run lower than the insect's. The phenomenological pointer cannot separate "capacity intact, process paused" from "capacity absent", because it registers only what is reported as currently experienced. The distinction gives four cases:

| | Process running | Process paused, reduced or absent |
|---|---|---|
| **Capacity present** | waking human | deep NREM sleep; general anaesthesia with structural connectivity preserved |
| **Capacity absent** | no case, since the process presupposes the capacity | brain death; a thermostat |

Anaesthesia belongs in the upper right because the explicit models are suppressed while the implicit structure is preserved, and recovery after washout confirms suppression; the master paper cites propofol for this pattern (Gruber, 2026, Tables 1 and 1b).

## 6. The bridge

### 6.1 The objection in its strongest form

Grant the derivation of Sections 3 and 4. What has been defined is a process of self-modelling. To check that this process is consciousness, one needs an antecedent grip on consciousness, and that grip is phenomenological. Either "consciousness" in FMT is a new technical term, in which case FMT has changed the subject, or FMT is analysing the ordinary concept, in which case the phenomenological concept is presupposed and the charge of circularity returns. Either way the self-modelling definition reaches cognition and access and leaves phenomenality untouched.

### 6.2 Reference fixing and theoretical identification

The dilemma conflates the two kinds of definition distinguished in Section 2. Using the phenomenological pointer to fix the referent is legitimate, for FMT as for any theory, and it is not circular: a pointer is not offered as an analysis, so it does not need to avoid the definiendum. Searle says this of his own formulation. The master paper's opening sentence, "Consciousness is a specific kind of process: ongoing self-simulation" (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.1), makes the theoretical claim, and the referent it makes the claim about is the one the pointer fixes. The 2015 book, as shown in Section 4.3, used such a pointer as a check when it rejected the self-knowledge definition.

The FMT definition is a candidate theoretical definition of the referent the pointer fixes. It is not a stipulated replacement term: the 2015 text explicitly declines to coin one and keeps the ordinary word (p. 57). So the first horn does not apply. The second horn applies only if fixing a referent by a pointer makes every later theoretical definition circular, which would make "water is H₂O" circular as well. It is neither circular nor a change of subject. It is an identification hypothesis, and it can fail in two ways: the extension can come apart from the pointer's on cases where the pointer is undisputed, or the identification can fail to explain anything the pointer did not already contain. The first is checked case by case: waking against anaesthesia, dreaming against dreamless sleep, the clinical dissociations. The second is checked by explanatory yield. Water was identified with H₂O partly because the chemistry explained the features the reference-fixing description used, transparency and liquidity at ordinary temperatures. The corresponding demand here is that self-modelling explain the features the phenomenological pointer uses: that states are present for someone, that they seem private and ineffable, and that a gap appears between them and any physical description. For this the current theory offers its account of why the explanatory gap appears (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.4), its answer to the meta-problem (Section 3.8), and predictions not derivable from report (Section 8).

### 6.3 Every theory has such a step

The step from a definiens to the referent of the pointer is present in every theory of consciousness. Kleiner and Ludwig (2024) give a definition of a mathematical structure of conscious experience that holds "according to one's chosen concept of conscious experience", so the concept is a free parameter of the framework. Integrated information theory starts from axioms stated in the first person, "my experience here and now exists … is the only fact I can be absolutely sure of" (Tononi & Koch, 2015), and moves from them to postulates about physical substrates; the move from axioms to postulates is its bridge. FMT names its own. Its third principle, that phenomenology is the modelling closed on itself, is stated as an identity, "phenomenality is closure", and described as "the theory's single bridging commitment, and it is argued for rather than derived" (Gruber, 2026, Section 3). The premise is tested apart from its markers. In vivo, disrupting the self-modelling loop tests the markers of closure, since a recurrent network opened in one place closes again elsewhere; in silico, an architecture made return-free, where closure cannot relocate, tests the premise itself (Gruber, 2026, Sections 4.4 and 8.11). The claim that FMT's definition is not a definition of consciousness, if it rests on the presence of an underived identification, holds against every theory or against none.

## 7. What the phenomenological formulations presuppose

### 7.1 The pointers, unpacked

If the phenomenological formulations are read as reference-fixers, they are in order. If they are promoted to definitions, two things follow. Their definiens contains the definiendum, which fails C1. And the conditions under which they are applied to a case include a self-model, memory and report.

*Nagel (1974):* an organism has conscious mental states if and only if "there is something that it is like to be that organism, something it is like *for* the organism". The word "for" requires a point of view, and a point of view requires a represented distinction between the organism and the rest of what is represented. Stoljar (2016) rejects two common readings of the formula, that it is empty and that it requires the subject to be aware of being in the state, and analyses "what it is like for" as an affective relation between an event and an individual. On his analysis too, the formula cannot be applied without an individual to whom the state is related.

*Block (1995):* phenomenal consciousness "is experience". In ordinary use, "I experienced X" has an indexical subject ("I"), a tense (the experience is past by the time it is reported, so it is recalled) and a report. Block concedes that only pointing is possible, and pointing at one's own experience is itself a report.

*Searle (1998):* the definiens contains "unconscious". The boundaries it names, falling asleep and waking, are known to the subject only afterwards, as gaps in autobiographical memory; the onset of unconsciousness is never experienced.

*Integrated information theory:* the existence axiom asserts "my experience" from "its own intrinsic perspective" as the one certain fact (Tononi & Koch, 2015). It is a self-attribution, made by a system that represents itself as the bearer of the experience.

*Schwitzgebel (2016):* phenomenal consciousness is defined by example, as the most obvious feature shared by positive examples (sensory experience, imagery, emotion, dreams) and lacking in negative ones (hormone release, dispositional knowledge, reactions to masked stimuli). The definition avoids synonymy on purpose. The examples are sorted by introspective classification, and masked stimuli count as negative because they cannot be reported.

In each case, applying the formulation to a case runs through a subject that represents itself, remembers, and reports. That is the structure the FMT definition names.

### 7.2 Report is constructed afterwards

The 2015 book states the problem in its definitions chapter:

> wir akzeptieren nur als bewusste Wahrnehmung, wovon wir nachher auch berichten können. Wahrnehmungen, die sich sofort nach dem Wahrnehmungsvorgang wieder verflüchtigen, bezeichnen wir als „unterbewusst" oder „unbewusst", und da wir über diese nicht berichten können, sehen wir sie nicht als bewusst an, selbst wenn sie während dem Wahrnehmungsvorgang vielleicht sehr wohl kurz bewusst waren. (p. 59)
>
> *We accept as conscious perception only what we can report afterwards. Perceptions that vanish immediately we call subconscious or unconscious, and because we cannot report them we do not count them as conscious, even if they were briefly conscious while they occurred.*

It returns to the problem with a clinical case (p. 256). Under a high dose of a benzodiazepine a patient can remain more or less awake, yet afterwards reports nothing and says the period was not consciously experienced: "Einige phänomenologische Definitionen des Bewusstseins stoßen hier schon auf Schwierigkeiten" (some phenomenological definitions of consciousness already run into difficulty here). A person with anterograde amnesia from birth would never learn a language and could never report anything, so no consciousness could be demonstrated in them, "wie einem Tier", although their experience would correspond to conscious experience (p. 257).

The empirical literature agrees that reports are constructed. Nisbett and Wilson (1977) showed that verbal reports on higher-order mental processes rest on implicit causal theories and do not come from introspective access to those processes. Schwitzgebel (2008) documents broad unreliability in naive introspection of current experience. Heterophenomenology treats reports as data about what the subject believes about its experience (Dennett, 2003).

A definition that takes its cases from report takes them from the output of a self-model consulting memory and producing language, and so presupposes the extended rungs of the structure FMT defines. A definition built on phenomenology therefore depends on that structure for every case it classifies.

## 8. Objections

### 8.1 "Access only"

Report dependence has a limit as evidence. If every datum about consciousness reaches us through a self-model, memory and report, the association between reported consciousness and self-modelling is guaranteed by the method of collecting data. It cannot also count as evidence that consciousness is self-modelling. What Section 7 establishes is negative: no rival definition that takes its cases from report escapes the structure FMT names. The positive case for the identification rests on the explanatory yield described in Section 6.2.

The report argument might seem to concede that FMT's definition covers only reportable, accessible consciousness. It does not, because FMT takes a position on phenomenal content beyond report. On the current theory, "qualia are constitutive properties of the computational level", and "explicit content that enters the closed loop of self-model and world-model *is* phenomenal experience" (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.4.2 and Section 3.4.6; wording of the forthcoming version 21). For accessed content, access and phenomenal consciousness are "two descriptions of the same running process at different levels: access is the functional characterization, phenomenality is the processual reality". The theory also predicts phenomenality with little or no report: "minimal and basic consciousness (Section 3.5) can exist without global broadcasting, producing phenomenal experience with little or no reportability". On this account what Block (2007) calls overflow is "a thin simulation running below the threshold at which its contents reach the system's evaluative workspace" (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.4.6). The 2015 book makes the same judgement about the amnesic patient and the perceptions that were "kurz bewusst" yet unreportable.

The 2015 book also seems to place phenomenality outside its definition. The essence of consciousness is "nur das Selbstmodell, welches in die Verarbeitung von Sinnesinformationen eingebunden ist" (only the self-model that is integrated into the processing of sensory information), while the essence of phenomenological consciousness, of what "wir … uns im allgemeinen Sprachgebrauch zusätzlich zum reinen Selbstmodell unter dem Begriff des Bewusstseins vorstellen" (we imagine under the word consciousness in ordinary usage in addition to the bare self-model), is the qualia (p. 264). The same page says where the qualia come from: they arise because the brain "ein virtuelles Ich in einer virtuellen Welt simuliert" (simulates a virtual I in a virtual world). The addition is therefore the running simulation of the self-model within its world, which the current theory identifies as the explicit models. On this reading the 2015 separation is the one between capacity and process drawn in Section 5, and it does not put phenomenality outside the structure the definition names.

FMT's position on residue is therefore this. There is no phenomenal content outside the explicit models, and none outside the closed loop within them. There is phenomenal content inside the loop that does not reach report, because the self-model is too thin or its recursion too shallow for the system to take the content as an object. This separates FMT from illusionism. Strong illusionism holds that states with phenomenal properties do not exist and that introspection misrepresents functional states as having them (Frankish, 2016). FMT holds that the phenomenal properties are real at the level of the running simulation and absent from the substrate (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.4.5). It requires no micro-experience and cannot empirically exclude it. If panpsychism holds, one further ingredient is needed, a property of physics not yet known, and closure is what forms a subject from it, so the theory would answer the combination problem (Gruber, 2026, Section 4.3).

### 8.2 Why the self?

Why should self-modelling define consciousness, and not world-modelling or modelling as such? Metzinger (2020) proposes minimal phenomenal experience as the simplest form of consciousness, possibly without a self, modelled as a Bayesian representation of tonic alertness, and Windt et al. (2016) argue that experience may persist in dreamless sleep. If selfless experience exists, the self-model is not the defining feature.

There are three replies. The first concerns what "selfless" can mean. FMT distinguishes the basic self-model, which positions the system in a world without the system modelling itself as a subject, from the extended rungs, on which the system represents itself experiencing. The 2015 text already holds that non-extended consciousness implies "ein minimales Ich-Modell" (p. 62). A state in which nothing is represented as a self that experiences, but in which experience is still organised from one position and bounded against what is not the organism, has no extended self-model and does have a basic one. Phenomenal selflessness is the absence of the recursive rungs, which is not the absence of a self-model.

The second reply concerns evidence. The selfless character of such states is known from reports given afterwards, and these reports are produced by an extended self-model consulting memory. They can establish that no self was represented as experiencing. They cannot establish that no self/world partition was being computed.

The third reply concerns world-modelling as an alternative. A world-model without a self-model has no inside: the master paper's weather simulation computes level-specific properties that an external observer can describe completely, and nothing in it is a position from which the simulation meets itself (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.4.3). Phenomenality, on the theory, is modelling closed on itself, and a self-model entering the world-model's update is the minimal form of that closure. The 2015 construction arrives at the same point from the other side: interpreting a self-model requires a containing world-model (Section 4.2). Self-modelling and world-modelling are both required; the self-model is named in the definition because it is the component whose presence closes the loop.

The definition carries a commitment that could fail. On the position taken here, a floor of minimal consciousness lies beneath the basic self-model: a rudimentary closure, phenomenal, whose content is the now plus primordial pain or nothingness, close to what the animal-welfare literature calls sentience (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.5). Its self model is still a model of the organism itself, however simple. Reportability grows with recursion depth, since a report needs content taken as object, memory and language; the floor is conscious content with no recursion above it, so it is unreportable by definition. The definition implies that this floor is still organised around a self/world partition at the level of the organism, however thin. A phenomenal state with no such partition at all, shown by a method that does not depend on reports given afterwards, would count against the definition.

### 8.3 Trivial self-models

If the 2015 sentence admits minimal artificial consciousness "recht einfach", a thermostat with a model of its own setpoint might qualify. Section 4.5 gives the answer. The definition in current use requires free modelling, closure and a self model that models the system itself, and the master paper's analysis excludes self-monitoring without closure explicitly. A robot vacuum running simultaneous localisation and mapping fails: its "self" is a position, it has no implicit/explicit split, its algorithm is fixed, and it recruits no free compute (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.4.3). The 2015 threshold word "nicht-trivial" is replaced by these conditions, each with a proposed empirical handle: a novel-input probe for free modelling, dissociation designs with fixed decision criteria and no-report paradigms for closure in reporting subjects, and, where the system can be inspected, a trace of a signal to the closed self and world models (Gruber, 2026, Table 1b).

FMT has two limitations on these conditions. ## 10. Conclusion

The FMT definition of consciousness was derived in 2015 in a specific sense. Its terms are grounded in a chain of definitions that never uses consciousness: data, coding, information, knowledge without truth, interpretation, and reference systems identified with models and traceable to physical ones. Applied to a test case, the definition yields the minimal architecture of a self-model inside a world-model with a functional boundary between them. The choice of self-modelling as the defining feature is not derived; it is a theoretical identification of the referent that phenomenological pointers fix, of the same form as the identification of water with H₂O, and every theory of consciousness makes one. The phenomenological formulations fix that referent legitimately. Promoted to definitions, they contain their definiendum and select their cases through a self-model, memory and report, the structure FMT defines. Restoring the 2015 distinction between capacity and process lets the definition say that a sleeper retains the capacity for consciousness while the process runs low or stops, without counting the thermostat's responsiveness as evidence. The definition would be refuted by a felt state without any self/world partition, shown without reliance on later report, or by an extension that comes apart from the pointer's on undisputed cases.
