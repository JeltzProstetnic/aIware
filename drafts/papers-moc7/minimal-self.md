# Too Late to Report: Why First-Person Methods Cannot Reach the Minimal Self

**Matthias Gruber**\
Independent researcher · ORCID 0009-0005-9697-1665 · research@matthiasgruber.com

2026-10-30 · Preprint, version 3

---

## Abstract

First-person methods, micro-phenomenology included, cannot establish the structure of the minimal self. The minimal self is defined as pre-reflective: it is lived without being taken as an object. A report about it is a behavioural output, and producing that output requires the reporting system to represent its own state as an object, which is the reflective operation the minimal self is defined by lacking. The report is then reconstructed from memory, re-simulated during evocation and serialised into language. What reaches the transcript is a description of a reflective model of the minimal self, however skilled the interview. The core of the argument is Natorp's objection that reflection objectifies, stated within the Four-Model Theory of consciousness (FMT), on which the minimal self corresponds to the basic, non-recursive explicit self-model together with a floor of minimal consciousness (sentience), while reporting requires the recursive rungs of self-modelling above them. Micro-phenomenological reports carry accurate information about experiential content and procedure, generate hypotheses and can predict neural data. The claim concerns the pre-reflective self-structure: ubiquity of for-me-ness, agentive versus passive selfhood, selfless states. On these questions reports can neither confirm nor refute a position. The paper sets out third-person methods that bypass report and proposes a test, "minimal-self blindness", that adapts the choice-blindness design of Petitmengin et al. (2013) to covert manipulations of bodily self-location, ownership and agency. The thesis fails if, at matched manipulation strength, trained interviews gain as much in tracking the ownership or agency manipulation as in tracking a content manipulation.

**Keywords:** minimal self, pre-reflective self-consciousness, micro-phenomenology, introspection, self-model, first-person methods, no-report paradigms, for-me-ness

## 1. Introduction

Every first-person report about the minimal self is produced by a system operating one level above it. The minimal self, in the phenomenological tradition, is the pre-reflective, non-positional self-presence that accompanies experience without being an object of it (Gallagher 2000; Zahavi 2005). To report on one's own experience, a system must represent that experience as something it has, which is to take it as an object. The report is then assembled from memory and put into words. It follows that first-person methods, however refined, describe the explicit self-model's reconstruction of experience, and so they are the wrong instrument for the structure of the minimal self. They remain a good instrument for much else, including the content of experience.

At the seventh Models of Consciousness conference (MoC7, Copenhagen, October 2026), micro-phenomenology appeared as a growth area, combined with 7T imaging (Allen 2026), clinical trials and transformer-based analysis of interview corpora, and the same programme carried a session on the minimal self and the regress problem of self-awareness. Micro-phenomenology is increasingly offered as the method by which the structure of pre-reflective experience can be described with rigour (Petitmengin 2006; Petitmengin et al. 2019).

The core point is old. Natorp objected that reflection objectifies lived experience and so cannot give it as it was lived; Zahavi (1999, 2005) reconstructs the objection and answers it on Husserl's behalf, holding that reflection modifies what it discloses without falsifying it. Schooler (2002) gives a psychological version: reports issue from meta-consciousness, a re-representation of experience that can lag it or distort it in translation. Schear (2009) and Howell and Thompson (2017) doubt that for-me-ness is a ubiquitous phenomenal feature. What follows adds three things to this line. It assigns the minimal self and the reporting self to different levels of a specified self-modelling architecture, so that the conversion follows from where a report is generated. It divides report production into four stages, objectification, reconstruction, re-simulation and serialisation, of which the first suffices for the thesis and the other three add distortion by degree. And it applies the result to both sides of the disputes over the minimal self, removing report-based evidence from the case for ubiquitous for-me-ness and from the case against it alike. Unlike the empirical critiques of introspection (Nisbett & Wilson 1977; Schwitzgebel 2008), the objectification step holds for trained introspection, because it concerns what a report is, whatever the reporter's skill.

Experience is not thereby illusory: FMT is realist about phenomenal properties at the level of the running simulation (Gruber 2026, §3.4.5), which separates the position from Dennett's heterophenomenology (Dennett 2003). Nor are reports worthless or micro-phenomenology bad science. And the minimal self is not unknowable. It can be studied by inference from data that do not pass through the explicit self-model; Section 6 lists such methods.

## 2. The minimal self

Gallagher (2000) drew the distinction now standard in cognitive science between a minimal self, "consciousness of oneself as an immediate subject of experience, unextended in time", and a narrative self that is extended in time and constituted partly by the stories one tells. The minimal self carries a sense of ownership (this experience is mine) and a sense of agency (I am causing this movement), and the two can come apart, as in schizophrenic thought insertion, where a thought is experienced as one's own in location but not in authorship.

Zahavi (2005, 2014) develops the phenomenological version. Pre-reflective self-awareness is the "for-me-ness" or first-personal givenness of experience: every experience is given to someone, and that givenness is not one more item among the contents of experience. It is the subjective character of experience as such (Zahavi & Kriegel 2016). Zahavi also defends the Heidelberg claim that reflective self-awareness presupposes pre-reflective self-acquaintance: if self-awareness required reflection, the reflecting act would need its own reflection, and so on without end.

Legrand (2006, 2007) roots pre-reflective self-consciousness in sensorimotor integration, distinguishing self-as-subject from self-as-object. Christoff, Cosmelli, Legrand and Thompson (2011) draw a methodological consequence that is close to the present argument. Much of the cognitive neuroscience of the self, including the cortical midline literature (Northoff & Bermpohl 2004), uses tasks in which participants judge whether trait adjectives describe them, and so studies self-as-object and misses the pre-reflective self-as-subject, which they locate in sensorimotor self-specifying processes. Their point concerns task paradigms; Section 4 argues that it holds for reports as well.

Blanke and Metzinger (2009) define minimal phenomenal selfhood through three components: self-identification with a body, self-location in space and a first-person perspective. In full-body illusions these components are manipulated by visuotactile or cardio-visual stimulation and measured implicitly, as drift in self-location and as skin-conductance responses to threats directed at the illusory body (Lenggenhager et al. 2007; Ehrsson 2007; Park & Blanke 2019), without asking anyone about them.

Below this level, several authors posit a more basic affective core. Damasio (1999) distinguishes a non-conscious protoself, a transient core self that survives severe amnesia, and an autobiographical self. Panksepp (1998) locates a primary-process "SELF" in subcortical emotional circuits, and Panksepp and Northoff (2009) argue for a trans-species core self. Their evidence comes largely from animal and lesion studies. Baggio and Gallagher (2026) extend minimal pre-reflective self-awareness to non-human animals as "minimal self-sentience": a reflexive, automatic, affectively valenced bodily responsiveness through which an organism experiences its own interactions with the environment before any higher-order cognition or reflective act.

Whether the minimal self is ubiquitous in experience is disputed. Guillot (2017) separates three notions often run together: for-me-ness (experience is given to a subject), me-ness (the subject is aware of herself) and mineness (experience is given as belonging to the self). Schear (2009) argues from absorbed, skilled activity that not all experience involves pre-reflective self-consciousness, Howell and Thompson (2017) find no distinctive phenomenal mineness, and Zahavi (2014, 2018) replies to these critics. Letheby (2020), Letheby and Gerrans (2017) and Millière (2017) argue that psychedelic ego dissolution may be a case of experience without minimal selfhood. At MoC7, Lépingle (2026) argued that the minimal self is embodied but passive and affective, with the "I can" of agency a founded layer above it, and argued from dissociation cases where bodily self-presence survives a collapse of agency.

Most of the evidence in this dispute, on both sides, is retrospective report.

## 3. Micro-phenomenology at its best

Varela (1996) proposed neurophenomenology as a research programme in which disciplined first-person descriptions and third-person neural data constrain each other. Vermersch (1999) treated introspection as a practice that can be trained and improved without first possessing a complete theory of it. Depraz, Varela and Vermersch (2003) described "becoming aware" as three gestures: suspension of habitual attitudes, redirection of attention from the object of experience to the way it is given, and letting-come of what was lived but not yet noticed. They describe becoming aware as a transformation of experience, so the method was never built on the assumption that reflection leaves its object untouched.

Petitmengin (2006) set out the micro-phenomenological interview in its current form. The interviewer helps the participant stabilise attention on a single, singular past episode; guides them into "evocation", a re-living of the episode that is checked through linguistic and non-verbal markers; asks about the how of the experience and avoids questions of what and why; and uses "content-empty" questions that do not suggest an answer. The aim is the pre-reflective, procedural dimension of experience: what a person was doing inwardly while performing a task, which they could not have said unprompted. Petitmengin, Remillieux and Valenzuela-Moguillansky (2019) describe the analysis by which generic diachronic and synchronic structures are extracted from sets of such interviews.

The method also has a theory of its own validity. Petitmengin and Bitbol (2009) argue that the classical critiques of introspection, from Comte to Nisbett and Wilson, rest on a poor understanding of the introspective process, and propose that the validity of a first-person description be assessed by its authenticity, meaning that it issues from evocation of a singular experience, and its coherence, meaning its internal consistency and its consistency with other descriptions, in place of correspondence with an independent fact (see also Bitbol & Petitmengin 2013, 2017).

Descriptive Experience Sampling (DES) is a separate method with a different timing. A random beep interrupts daily life; the participant notes the "pristine" experience ongoing at the moment of the beep, and an expositional interview follows within a day. Iterative training is used to bracket presuppositions (Hurlburt & Akhter 2006; Hurlburt & Heavey 2006; Hurlburt 2011; for a proponent-and-sceptic test on real samples, Hurlburt & Schwitzgebel 2007).

The methods have empirical successes. Lutz, Lachaux, Martinerie and Varela (2002) used trained first-person reports of preparation before a stereoscopic task to sort trials into clusters, and the clusters had distinct patterns of EEG synchrony that would otherwise have been averaged away. Petitmengin, Remillieux, Cahour and Carter-Thomas (2013) repeated the choice-blindness protocol of Johansson et al. (2005), in which most participants fail to notice that the photograph they are asked to justify is not the one they chose, and showed that participants guided by a micro-phenomenological interview detected the manipulation in 80% of cases. Their conclusion was that people are usually unaware of their decision processes but can reach them through specific mental acts. At MoC7, Allen (2026) reported that micro-phenomenological interviews on a somatosensory and motor timing task revealed experiential dimensions of sound and speech the design did not contain, and that pre-registered hypotheses derived from them predicted auditory and verbal activity where such experience was reported, with preliminary support in 49 participants. This follows Gallagher's (2003) proposal of "front-loading" phenomenology into experimental design (see also Gallagher & Sørensen 2006).

Training also improves introspective accuracy on bodily sensitivity tasks (Fox et al. 2012), and finer report scales reveal graded experience that binary scales hide (Ramsøy & Overgaard 2004).

## 4. The architecture of a report

### 4.1 The Four-Model Theory in brief

FMT (Gruber 2026; first formulated in Gruber 2015) holds that consciousness is constituted by ongoing self-simulation. It distinguishes four model kinds along two axes: scope (world or self) and mode (implicit or explicit). The implicit world model and implicit self model are learned knowledge stored in the substrate's connectivity; they are never directly conscious. The implicit self model holds body schema, proprioceptive calibration, motor skills and autobiographical memory structures. The explicit world model and explicit self model (ESM) are generated from the implicit models and current input as transient activity patterns, and their content is phenomenal when it enters the self-model's closed loop: phenomenality is the modelling closed on itself (§3.4.3). The four are extremal points of one continuous modelling ecology in a single substrate. They are not four modules.

FMT grades consciousness along two axes, richness of content and depth of recursive self-modelling (Gruber 2026, §3.5). At the floor of both, minimal consciousness, the explicit world and self models close on each other with no discernible content. Basic consciousness sits at the same depth and higher on the content axis: the system generates an explicit world model and a rudimentary, non-recursive ESM, experiences a world with discernible qualities and has a minimal sense of being a subject in it, but the self-model does not take itself as an object. At simply extended consciousness the ESM includes a model of the system's own states and processes; the system experiences and also represents that it experiences. Doubly and triply extended consciousness add further orders of self-modelling. FMT identifies these rungs with what higher-order theories call higher-order representation, and holds that the minimal and basic levels are phenomenal without one (§7.2). The levels are points on a continuum, and an individual moves among them with state. FMT describes the onset of a lucid dream as this crossing: the dreaming ESM goes from experiencing as an agent to experiencing that it is an agent (§6.3).

FMT also states what converts content that is present in experience into content the system can interrogate (§3.4.6). At the minimal and basic levels, self-content can be present in the explicit models without the system being able to grasp, examine or manipulate it. Reportability begins when the self-model maps and operates on its own content, so that the system can take that content as an object, and each rung adds one degree of such self-interaction. Reportability is added recursive depth, with no separate broadcast stage. FMT distinguishes this depth from the capacity of the narrow channel through which the explicit models read and write the high-dimensional implicit models (§3.6.1): rank sets how much the simulation holds at once, while depth sets whether the simulation can take its own contents as an object.

### 4.2 Where the minimal self sits

On this architecture the minimal self of the phenomenological tradition is the basic, non-recursive ESM together with the floor of minimal consciousness on which it rests. The basic ESM is phenomenal, it is a sense of being a subject in a world, and it does not take itself as an object. Its source material is the implicit self model, which is never conscious and so cannot itself be the minimal self. The floor (Gruber 2026, §3.5) is a rudimentary closure, consciousness at its minimum, whose content is the now plus a primordial pain or a nothingness, with no reflection, no modelled past or future and no discernible qualia. Its self model is still a model of the system, so the floor keeps an implicit boundary between self and world, though nothing in it represents a self (§7.2). It is the closest counterpart to what the animal-welfare literature calls sentience. Reportability grows with recursion depth, since a report needs content taken as object, memory and language (§3.4.6); the floor has no recursion above it and no discernible content to take as an object, so it is unreportable by definition. Deep sleep is probably unconsciousness; non-deep NREM sleep is the reportable state closest to the floor (§6.3). The floor corresponds to Panksepp's primary-process core and plausibly to the passive, affective layer Lépingle identifies.

Baggio and Gallagher's (2026) minimal self-sentience is the nearest published counterpart to this floor, and the two accounts agree that a felt, valenced self-relation precedes any reflective act. Baggio and Gallagher locate it in bodily responsiveness arising from sensorimotor coupling with the environment, and criticise Humphrey for tying sentience to recursive loops. FMT requires a closure, a self model that enters the world model's update rule and is entered by it, which needs no recursive depth; responsiveness without such a self model does not qualify (§3.5). Baggio and Gallagher make no claim about report. The argument below needs only that the minimal self sits below the first reflective rung, which both the basic ESM and the floor satisfy.

The mapping is summarised as follows.

| Phenomenological term | FMT locus |
|---|---|
| Sentience; candidate "selfless" states | Minimal consciousness: rudimentary closure of world and self models, no discernible content, no represented self |
| Minimal, pre-reflective self; for-me-ness | Basic consciousness: rudimentary, non-recursive ESM with discernible content, resting on the floor |
| Reflective self-awareness; the self that reports | Extended consciousness: ESM modelling the system's own states |
| Narrative self | ESM self-narrative, extended in time |
| Source material for all of the above | Implicit self model, never conscious |

### 4.3 Step one: reporting requires objectification

Every first-person datum, whether an interview transcript, a DES description, a questionnaire item or a confidence rating, is a behavioural output of the reporting system. A report about one's own experience requires that the system represent its own state as a state it is in. In FMT's terms, the ESM must include a model of the system's own states, which is the definition of simply extended consciousness. A system running only the basic ESM has a self-perspective but has nothing that represents the self-perspective; it can experience as a subject and cannot represent that it is doing so. It therefore has no representation from which a report about the self-perspective could be generated.

The minimal self is defined by not being taken as an object (Zahavi 2005; Zahavi & Kriegel 2016). The two definitions together give the first conclusion. Any report about the minimal self is generated from an extended-level model of it. Producing the report changes the level at which the self is running, and the report describes the higher level's model of the lower one.

Guillot's (2017) taxonomy gives this a precise form. For-me-ness is a structural feature of how experience is given. Me-ness is awareness of the self, which places the self among the contents of experience. Reporting converts the first into the second, because what the reporting system describes is a self that has been made into content. A report saying "there was a sense that the experience was mine" is a report of me-ness or mineness, produced by a model in which the self figures as an object, about an earlier state in which, on the phenomenologist's own definition, it did not.

The introspection literature discusses a version of this under the name of the refrigerator-light problem (see Schwitzgebel 2007): opening the door to check whether the light is off turns it on. In the case of the minimal self, the looking is done by the same system that is being looked at, and the act of looking is the change of level. Schooler's (2002) meta-consciousness, the intermittent re-representation of experience from which reports issue, is the psychological name for this step; the extended ESM specifies what the re-representation is and where it runs. Schooler treats the dissociation between experience and its re-representation as something that occurs on occasion, in mind-wandering or verbal overshadowing. For the pre-reflective self-structure it occurs every time, because the re-representation is what the structure is defined by lacking.

### 4.4 Step two: memory reconstructs

Micro-phenomenological interviews are retrospective. The episode under study is usually minutes to hours old, sometimes older. The report is therefore built from a memory trace, and recall is reconstructive: it fills gaps with schemas (Bartlett 1932) and is sensitive to the wording of the question (Loftus & Palmer 1974). The interview's content-empty questions reduce suggestion, and evocation is designed to reinstate the episode in its singularity. Neither makes retrieval non-reconstructive.

FMT adds a specific point about what a memory of the minimal self could contain. Consolidation writes into the implicit models what passed through the explicit models. If the episode was lived at the basic level, the trace holds the episode's contents together with whatever self-specifying information the implicit self model encodes (body schema, posture, interoceptive state). It holds no reflective representation of the minimal self, because none was formed. Whatever reflective structure the report attributes to the self in that episode must be generated at retrieval.

DES shortens the delay by sampling at a beep; describing the pristine moment still requires taking it as an object.

### 4.5 Step three: evocation re-simulates

Evocation, the core technique of the micro-phenomenological interview, asks the participant to re-live the episode: to return to the place, the posture, the sensations, until the episode is present again (Petitmengin 2006). On FMT this is a new run of the simulation, generated from the implicit models with the past episode as the target. The explicit models are not stored anywhere; they are generated each time from what the substrate holds (Gruber 2026, §3.2). The participant, now attending to their own experience at an interviewer's invitation, runs the ESM at an extended level while the episode is re-generated.

The re-lived episode can carry accurate content: what was seen, the sequence of inner actions, the location of a sensation. What it cannot carry is the original's self-structure, because the self-structure of the re-simulation is that of a reflecting subject attending to its own past. The micro-phenomenologist's evocation markers (slowed speech, unfocused gaze, present tense) certify that re-simulation is taking place. They cannot certify that the self-structure of the re-simulation matches the self-structure of the original.

### 4.6 Step four: language serialises

A report is a sequence of words produced through a narrow channel from a high-dimensional state. Serialisation compresses: the reader of a report reconstructs a model of what the speaker intended (Gruber 2015), and two very different states can yield the same sentence. Serialisation can also write back. Describing a face in words impairs later recognition of that face (Schooler & Engstler-Schooler 1990), so that the act of verbal description changes the memory it describes. A micro-phenomenological interview typically runs for an hour or more on a single episode, describing the same moments repeatedly in increasingly fine vocabulary, and every round can overwrite the trace.

The ESM also fills gaps fluently. The split-brain interpreter explains actions initiated by the disconnected hemisphere (Gazzaniga 2000), and in choice blindness justifications for manipulated and non-manipulated trials are linguistically almost indistinguishable (Johansson et al. 2006; Hall, Johansson & Strandberg 2012). On FMT the ESM maintains a continuous self-narrative by confabulating across gaps, on waking, after anaesthesia and wherever its sources are incomplete (Gruber 2026, §4.2.2). The confabulating ESM and the reporting ESM are one process.

### 4.7 The conclusion of the architectural argument

A first-person method samples the extended-level ESM's re-simulated, verbally serialised model of an earlier frame. At its best, with a trained participant, a skilled interviewer, authentic evocation and coherent descriptions, it yields an accurate description of a reflective model of the minimal self. These are data about the reporting system: how the reflective self-model represents its own pre-reflective layer. It cannot settle claims about the structure of the pre-reflective layer itself, such as whether for-me-ness accompanies all experience, whether minimal selfhood is agentive or passive, or whether selfless experience occurs, unless it is anchored to evidence that does not pass through the explicit self-model.

The empirical critiques of introspection reach a convergent conclusion by a different route. Nisbett and Wilson (1977) showed that people explain their behaviour by applying causal theories; Schwitzgebel (2008) argued that naive introspection is unreliable even about current experience; Irvine (2012, 2021) argued that subjective measures inherit these problems and that, treated as measurements, introspective reports cannot carry a strong justificatory role. Bayne and Spener (2010) and Spener (2015) hold that introspection can be trusted only where it can be calibrated against independent measures. The present argument gives a reason why one domain resists that calibration from the inside. Calibration through report would require a report that reaches the pre-reflective layer, and that is the thing every report lacks.

The architectural argument also predicts which features of experience reports will distort. Features that consist in not being objectified, such as for-me-ness, perspectival origin, the absence of a subject-object split and the temporal thickness of the lived present, should fare worst. Contents of the explicit world model, and self-content that is already object-like (the felt location of a sensation, the posture of the body, the sequence of inner actions), should fare best. Training should help the second group substantially and the first group little. Section 7 turns this into an experiment.

## 5. Replies

**R1. Micro-phenomenology is not naive introspection.** Training, second-person guidance, evocation, the focus on how, and the bracketing of theory answer the critiques of Nisbett and Wilson and of Schwitzgebel, which concern untrained reports. This reply is decisive against the empirical critiques. The architectural argument concerns what a report is and holds for a perfectly skilled reporter: training improves the model produced at the extended level, and producing a report still requires that level.

**R2. The method exists to reach the pre-reflective, and pre-reflective means lived but unnoticed, which is still conscious.** This is the core claim of the method, and much of it is correct. FMT distinguishes content that is present in experience from content that is reportable (§3.4.6), and the micro-phenomenological interview is a technique for bringing present content into report. It works for contents that were lived pre-reflectively: an inner strategy, the location of a sensation, a fleeting image. Such contents become objects of the reflective model and can be described accurately, because being an object does not change what they are. FMT supplies the mechanism of the transformation Depraz, Varela and Vermersch (2003) describe: a step up the recursive ladder. For the pre-reflective self-structure, whose defining property is that it is not an object, the step up changes what it is.

**R3. Validity is authenticity and coherence, not correspondence.** Petitmengin and Bitbol (2009) set aside the demand that descriptions match an independent fact. Claims about the structure of the minimal self are correspondence claims: that for-me-ness is present in every experience, that some experiences lack it, that minimal selfhood includes a sense of agency. If validity is authenticity and coherence, an authentic and coherent description can still fail to correspond, and the method's own theory of validity does not license it to settle these claims. A coherent body of reflective models of the minimal self is a coherent body of reflective models.

**R4. Micro-phenomenology defeats choice blindness.** This is the strongest empirical reply (Petitmengin et al. 2013). The manipulated feature in choice blindness is content of the explicit world model: which face was chosen, which features it had. Detecting the swap means detecting a mismatch between two explicit models, the remembered choice and the presented photograph, which the reflective self-model can do once the interview directs attention to the choice; unguided participants mostly fail at it. The result shows that guided interviews improve access to decision content and process. It does not show access to the pre-reflective self-structure. Section 7 proposes the experiment that would.

**R5. Micro-phenomenological reports predict brain data.** Lutz et al. (2002) and Allen (2026) show that reports carry information with neural correlates. What Allen's interviews revealed in a non-verbal task was sound and speech: content of the explicit models, and language-adjacent content at that. Neither study is evidence about the structure of minimal selfhood, and neither was designed to be.

**R6. The argument contradicts itself.** On the Heidelberg argument (Zahavi 2005), reflection presupposes pre-reflective self-acquaintance, so any reflection that denies access to the pre-reflective self depends on that self in the act of denying. FMT agrees that reflection presupposes the minimal self: the extended ESM is generated on top of a running basic ESM. That is the Heidelberg founding relation in architectural terms, and Frank's (2022) separation of an anonymous pre-reflective self-consciousness, with no subject–object structure, from the egological self-consciousness it founds draws the same line. Founding does not confer descriptive access. A higher layer can depend on a lower one in every operation and still represent it only as an object. Formal treatments presented at MoC7 resolve the regress without giving reflection that access: Williford (2026) models pre-reflective and reflective self-consciousness with non-well-founded sets, and Kawakami (2026) formalises Zahavi's retentional solution by guarding each self-reference with a one-step delay, so that reflection operates on the retained, just-past phase of the stream. On the present account the retained phase is still received by the extended ESM as an object, so retention shortens the delay, as DES does, without removing the change of level.

**R7. Reflection need not objectify.** This is Zahavi's answer to Natorp (Zahavi 1999, 2005): reflection modifies the experience it thematises, but it discloses something that was already there, and an accompanying, non-objectifying reflection can make the lived experience explicit without turning it into a detached object. The question here is what a report can be generated from. Either the accompanying awareness contains a representation of the self-perspective as something the system has, in which case it is the extended level in FMT's sense, whatever it feels like from inside, and the conversion of Section 4.3 has occurred; or it contains no such representation, in which case nothing about the self-perspective is available to drive a report. Zahavi's further point, that the modification introduced by reflection can itself be noticed, is answered as the clinical case below is: noticing that a reflective model has changed is a comparison between reflective models.

**R8. Training brings hidden items into view.** Morris (2025) argues that much apparently unconscious high-level cognition is preconscious, missed through internal inattentional blindness, and would become reportable with attention, which makes introspection training a research programme. For contents and processes this fits the present account and the successes of Section 3. FMT separates attention, which selects what enters the narrow channel between implicit and explicit models, from recursive depth, which lets the system take what the channel holds as an object (§3.6.1). A preconscious item is a content waiting for selection. The pre-reflective self-structure is the way contents are given, so there is no item to select: attending to it builds a model of it at the next rung. Training should therefore extend what reports reach about contents and procedure and leave the self-structure where it is, which is the asymmetry Section 7 measures.

A further consequence applies to both sides of the dispute over whether minimal selfhood is ubiquitous. The arguments that psychedelic ego dissolution or minimal phenomenal experience are selfless rest on retrospective reports (Letheby 2020; Millière 2017), as do the reports of self-perception from objectless awareness in dreamless sleep collected by micro-phenomenological interview (Alcaraz-Sánchez 2023). So does much of the case for and against ubiquitous for-me-ness (Zahavi 2014; Schear 2009; Howell & Thompson 2017). If reports cannot reach the pre-reflective self-structure, they can neither establish ubiquitous for-me-ness nor refute it. A report that "there was no self" is produced by a reflective self-model describing, after the fact, a state it represents as selfless. Whether the earlier state lacked minimal selfhood, or lacked a represented self, is a question the report cannot decide. FMT's own reading is the second: at the floor nothing represents a self, so a report can find nothing there to describe as one (Gruber 2026, §7.2). Third-person evidence can decide. FMT predicts a minimal but non-zero signature of self-model activity, measured independently of the default mode network, during verified episodes of minimal phenomenal experience, where the strict selfless reading predicts none (§7.2); the raised neural entropy reported for such episodes, which Lopez-Sola (2026) reinterprets through complexity measures, is a further handle.

Clinical phenomenology presents a harder case. Interviews such as the Examination of Anomalous Self-Experience detect disturbances of ipseity in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, and these disturbances have diagnostic value (Sass & Parnas 2003). On the present account, such interviews detect that the reflective self-model's representation of the self has changed. A change can be visible from a level that cannot recover what changed, as one can see that a photograph is blurred without being able to recover the scene. That answer is partial (Section 8).

## 6. What reports deliver, and what reaches the minimal self

### 6.1 What first-person reports deliver

The argument removes one thing from first-person reports, the role of ground truth for the pre-reflective self-structure. What remains is substantial.

1. *Content.* Trained reports recover what was seen, heard and felt and in what order, accurately enough to predict neural data (Lutz et al. 2002; Petitmengin & Lachaux 2013; Allen 2026).
2. *Procedure.* The inner steps of a task, which participants cannot state unprompted, can be elicited (Petitmengin 2006), and the resulting categories can sort trials for neural analysis (Lutz et al. 2002).
3. *Hypotheses.* Front-loaded phenomenology yields hypotheses that measures independent of report then test (Gallagher 2003; Gallagher & Sørensen 2006; Allen 2026).
4. *Trial classification.* Reports split trials into seen and unseen, present and absent, so that neural or behavioural measures can be contrasted, provided the report format is chosen with its route of access in mind (Overgaard & Sandberg 2012).
5. *Mismatch detection.* Reports are good at noticing that two explicit models differ, as in guided detection of choice-blindness swaps (Petitmengin et al. 2013).
6. *Calibrated domains.* Where an independent measure exists, the reliability of report can be quantified and trusted to that degree (Bayne & Spener 2010; Spener 2015).
7. *The reporting system.* How the reflective self-model represents its own lower layer, which vocabulary and theory it brings, and how both change with training (Schwitzgebel 2011; Fox et al. 2012). On FMT this is the extended ESM's model of the basic ESM, an object of study in its own right.

None of the seven extends to structural claims about the pre-reflective layer: whether for-me-ness is ubiquitous, whether the minimal self is agentive or passive, whether selfless experience occurs.

### 6.2 Methods that do not pass through the report

The methods that reach the minimal self share one principle: manipulate or measure it along a channel that does not require the participant to model themselves as an object, and treat any report as one more channel whose generator, the extended ESM working on memory through language, is known. The following are available.

**Implicit bodily-self measures under covert manipulation.** Full-body and rubber-hand illusions alter self-identification and self-location, and the alteration can be measured as drift in self-location, as skin-conductance responses to threats directed at the illusory body and as changes in heartbeat-evoked potentials (Lenggenhager et al. 2007; Ehrsson 2007; Park & Blanke 2019). These measures index components of minimal phenomenal selfhood without asking.

**No-report and no-cognition paradigms.** Optokinetic nystagmus and pupil size track perceptual alternations in binocular rivalry without report, and when report is removed, frontal activity associated with introspection and action drops out (Frässle et al. 2014; Tsuchiya et al. 2015). Related designs separate the correlates of perception from the correlates of reporting (Pitts, Metzler & Hillyard 2014) and decode transitions of conscious content from prefrontal activity in the absence of report (Kapoor et al. 2022). Overgaard and Fazekas (2016) questioned whether no-report designs isolate the true correlates of consciousness, and Block (2019) argued that removing report does not stop participants thinking about their experience, so that a no-cognition condition is needed. Silent reflection may engage the extended ESM as fully as spoken report, so for the minimal self the strict design has an implicit or physiological readout and no task directed at the participant's own experience.

**Perturbational complexity.** The perturbational complexity index measures, without report, the capacity of cortex to sustain complex responses to direct stimulation, and it separates conscious from unconscious states (Massimini et al. 2005; Casali et al. 2013). It can be applied in contrasts that remove the extended ESM while sparing the basic level: lucid against non-lucid REM sleep, ketamine against propofol, deep meditative absorption against rest.

**Serial awakening scored against physiology.** Serial-awakening designs distinguish three outcomes: experience with recall of content, experience without recall of content, and no experience (Siclari et al. 2017; Windt, Nielsen & Thompson 2016). The middle category already separates having experienced from being able to say what was experienced. If non-deep NREM sleep is the reportable state closest to the floor and deep sleep is unconsciousness, awakenings from deep NREM sleep should yield no experience reports beyond chance, while in non-deep NREM sleep "experience without recall" should rise with arousal threshold, a re-analysis of existing data.

**Lesion and dissociation logic.** Anosognosia for hemiplegia is the clearest clinical case in which the reporting self is wrong about the subject's own body. On FMT, the implicit self model registers the deficit and the ESM does not receive it (Gruber 2026, §3.6). Fotopoulou et al. (2010) found that anosognosic patients who explicitly denied that sentences about their paralysis applied to them were nonetheless slowed in suppressing completions of those sentences, a dissociation between implicit and explicit awareness of the deficit. The sincere report and the implicit measure disagree, and the implicit measure tracks the fact. Split-brain confabulation, thought insertion and depersonalisation supply further dissociations, and Lépingle's case for a passive, affective minimal self was built from them.

**Physiological measurement of altered self-states.** Ego dissolution and minimal phenomenal experience can be studied through complexity, entropy and self-model markers (Section 5), with reports as one dependent variable whose relation to physiology is modelled.

**In-silico ablation.** In an artificial system with an explicit self-model and a separable component that generates reports, the state of the basic self-model can be read directly and compared with what the report component produces. This is the one setting in which the ground truth about the lower self-model is available, so the distortion introduced by reporting can be measured as a function of recursion depth and channel bandwidth. This would extend the in-silico program of Gruber (2026, §8.12), which does not yet include a report component. The point concerns the reporting channel; nothing about the method assumes that such a system is conscious. Where the running system can be inspected, FMT's white-box trace, which follows a signal to a self model closed with the world model, gives a verdict on the floor that needs no report (§3.5, Table 1b).

## 7. A test: minimal-self blindness

The architectural argument makes a differential prediction. If reporting converts for-me-ness into me-ness, then training in first-person methods should improve the accuracy of reports about experiential content much more than it improves the accuracy of reports about the pre-reflective self-structure. The design below adapts the protocol of Petitmengin et al. (2013).

**Design.** A within-participant study with two manipulation arms and two or three report conditions.

The content arm repeats a choice-blindness manipulation: a covert change to a feature of a stimulus the participant has chosen, presented for justification.

The minimal-self arm manipulates three features covertly. Self-location and self-identification are manipulated with the cardio-visual variant of the full-body illusion, in which a virtual body flashes in or out of time with the participant's heartbeat and the heartbeat signal is supplied surreptitiously (Aspell et al. 2013; Park & Blanke 2019). The cardio-visual variant is preferred to visuotactile stroking because asynchronous stroking is visible, and detecting it would be detecting content. Sense of agency is manipulated by inserting a variable delay between a voluntary key press and its visual effect, kept below each participant's piloted threshold for detecting the delay itself, with intentional binding, the compression of perceived time between action and effect, as the implicit marker (Haggard, Clark & Kalogeras 2002). Binding is taken here as an index of what Synofzik, Vosgerau and Newen (2008) call the feeling of agency; any verbal report of agency is what they call a judgement of agency. Implicit markers are recorded throughout: self-location drift, skin-conductance response to threat, heartbeat-evoked potential, intentional binding.

The report conditions are an untrained retrospective report and a full micro-phenomenological interview conducted by a certified interviewer. An immediate DES-style probe can be added as a third condition if resources allow.

**Prediction.** The gain from the micro-phenomenological interview over untrained report will be ordered. It will be largest for the content manipulation, replicating Petitmengin et al. (2013); intermediate for self-location, the feature closest to the object-like self-content of Section 4.7; and smallest for self-identification and agency, which are features of the pre-reflective self-structure. In the minimal-self arm, interview reports will track the implicit markers trial by trial less closely than they track the change in the content arm. Their content will converge on the participant's prior beliefs about selfhood and on the descriptive vocabulary of the interview itself, which can be scored with topic-modelling tools of the kind now applied to first-person reports (Beauté 2026). The implicit markers will show that the manipulation took effect in both arms.

**Refutation.** Two quantities are fixed in the pre-registration: the interview's gain in detection sensitivity (d′) over untrained report, and the trial-wise correlation between report and implicit marker, each with an equivalence margin. If, with manipulation strength matched on the implicit markers, the gain for self-identification or agency falls within the margin of the gain for content, and the report-marker correlation is not lower, the thesis is false for that feature. Prior beliefs about selfhood are measured by questionnaire before the session, so that a match produced by belief can be separated from a match produced by tracking. An ordering in which self-location fares worse than self-identification also counts against the account, since it reverses the predicted order.

**Design risk.** If the implicit markers show a weaker effect in the minimal-self arm, a weaker report effect is uninterpretable, so pilot work must calibrate the arms on the implicit markers before the report comparison is run, and the list of which features count as content, which as object-like self-content and which as self-structure must be fixed in the pre-registration. Without that list, any reportable feature could be reclassified as content after the fact. A residual asymmetry cannot be fully removed: in the content arm the swapped photograph is in front of the participant at the time of report, while in the minimal-self arm the changed feature is a past felt state. Withdrawing the content stimulus before the interview reduces the asymmetry, at the cost of departing from the protocol of Petitmengin et al. (2013).

The study uses established paradigms and the best version of the method under test, and requires a laboratory with both micro-phenomenological and bodily-illusion expertise.

## 8. Limits

**Performative contradiction.** FMT was itself developed partly from introspection (Gruber 2015), and the floor of minimal consciousness invoked above is in origin the author's own intuition. Serial-awakening data are reports as well. Reports are outputs of a known generator; like any instrument whose response function is understood, they can be modelled and combined with measures that do not share their generator. The claims made here about the minimal self rest on architecture, implicit and physiological data and dissociations. A residue remains: the existence of a minimal self, as distinct from its structure, is motivated partly by reflective intuition.

**Falsifiability.** Cohen and Dennett (2011) argue that experiences unreportable in principle and functionally inert are scientifically unknowable, so that a theory positing them cannot be tested. The floor is unreportable by definition, and the self-structure of the basic level is unreportable as structure, so the charge applies. Neither layer is functionally inert on FMT: the closed self model enters the world model's update rule and guides behaviour, and the bodily-self measures of Section 6.2 index it without report. The account also names third-person outcomes that would count against it: no self-model signature during verified minimal phenomenal experience (Section 5), and, in inspectable systems that also report, a white-box trace that disagrees with no-report markers of experience (Gruber 2026, Table 1b). Taken alone, the objectification step is conceptual: that a report is generated at the extended level follows from the two definitions. The empirical claim is that this change of level costs accuracy for self-structure and not for content. That claim forbids trained reports tracking a covert ownership or agency manipulation as well as a matched content manipulation, and Section 7 states the margins by which that outcome would be measured.

**The content-structure line.** The argument depends on separating content from pre-reflective self-structure. Some self-related features, such as felt self-location and body posture, may be partly reportable as content of the explicit models. If the line is drawn after the data, it can absorb any result. Section 7 therefore fixes it in advance and predicts an ordering across it.

**Phenomenology is not introspection.** Zahavi (2005) and others hold that claims about for-me-ness are claims about the invariant structure of experience, reached by phenomenological analysis, and not reports of particular episodes. The present argument concerns reports used as data. It applies once the ubiquity of for-me-ness is put to empirical test, as in the dispute over psychedelic and meditative states, where the evidence on both sides is reports.

**Clinical phenomenology.** The detection of ipseity disturbance through interview (Sass & Parnas 2003) is answered only partly by the claim that interviews detect a change in the reflective model. If changes in the pre-reflective layer can be read off reliably from changes in the reflective model of it, the reflective model carries some structural information about the layer below, and the gap is narrower than argued here. How much narrower is an empirical question.

**Relation to Dennett.** Heterophenomenology also treats reports as texts to be interpreted (Dennett 2003, 2007). The present position is realist about experience at the level of the running simulation, so the reports describe something real, and it says where the distortion enters, which lets it predict which features will be distorted.

**Dependence on FMT.** The mapping of the minimal self onto the basic ESM is FMT's. A reader who rejects FMT can still accept the first step of the argument if they accept two premises: that reporting on one's own state requires representing it as an object, and that the minimal self is defined by not being represented as an object. The second is standard in the phenomenological literature. The first is Natorp's premise, which Zahavi (1999, 2005) contests (R7); a reader who accepts that reflection discloses the pre-reflective structure without distorting it will reject the first step. FMT adds the levels at which the two operations occur and the mechanism that relates them.

**Method diversity.** Micro-phenomenology, DES, explicitation interviewing and questionnaires differ in timing. The memory step bears most on retrospective interviews and least on DES; the objectification step applies to all equally.

## 9. Conclusion

A report about the minimal self is produced by the reflective self-model, from a reconstructed memory, during a re-simulation, through language. Each stage is well documented on its own; together they mean that the report describes a reflective model of the minimal self. Training improves that model and can make it accurate about experiential content. It cannot make a reflective model pre-reflective. The questions that divide the literature on the minimal self, including whether for-me-ness is ubiquitous, whether minimal selfhood is agentive and whether selfless experience occurs, therefore need third-person evidence: covert bodily-self manipulations measured implicitly, no-report and no-cognition designs, perturbational complexity, serial awakening scored against physiology, dissociations, and artificial systems in which the lower self-model can be read directly. First-person methods keep what Section 6.1 lists: content, procedure, hypotheses, trial classification, mismatch detection, calibrated domains and data about the reporting system. The experiment in Section 7 can show whether the gap between content and self-structure exists in the form the architecture predicts.
