# Pointing Is Not Defining: The Definition of Consciousness Is Not Settled

**Matthias Gruber**\
Independent Researcher\

## Abstract

It is widely held that the explanandum of consciousness science is adequately fixed by phenomenological characterisation (Nagel's "something it is like" and its kin), so that no further definition is needed before theories are compared, and functional or architectural formulations are to be judged as hypotheses about that fixed target. This holds only for the uncontested cases. For the cases that motivate much of the science (unresponsive patients, infants, animals, machines) the characterisation does not fix a target, and the practice of carrying it to those cases imports a theory that is stated in a few recent programmes and left implicit in most practice. Four senses of "definition" are distinguished here, and "settled" is defined as shared and decisive for the cases a science investigates, which is what a programme of tests for consciousness needs if its tests are to be validated beyond the consensus cases (cf. Bayne et al., 2024). In that sense the definition of consciousness is not settled. Each of the twelve formulations examined rests on terms it does not define, and none can be applied to a new case without first-person report, inference from reporting cases, or a prior theory. The one systematic survey of the field concludes that the definition of what is to be explained is not shared among its respondents. The conditions set out below apply to a formulation used to extend the term to systems that do not report; a phenomenal realist may decline that role for the phenomenal concept. They express the obligation the survey's authors already recommend: to state the theory that does the extending. Applied to the perturbational complexity index, the conditions show which steps of an existing extension are stated and which are inherited from report.

**Keywords:** consciousness; definition; reference-fixing; explication; phenomenal consciousness; introspective report; contested cases

## 1. Introduction

Crick and Koch (1990) wrote that "everyone has a rough idea of what is meant by consciousness" and that "it is better to avoid a precise definition of consciousness because of the dangers of premature definition" (p. 264). Searle (1998) offered a common-sense characterisation of consciousness, "whose job is to identify the target of scientific investigation and not to provide an analysis", and judged that it "is adequate and indeed is exactly what we need to begin our study" (p. 381). Tononi (2004) opened the main text of a founding paper of integrated information theory with "Consciousness is everything we experience. Think of it as what abandons us every night when we fall into dreamless sleep and returns the next morning when we wake up." On this view the explanandum is fixed by phenomenology. There is something it is like to be in certain states, those who have such states know which ones they are, and theories are judged by how well they explain them. The structural programme in mathematical consciousness science takes conscious experience as the given explanandum in this way and formalises its structure (Kleiner, 2020, 2024; Kleiner & Ludwig, 2024).[^1]

The same field records that its target is not shared. Francken et al. (2022), after surveying 166 consciousness researchers, conclude that "the definition of 'what' needs to be explained by consciousness science is not shared between all respondents" and recommend that researchers state their target "in the form of a conceptual or computational definition".[^2]

Both positions are correct, for different cases. The phenomenological characterisation fixes reference for the cases in which subjects report and for systems that closely resemble reporting subjects. For the cases on which the most consequential questions turn (an unresponsive patient, a newborn, an octopus, a language model) it does not fix a target. Whoever carries it to those cases supplies the missing decision from a theory: a theory of which features of reporting humans matter, and why. The thesis is that such a theory is always present when the characterisation is extended to these cases. It is stated in a few recent programmes (Casali et al., 2013; Butlin et al., 2023; Bayne et al., 2024) and left implicit in most clinical, developmental and comparative practice, and the field's obligation is to state it. "The definition is not settled" abbreviates that thesis (Section 2.1).

## 2. Reference-fixing and definition

### 2.1 Four senses of "definition", and what "settled" means

"Definition" is used in at least four senses in this literature. (i) A *lexical* definition records what competent speakers mean by a word. (ii) A *reference-fixing description* picks out a phenomenon by features that speakers can use to find instances. (iii) A *theoretical identification* states what the phenomenon essentially is; in the natural-kind cases it does so in terms independent of the description that picked it out ("water is H₂O"). (iv) An *operational definition* gives a procedure for deciding whether a given case falls under the term.

A definition is *settled*, as the term is used here, when researchers share it and it decides membership for the cases the science investigates. On this usage, sense (ii) is shared for the uncontested cases, and that agreement is real. No formulation in sense (iii) or (iv) is shared. The question is whether sense (ii) decides the contested cases. It does not, and the practice of applying it to them borrows the decision from an unstated theory.

Kripke (1980) separated senses (ii) and (iii): a description can fix the reference of a natural-kind term without stating the essential properties of what it picks out. Putnam (1975) made the point for *water*: the term refers to whatever stuff stands in the right relation to the samples we call water, and that this stuff is H₂O was a discovery. The two jobs have different success conditions. A reference-fixer succeeds if users converge on the same cases. It may be vague at the edges and may use words that presuppose the phenomenon, because its task is to direct attention. A theoretical identification succeeds if it states conditions that are independent of the term, that apply to cases the reference-fixer leaves open, and that explain why the paradigm cases are paradigm cases. It is tested against the reference-fixer: had H₂O failed to pick out most of the paradigm samples, the identification would have been rejected.

Searle draws this distinction for consciousness and uses the water example himself. He contrasts the analytic definition "water is H₂O" with the common-sense definition of water as a clear, colourless liquid found in lakes and rivers, and adds that "analytic definitions typically come at the end, not at the beginning of a scientific investigation" (Searle, 1998, p. 381). On his account the field has a reference-fixer and is still looking for the definition in sense (iii). Searle holds that this is no obstacle to beginning the science, and he is right about beginning it. He also holds that conscious states are "ontologically subjective": they exist only as experienced by a subject. He denies that this rules out a science of them, since it does not prevent "an epistemically objective science of a domain that is ontologically subjective" (p. 382), and his position is compatible with condition C3 below. The first consequence he draws from his definition supports the thesis: "until we get an adequate scientific account of consciousness it is not useful to worry about whether, e.g. snails are conscious" (p. 381). On his account too, the contested case is decided by the theory, and the common-sense definition leaves it open. The disagreement with Searle concerns only the cases he treats as uncontested: consciousness "typically present in humans and the higher mammals" (p. 381). The question is whether his definition itself reaches the higher mammals, or whether what reaches them is an analogy from humans that already carries a theory.

Kripke himself denied that the water model carries over to sensations. Heat is picked out by a contingent property, the sensation it produces in us. Pain, he wrote, "is not picked out by one of its accidental properties; rather it is picked out by the property of being pain itself, by its immediate phenomenological quality" (Kripke, 1980, p. 152). If that is right, the phenomenal characterisation states an essential property. The phenomenal-concept strategy accepts that phenomenal concepts present their referents directly while holding that the property presented may be physical (Loar, 1990; cf. Papineau, 2002). Neither position can simply be assumed in order to declare the definition settled, and the thesis holds whichever is right. If Kripke's disanalogy fails, the phenomenal characterisation is a reference-fixer in sense (ii), and the theoretical identification is still open. If it holds, the characterisation already states the essence, and no further identification of kind (iii) is to be had. But then the characterisation can be applied directly only by a subject to its own states. Applying it to any other system is an inference, and Section 6 examines its premises.

What Section 7 proposes is an *explication* in Carnap's (1950, ch. 1) sense: the replacement of an inexact concept in use (the explicandum) by an exact one (the explicatum), judged by similarity to the explicandum, exactness, fruitfulness and simplicity. An explication does not claim to report what speakers already meant. It is a proposal, assessed by what it lets a science do.

### 2.2 Conditions on a formulation that decides contested cases

The conditions below are not conditions on a concept as such. A reference-fixer need meet none of them, and no fault follows. They apply to a formulation that is meant to settle disputed cases or to extend the term to new ones: a formulation that decides, for systems that do not report, whether they fall under the term.

**C1. Non-circularity (diagnostic).** The definiens does not contain the definiendum or a term whose application presupposes it. Failing C1 does not disqualify a formulation. It marks the point at which the formulation will need report or a further theory in order to be applied.

**C2. Grounding.** The terms in the definiens either are defined elsewhere without circularity or have application conditions that do not depend on the concept being defined.

**C3. Explicit operationalisation.** The definition, together with a *stated* theory, yields a decision procedure for whether a given system at a given time falls under it, and the procedure does not rely solely on the system's own first-person report. A definition that can be applied only by the subject to itself fails C3 whatever theory is added.

**C4. Substrate neutrality.** The definition can be applied to organisms and machines without building in the answer, in either direction.

**C5. Discrimination of contested cases.** The definition sorts the cases on which researchers disagree (dreamless sleep, anaesthesia, disorders of consciousness, infants, non-human animals, artificial systems), or states what further fact would sort them and how that fact could be established for a system that does not report.

C3 allows a theory to be used. "H₂O" meets C3 because the atomic theory it relies on is stated and shared.

A phenomenal realist may reply that consciousness is a primitive and that C1 is misplaced, as it would be for "red". The reply is correct for "red": it is a primitive, but any normal perceiver can check its application to an object from outside. On the realist's own account, the phenomenal primitive can be applied directly only by the subject to its own case, and it has no such outside check. C1 to C3 therefore do not demand that every concept be reducible. A realist may decline, for the phenomenal concept, the role of deciding contested cases. If so, the contested cases are decided by analogy from reporting subjects, on premises that are not stated. The thesis concerns exactly those premises.

Nine rows (Nagel, Block's P-consciousness, Searle, Chalmers, IIT's axioms, Schwitzgebel, the structural programme, the clinical awareness axis and minimal phenomenal experience) are characterisations of consciousness or of its phenomenal sense. Each either contains a term whose application presupposes consciousness or depends on report for its application beyond the first-person case. Three rows (Block's A-consciousness, illusionism and the attention schema) give conditions that a stated theory could apply from outside, though the C1 entries for A-consciousness and the attention schema are not clean either. Phenomenal realists reject these three as accounts of something else: of availability, of the belief that one has experience, or of the report. Illusionists reply that whatever deserves the name is functional (quasi-phenomenal), and that realists are wrong that anything further exists to be defined.

None of the nine, as stated, decides a contested case without an inference from reporting subjects. None of the three is accepted by the other side as deciding it. A formulation that the whole field would accept as deciding the contested cases would have to do both things at once: characterise the phenomenal sense, as the nine do, and apply from outside, as the three do. No current formulation does both.

### 3.1 Nagel

Nagel's formula is the one most often cited. Its first undefined part is "like". Nagel excluded the resemblance reading in a footnote: the phrase concerns how it is for the subject, not what the state resembles in our experience (Nagel, 1974, fn. 6, p. 440). Stoljar (2016) gives a semantics for the construction on which "what it's like" sentences express an affective relation between an event and an individual, and argues that on this analysis Nagel's definition is neither empty nor committed to the view that a conscious subject must be aware of being in the state. Hacker (2002) argues that the phrase is malformed as a characterisation of consciousness. The second undefined part is "for the organism", which introduces a point of view. Having a subjective point of view is close to what the formula was meant to pick out, so here the definiens comes close to containing the definiendum. Nagel extended the formula to the bat by inference from its similarity to us, which is the route examined in Section 6.

### 3.2 Block

Block's distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness is the field's most widely used conceptual tool, and its halves are of different kinds. A-consciousness is characterised functionally. P-consciousness is introduced by synonyms ("experience", Nagel's phrase) and then by examples (Block, 1995). Block does not regard the absence of a noncircular definition as a defect, since "the history of reductive definitions in philosophy should lead one not to expect a reductive definition of anything" (p. 230). He argues that the two concepts can come apart, using hypothetical cases such as superblindsight, which he notes may not exist. The distinction thus pairs a functional characterisation with a pointer: a formulation that directs attention to the phenomenon through synonyms and examples, without stating conditions for membership. Whether the two pick out different sets of states is one of the field's open questions.

### 3.3 Searle

In Searle's characterisation the antonym of the definiendum appears in the definiens, which is harmless for a reference-fixer. Sleep onset and offset can be measured, but what they measure is arousal, which clinical neurology treats as a dimension separate from awareness (Laureys, 2005). That dreamless sleep is empty of experience is disputed: Windt, Nielsen and Thompson (2016) argue that, unless dreamless sleep is defined as unconscious from the outset, there is reason to think experience can occur in all stages of sleep, and Tononi and Koch (2015) allow that integrated information need not vanish entirely in dreamless sleep. Searle, Tononi (2004) and the clinical tradition anchor the boundary at dreamless sleep. The anchor case is itself contested.

### 3.4 Chalmers

Chalmers (1995, 1996) adopts Nagel's criterion for the phenomenal sense and reserves *awareness* for functional access. He holds that the phenomenal concept cannot be analysed in functional terms and that this does not rule out a (nonreductive) science of consciousness. If he is right, the concept is primitive and needs no further analysis. It is not thereby settled in the sense of Section 2.1: an experimenter facing a contested case then needs the psychophysical laws that connect physical states to experience (Chalmers, 1996, p. 213). Those laws decide the case, and they are a theory.

### 3.5 Integrated information theory

IIT starts from axioms described as essential properties of experience and derives postulates about physical substrates from them (Tononi & Koch, 2015; Tononi et al., 2016). In its current form the zeroth axiom states that experience exists, and the five further axioms state that every experience is intrinsic ("for the experiencer"), specific, unitary, definite and structured (Albantakis et al., 2023). The theory does not offer the axioms as a definition of consciousness. It proposes an explanatory identity: an experience is identical to the cause–effect structure unfolded from a maximal substrate (Albantakis et al., 2023). The axioms fail C1, as they are meant to, since they characterise the explanandum from the inside. The identity is a candidate theoretical identification, connected to the axioms by an argument its critics dispute.

### 3.6 Schwitzgebel

Schwitzgebel (2016) gives the most careful formulation of the phenomenal group. He rejects definition by analysis, by function and by synonymy, and defines phenomenal consciousness by examples. The positive examples include sensory experiences, imagery experiences, vivid emotions and dreams. The negative examples include growth hormone release, dispositional knowledge, standing intentions and sensory reactivity to masked visual displays; he adds that we normally think dreamless sleep involves a complete absence of phenomenal consciousness. Phenomenal consciousness is the most folk-psychologically obvious feature the positive examples share and the negative ones lack. The examples are chosen by the theorist, and Schwitzgebel explicitly declines to identify the target feature with reportability or with access. He restricts the lists to uncontested cases so as to leave the contested ones open (consciousness without attention, consciousness in simpler animals). He presents that openness as what lets the concept meet his "wonderfulness condition" and keeps it innocent of theoretical commitment. His definition fixes the target for cases that subjects can classify for themselves and leaves every other case to further inquiry. The list of negatives also shows where the extension comes from: masked displays count as negative examples because subjects deny having seen them.

### 3.7 The structural programme

Kleiner and Ludwig (2024) give a precise account of what a mathematical structure of conscious experience is. Their definition applies "according to one's chosen concept of conscious experience" (fn. 3), provided that on the chosen concept every experience exhibits a set of aspects. The relativisation keeps the framework neutral across theories and concepts of experience. It does not concede that the explanandum is undefined; it treats the choice of concept as an input to the formalism. The unity of experience enters as a "phenomenal unity aspect" following Bayne and Chalmers's (2003) subsumptive unity thesis: the experience of what it is like to experience the aspects together. That is a structural description built on the phenomenal concept, not a definition of it. The wider programme (Kleiner, 2020, 2024; Fink, Kob & Lyre, 2021) is rigorous about structure and takes the phenomenal concept as given, as it is entitled to do. Tsuchiya and Saigo (2021) are the exception: they aim to define consciousness relationally, via the Yoneda lemma. What the programme inherits is the extension of that concept, the set of states that fall under it. For the contested cases that extension is fixed by inference from reporting subjects (Section 6).

### 3.8 Clinical neurology

The clinical tradition distinguishes wakefulness and awareness (Laureys, 2005). The wakefulness axis is operational. The awareness axis is assessed by behavioural evidence of voluntary, purposeful interaction, including command-following (Giacino et al., 2004), whose relation to awareness is what is disputed in disorders of consciousness. The clinical vocabulary cuts across the philosophical one: in Chalmers's usage *awareness* names access and *consciousness* names experience, while in clinical usage *consciousness* covers both axes and *awareness* names the content dimension. Clinical awareness, as measured by command-following, is close to what Chalmers calls awareness, namely functional access.

### 3.9 Minimal phenomenal experience

Metzinger (2020) proposes that the simplest form of conscious experience may lack a self. He constrains the concept by six semantic constraints drawn from the literature and by phenomenological case studies, with reports of "pure awareness" from contemplative practitioners among the sources, and proposes that its content may be a Bayesian representation of tonic alertness. It retains *phenomenal* as a primitive, and its extension is constrained by the reports of a narrow, trained population.

### 3.10 Functional and deflationary formulations

Frankish's (2016) illusionism and Webb and Graziano's (2015) attention schema theory characterise something precise: introspective representations that misrepresent, and a simplified self-model of attention that leads a system to conclude that it has experience. Phenomenal realists regard both as accounts of why we say we are conscious. Frankish presents illusionism as a theory of consciousness that replaces the hard problem with the illusion problem (Frankish, 2016, §4). The realist holds that something further exists and that it is what the pointers point at. The dispute bears directly on the contested cases, since the two sides extend the term to non-reporting systems by different criteria.

## 4. What the authors say

Block (1995, pp. 229–230) writes: "Let me acknowledge at the outset that I cannot define P-consciousness in any remotely noncircular way. I don't consider this an embarrassment." He adds that "really all one can do is point to the phenomenon" and that "it is important to point properly" (p. 230), and he calls consciousness a "mongrel concept" (p. 227).

Searle (1998) describes his formulation as a common-sense definition whose job is to identify the target, and places analytic definition at the end of the investigation.

Schwitzgebel (2016) offers a definition "as innocently as I can manage" and states the conditions under which a definition by example would fail: the concept must not be "empty, or broken, or a hodgepodge".

Chalmers (1996, pp. 3–4) quotes Sutherland's (1989) dictionary entry with sympathy, holds that conscious experience cannot be defined in more primitive terms, and offers characterisations instead.

Block, Searle, Schwitzgebel and Chalmers thus say in print that their formulation is not a theoretical definition, or that the concept resists one. Nagel does not say whether his biconditional is meant as a definition, a conceptual analysis, or a mark of the phenomenon. Block, Searle and Chalmers also hold that this is no defect, and for the uncontested cases they are right. Their formulations direct the reader to the same paradigm cases (waking perception, pain, imagery, dreams) and to the same contrast (dreamless sleep, general anaesthesia). The disagreement with these authors concerns only whether this shared pointer, without a stated theory, settles the cases that the science is now asked to decide.

## 5. Evidence on convergence

### 5.1 Survey evidence

Francken et al. (2022) put 22 questions to 166 researchers at the 2018 and 2019 meetings of the Association for the Scientific Study of Consciousness. They treat four questions as probing respondents' definition of consciousness; three bear on it directly. Asked whether there is phenomenal content beyond what is cognitively accessed, 60.6% answered yes. That question concerns the empirical thesis of overflow, and a respondent can accept Block's distinction and deny overflow. What the number shows is narrower. The field's most-used pointer does not by itself fix whether the states it points at extend beyond the states that can be accessed. That boundary is one a theoretical definition would have to draw. The other two questions, on whether consciousness requires higher-order states (34.5% yes) and whether the transition from unconscious to conscious processing is sharp (33.5%; senior and junior researchers differed significantly), record disagreement between theories. The authors conclude that "the definition of 'what' needs to be explained by consciousness science is not shared between all respondents". That is their interpretation of these results, and it is the only systematic survey evidence on the question.

The designers chose not to ask respondents to state a definition, because "we expected that many respondents would have difficulty formulating their definition". That expectation is not itself evidence; it is the judgement of seven researchers close to the field. They also note that operationalisation is theory-laden, so that researchers "may not be investigating the same phenomenon even though the same overarching label is used" (see also Irvine, 2013), and they recommend that each study state its target as a conceptual or computational definition.

### 5.2 Inventories and maps

Vimal (2009) collected about forty meanings attributed to *consciousness*, some of them mutually exclusive. Van Gulick (2014/2026) lists the senses of "conscious" in use for creatures and for states. Kuhn (2024) mapped some two hundred theories into a taxonomy of explanations. Bayne, Hohwy and Owen (2016) and Birch, Schnell and Clayton (2020) map dimensions along which global states and species differ, and so replace a single scale of consciousness with several. No published map of definientia and their dependencies is known to the author. A map of theories cannot be kept apart from a map of definitions: theories that differ in their definiendum cannot be compared on their explanations until it is known whether they are about the same thing.[^3]

### 5.3 The anchor case is contested

The status of dreamless sleep is open (Windt et al., 2016), and so are anaesthesia, the minimally conscious state, early infancy, non-human animals and artificial systems. For each, the phenomenal characterisation states which further fact would decide the case: whether there is something it is like to be in that state. What it lacks is a route to that fact from outside the system. "H₂O" alone gives no procedure for detecting water on Europa either; chemistry supplies it. The difference is that chemistry is stated and shared, and the corresponding theory for consciousness is neither. The demand that the deciding fact be accessible from outside, at least in principle, is C3. Searle accepts that an ontologically subjective domain can have an epistemically objective science, and Chalmers proposes candidate psychophysical principles for the purpose. A realist who declines C3 leaves the contested cases to the analogy from reporting subjects examined in Section 6.

### 5.4 Two responses to non-convergence

One response is eliminativist. Irvine (2013) argues that *consciousness* is not a viable scientific concept and should give way to the more specific notions that experimental work measures.

The other response holds that definition is not required for progress, and it is the response of Crick and Koch (1990) and Searle (1998).[^4] It holds for research questions whose cases are uncontested. A sleep researcher who studies dream recall after REM awakenings does not need a theoretical definition of dreaming to make progress on recall. The most consequential questions in consciousness science concern the cases where the pointer is silent: whether a patient in an unresponsive state is conscious, whether an animal or a machine is. Bayne et al. (2024) argue that tests for consciousness are lacking for many of the populations where they are most needed, that their validation faces fundamental challenges, and that a promising strategy is to begin from non-trivial human cases (infants, fetuses, disorders of consciousness) before moving to animals and artificial systems.[^5] An indicator validated on reporting humans inherits the pointer's limits. Carrying it to other populations requires a stated reason why the indicator tracks the target.

## 6. Report and inference from reporters

The phenomenal formulations are presented as independent of any method of observation. In practice, how far each extends beyond the first-person case is fixed by inference from report and from similarity to reporters. The report is produced by the system whose experience is in question.

Report here is evidence; it does not fix reference. On the realist account, reference is fixed by acquaintance with one's own case and extended to others by inference, as Nagel extends it to the bat. For humans, the inference runs through testimony. Searle's boundaries are known to the subject retrospectively, as gaps in autobiographical memory. The clinical awareness axis is assessed by command-following, which is report by motor response. Metzinger's minimal experience is constrained by contemplative reports, and the variations of experience that the structural programme formalises reach beyond the first person only through report. For animals, the inference runs through behavioural and physiological analogy to reporting humans (Birch et al., 2020), which carries the same dependence one step further. The IIT existence axiom is a first-person assertion; whether it is an act of self-attribution in the sense of attention schema theory is itself a theoretical claim.

Reports are limited as evidence. Nisbett and Wilson (1977) showed that reports about the causes of one's judgements and choices rest on implicit causal theories and are often wrong; their findings concern causes, not the occurrence of experience. They matter here because they show the reporting system bringing its own theory into the report. Schwitzgebel (2008) argues that people are unreliable about the character of their current experience in domains such as emotion, peripheral vision and the phenomenology of thought. His 2008 argument concerns the character of experience; in 2016 he treats its presence as folk-psychologically obvious. The point here concerns neither: the boundary of the extension is drawn by the same reports. Dennett's (2003) heterophenomenology treats reports as data about what subjects believe about their experience. Chalmers (2018) separates the meta-problem, why we say and believe that there is a hard problem, from the hard problem itself, and classes the meta-problem as an easy problem in principle while denying that its solution would by itself solve the hard problem.

None of this shows that experience is report or that the pointers fail to pick out anything. It shows that the extension of the pointer beyond the first person is drawn by reporting systems and by systems judged similar to them, and that a theory tested against that extension inherits whatever the reporting adds or omits.

A theoretical definition does not escape this evidential base. Conditions C7 and C8 below (agreement with the paradigm cases, and an explanation of the reports) test it against the same cases, which report fixes. What it adds is a stated condition that can be applied to systems that do not report. The extrapolation then becomes a hypothesis open to criticism. Without that condition it is an analogy whose premises are not given. A definition, together with the premise that links it to what the pointer picks out, decides contested cases by the theory it accepts. So neither the pointer nor a theoretical definition decides them neutrally. What is gained is explicitness.

## 7. Explication, and a map of terms

### 7.1 Conditions on a candidate explication

The reference-fixing formulations remain useful. What the contested cases require is an explicatum in Carnap's sense (Section 2.1): an exact concept that replaces the inexact one in use, assessed by similarity to it and by fruitfulness. A candidate explicatum is subject to C1 to C5 (Section 2.2) and to four further conditions.

**C6. A stated bridge.** The explicatum is connected to the pointer's referent by a premise, either an identity (as "water is H₂O" or "heat is mean molecular kinetic energy") or a nomological bridge law (as in the psychophysical laws Chalmers calls for). The premise is stated, together with what would count against it. Every theory needs such a premise, including those that begin from phenomenology. The structural programme leaves the concept of experience to be supplied by the researcher. IIT moves from its axioms to its postulates by an argument its critics dispute, and states its identity openly.

**C7. Agreement on the uncontested cases.** The explicatum classifies the paradigm cases the way the pointer does: waking perception and pain fall inside; hormone release, dispositional knowledge and the regulation of blood pressure fall outside. An explicatum that fails here has changed the subject. This is the test that H₂O passed against the river samples.

**C8. An explanation of the pointer.** The explicatum explains why systems that satisfy it produce the reports and beliefs on which the pointer rests: why they say there is something it is like, why they find the explanatory gap compelling, why introspection describes experience as it does. This is the meta-problem (Chalmers, 2018) used as a test of adequacy; Chalmers already presents the meta-problem as a challenge to theories of consciousness.

**C9. Creature and state distinguished.** *Conscious* is said both of creatures and of states (Rosenthal, 1993, 2005; Bayne, 2007), and of capacities and of occurrences. A sleeping person retains the capacity and lacks, or has a reduced form of, the occurrent state; a person under deep anaesthesia may lack both for a time; a system that never had the relevant organisation lacks the capacity. Ordinary usage already marks these distinctions, and the phenomenal pointer can mark them with a dispositional operator: a being for which there *can* be something it is like. C9 is a bookkeeping requirement: an explicatum should say which of these it explicates, because the contested cases differ in which of them is in question.[^6]

### 7.2 A worked case: the perturbational complexity index

Casali et al. (2013) state the theory that carries their index to unresponsive patients, so conditions C6 to C9 can be applied to it. The perturbational complexity index (PCI) is computed by perturbing the cortex with transcranial magnetic stimulation and compressing the spatiotemporal pattern of the evoked electrocortical response to measure its algorithmic complexity. It was tested on recordings including healthy subjects in wakefulness, dreaming, non-REM sleep and sedation with midazolam, xenon and propofol, and patients who had emerged from coma (vegetative state, minimally conscious state and emergence from it, locked-in syndrome). It "reliably discriminated the level of consciousness in single individuals" across these conditions.

*C6, a stated bridge.* The bridge premise is stated in the abstract, as what "theoretical considerations suggest": "consciousness depends on the brain's ability to support complex activity patterns that are, at once, distributed among interacting cortical areas (integrated) and differentiated in space and time (information-rich)". This is the integration-and-differentiation premise associated with integrated information theory (Tononi, 2004), which Casali et al. cite at this point and which Bayne et al. (2024) identify as the test's inspiration. Stated as a dependence, it is a nomological premise in the sense of C6. What would count against the index follows from the test design: a subject who is conscious by the independent criteria with a low value, or an unconscious subject with a high one. What would count against the premise itself is not stated separately. The two can come apart: a failure of the index could be blamed on the complexity measure or the stimulation site, and the premise would stand.

*C7, agreement on the uncontested cases.* This agreement is secured by calibration. The conditions on which PCI was tested were classified beforehand by behaviour (MOAAS responsiveness; the CRS-R, Giacino et al., 2004), clinical diagnosis and sleep staging. None of these is a report; each was calibrated, historically, on subjects who could report, and that is where the pointer enters. The index is judged by how well it reproduces that classification, and it also inherits it. One example is the placement of non-REM sleep among the low-level conditions; Section 3.3 found that anchor case contested. Within the uncontested cases this is what C7 asks. When PCI is applied to a patient whose behaviour is uninformative, the verdict rests on the bridge premise and on the calibration. The pointer contributes nothing further.

*C8, an explanation of the pointer.* PCI makes no claim to explain why systems with high values say that there is something it is like to be them, and as an index of level it need not. If the premise behind it were offered as an explicatum, it would owe that explanation: why integrated and differentiated activity yields the reports and beliefs on which the pointer rests.

*C9, creature and state distinguished.* PCI measures the level of consciousness of an individual at a time, which is an occurrent, creature-level quantity. It does not separate capacity from occurrence. A low value in non-REM sleep and a low value in a vegetative patient are the same reading, although the sleeper retains the capacity and whether the patient does is the clinical question. It does not address content. These limits fall within its stated scope.

*C4, substrate neutrality, and C5, discrimination of contested cases.* The premise makes no reference to substrate; the procedure does, since it requires a cortex that can be stimulated and recorded. For the contested human cases PCI states what further fact would sort them and how to obtain it, which C5 asks. For animals with different neuroanatomy and for artificial systems, applying the premise would need a new procedure, and the calibration would not transfer.

PCI's extension to unresponsive patients is thus a stated hypothesis that can fail in the ways listed under C6, which is what the obligation stated in Section 1 requires. Butlin et al. (2023) meet the same requirement for artificial systems by other means: they adopt computational functionalism "as a working hypothesis" and derive indicator properties from named theories, so that the premise carrying each indicator to a machine is written down.

### 7.3 A map of definitions and terms

The more immediate need is a map. Francken et al. (2022) recommend that researchers state their definition. Kuhn (2024) has mapped theories, and Bayne et al. (2016) and Birch et al. (2020) have mapped dimensions. What is missing is a map of definientia and of the terms they use, maintained so that a researcher can cite an entry when stating a target, and so that two papers can be checked for whether they address the same thing before their results are compared.

Such a map would need at least the following dimensions, kept separate because current usage runs them together:

- **Arousal:** wakefulness and its physiological markers.
- **Content:** what a state represents.
- **Access:** availability of content for reasoning, report and the control of action.
- **Phenomenal reference:** the Nagel-type pointer, recorded as a pointer, with its paradigm cases.
- **Self-reference:** whether, and how, the system represents itself as the subject of its states.
- **Creature, state and capacity:** whether the term applies to a creature, a state, an ability or an ongoing occurrence.
- **Route of extension:** whether applying the term to a new case requires first-person report, inference from reporters, a behavioural proxy, or a stated theory.

Each entry would record the formulation, its source, its definiens, the undefined terms it rests on, its C1 status, how it is operationalised, which literature uses it, and which other entries use the same word for a different concept. Table 1 is a first slice of such a map. The entries for *awareness* would show the clinical and philosophical usages side by side.
The map takes no side among theories; it requires only that pointers, explicata and bridge premises be recorded as such.

## 8. Conclusion

The phenomenological formulations of consciousness fix reference for subjects who report and for systems that resemble them. Their authors say they are not theoretical definitions, and for the uncontested cases none is needed. The cases on which consciousness science is now asked for answers (unresponsive patients, infants, animals, machines) are decided by a theory of which features of reporting humans matter. That theory is stated in a handful of recent programmes (Casali et al., 2013; Bayne et al., 2024; Butlin et al., 2023) and left implicit elsewhere. Where it is stated, the conditions on an explication (C6 to C9) show which steps are stated and which are inherited from report (Section 7.2).

The definition of consciousness is in this sense not settled. Each claim about a contested case should state the theory that carries the pointer to it, as Francken et al. (2022) recommend; the formulations in use should be recorded with their dependencies; and candidate explications should be assessed against explicit conditions. Searle placed analytic definition at the end of inquiry. For the contested cases the field has not reached that end, and it already draws conclusions about them.
