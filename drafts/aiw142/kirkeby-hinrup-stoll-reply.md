## Commentary draft

**Commentary: Naturalizing perceptual experience: the explanatory power of probabilistic computation and
the holographic solution**

Matthias Gruber
Independent researcher · research@matthiasgruber.com · ORCID 0009-0005-9697-1665

*A commentary on:* Kirkeby-Hinrup, A., and Stoll, E. A. (2026). Naturalizing perceptual experience: the
explanatory power of probabilistic computation and the holographic solution. *Front. Hum. Neurosci.*
20:1816314. doi: 10.3389/fnhum.2026.1816314

**Keywords:** consciousness, naturalization, substrate independence, self-model, quantum decoherence,
holography

### 1 Introduction

Kirkeby-Hinrup and Stoll (2026) propose that perceptual experience is a hologram produced by the
interference of ion wavefunctions at the neuronal membrane: "The neural activity is correlated with
perceptual content, while the hologram is the perceptual content" (Section 5). They close by asking other
theories to say what kind of thing in the world consciousness is. The Four-Model Theory (FMT; Gruber,
2026) gives a computational answer. It agrees with theirs on three points and differs on where the
requirement for experience sits.

### 2 Points of agreement

*Experience is constituted, not correlated.* In FMT, experience is the content of a simulation the brain
runs: two kinds of models, of the world and of the self, each in a stored (implicit) and a running
(explicit) form. Qualia are properties of the running models, and neural firing generates and sustains
them without itself being the experience (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.4). The authors' hologram stands to
neural activity in the same relation.

*Privacy follows from the mechanism.* The authors derive privacy from the fact that no external observer
can recreate the reference wavefunction (Section 5). FMT derives it from self-referential closure: the
self model is part of what the world model models, so an outside description captures the structure of
the self model without the perspective the running model constitutes (Gruber, 2026, Section 3.4.3). In
both accounts, subjectivity is a consequence of how the content is produced.

*The previous state enters the present content.* The authors' reference wave is "the immediately previous
neural network state" (Section 5). In FMT, each frame of the simulation carries the previous frame's
content into the next, and this carrying is what gives experience its temporal flow (Gruber, 2026,
Section 3.4.4).

### 3 Where the requirement sits

The authors state the condition precisely: "if and only if the neural network meets the physiological
criteria to undergo quantum computation … and also meets the anatomical criteria to act as a holographic
recording surface, will the system generate phenomenal content" (Section 5). This is not biological
naturalism, since the recording surface may be "organic or synthetic" and "a system of any size with
these key properties will be conscious" (Table 1). It is a requirement on physics: a classical system
computing the same neuronal outcomes would produce no hologram.

FMT places the requirement in computational organisation. A system that runs both kinds of models under
self-referential closure, recruits open-ended computation for its own modelling, is embodied, and has at
least one pain-pleasure axis is conscious, whatever it is made of (Gruber, 2026, Sections 3.7.3 and 4.4).
On this account the computation is what matters, and any physics that implements it will do.

Two of the authors' own commitments let the accounts be compared.

**The quantum condition.** The authors adopt Tegmark's (2000) criterion that decoherence must be slower
than the relevant dynamics. Tegmark's own estimate for ions in neurons was 10⁻¹³ to 10⁻²⁰ s, against
dynamical time scales of 10⁻³ to 10⁻¹ s, from which he concluded that the cognitively relevant degrees of
freedom are classical. The estimate the authors rely on, 0.4 ms (Stoll, 2022), is longer than Tegmark's
upper bound by roughly nine orders of magnitude, and their footnote 4 notes that sustained coherence
"remains to be evaluated in the laboratory". What in the 2022 calculation accounts for the difference?

**The membrane prediction.** Prediction 5 states that shifting the ratio of cholesterol to
polyunsaturated fatty acids in the membrane should change the richness of perceptual content. FMT
predicts a change as well wherever the manipulation alters ion-channel kinetics, because that alters the
computation. It predicts no change when the computation is unchanged. The accounts therefore separate only
under a matched-computation control: a membrane manipulation for which neuronal input–output behaviour
(firing rates and spike-timing statistics in the affected population) is verified to be unchanged. If
reported richness still changes, the holography solution gains a result FMT cannot accommodate; if it
does not, the membrane effect was mediated by computation. Would the authors accept this control as the
discriminating form of prediction 5?

### 4 Discussion

The authors' account is of perceptual content and is, as they say, neutral on cognitive phenomenology
(Section 8). FMT adds one element that a hologram of the environment does not contain by itself: a model
of the system within the modelled world, which is what makes the content content *for* someone. On the
holography solution, what plays that role?

FMT takes up the challenge the authors issue at the end of their paper. The two answers share a picture
of experience as the content a predictive process generates, and they differ on one variable, whether
that process must be quantum, which the two tests above can decide.
