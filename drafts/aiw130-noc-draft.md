# More Than Computation, Made of Computation: Free Modelling as a Necessary Condition, and a Self-Model That Takes Itself for Its Bearer

---

## Abstract

Is there more to consciousness than computation? This paper answers yes, and locates the extra ingredient in computation itself: a second-order regime in which a system's modelling takes its own modelling as an object, run by a self-model that takes itself for the being it models. The account rests on two claims. The enabling claim is a necessity: consciousness requires free modelling, open-ended computation that the system recruits for its own world- and self-models, such that a feedforward model of matched capacity trained on the same history falls behind it on novel input as compositional depth grows. In cortex, free modelling usually shows as near-critical dynamics, and the state-transition literature on anaesthesia, sleep, seizure and disorders of consciousness records that signature; the relation runs from free modelling to the signature, so a near-critical cortex can be unconscious and a subcritical system conscious, and criticality data corroborate the account without testing it. The constitutive claim says what experience is: information from the system's implicit models is integrated into its explicit models and fed to an explicit self-model that takes the modelled self for the subject. On the computationalism–implementationism axis the theory is computationalist: what makes a system conscious is the form of computation it runs, and the neural signature is a contingent trace of that computation in one substrate. The paper states the falsifier for the necessity claim, a matched-capacity novel-input probe, and shows the account's consistency with integrated world-modelling and recursive self-modelling frameworks.

**Keywords:** consciousness; free modelling; self-model; criticality; computationalism; self-referential closure

---

## 1. Introduction

Is there more to consciousness than computation? The answer defended here is yes, and the "more" is itself computation. What a base account leaves out is a *second-order* regime: computation that operates on computation, a system's model of itself taking its own modelling as an object. Consciousness, on this view, is what a modelling system becomes when it integrates its own implicit models into its explicit ones, feeds the result to a model of itself, and that self-model comes to treat itself as the very being it models.

Two claims carry the argument. The enabling claim is a necessity: second-order modelling requires free modelling, open-ended computation that the system recruits for its own world- and self-models, and a system without it has no consciousness, whatever its dynamics. The constitutive claim says what experience is: the integration of implicit-model content into the explicit models and the self-model's conflation of itself with its bearer.

Free modelling is necessary and not sufficient. In cortex it usually shows as near-critical dynamics, and the relation has a direction: free modelling causes the near-critical signature in brains of a certain kind, under loads of a certain size, so the signature may be absent in other implementations and absent part of the time even in human conscious processing, while a near-critical cortex running heavy unconscious modelling is expected rather than anomalous. Criticality therefore enters the account as the usual cortical trace of the enabling condition, a fact the state-transition literature has recorded for two decades and that the theory explains without resting on it.

The question behind this paper is often posed as computationalism against implementationism: whether consciousness depends on the computations a system performs or on the physical details of how they are implemented. The four-model theory is computationalist. What makes a system conscious is that it runs a self-referential, second-order computation, a regime that is multiply realisable in principle, and the near-critical signature of that computation in spiking cortex is a fact about one substrate. A different substrate realising the same computation on different physics can display none of the avalanche statistics of cortex.

The field this paper enters has no settled paradigm. A recent adversarial collaboration pitted leading frameworks against one another with results that partly matched integrated information theory's prediction of a posterior hot zone — decodable stimulus content was found there — while failing its prediction of sustained posterior synchrony, and showed weaker-than-predicted prefrontal ignition for global neuronal workspace, confirming neither decisively (Cogitate Consortium, 2025). Surveys describe a proliferation of incompatible accounts with no agreed method for adjudicating among them (Seth and Bayne, 2022). The four-model theory offers a reframe of what the target *is* — a computational regime — and, from that reframe, an account of a fact the search for neural correlates has recorded without explaining: why conscious level co-varies with cortical criticality.

The full framework, including its treatment of the hard problem (Chalmers, 1995), the binding problem and altered states, is set out in Gruber (2026a). This paper develops one part of it: that free modelling is a necessary condition for the second-order computation that constitutes experience, why near-criticality is its usual signature in cortex, and which observation would show the necessity false.

## 2. An operational anchor for "consciousness"

The target is the field's standard operational notion of consciousness: phenomenal consciousness in the minimal sense: the presence of subjective experience, there being "something it is like" to be a system in a given state (Nagel, 1974). This is distinguished, as standardly, from access consciousness — the availability of information for report and control — because a system can be experientially present without being able to report (Block, 1995). The field individuates conscious states by converging report-based and no-report markers of presence versus absence. Report and behaviour anchor the easy cases; where they fail, as in sleep, anaesthesia and disorders of consciousness, perturbational complexity separates conscious from unconscious states (Casali et al., 2013; Casarotto et al., 2016). This operational consensus is what the theory takes as given.

The four-model theory adds to this target rather than replacing it. Where the operational programme measures *whether* experience is present, the theory says *what is present when it is*: an ongoing self-model, generated by the system, that models the world, the system within that world, and its own modelling. The architecture is a taxonomy of **two model classes along two dimensions** — scope (world versus self) and mode (implicit versus explicit) — whose crossing yields four model *kinds*: implicit and explicit models of the world, and implicit and explicit models of the self. The implicit kinds are substrate-level, learned, and non-phenomenal; the explicit kinds are generated and transient, and phenomenal where they enter the self-model's closed loop. These are kinds of modelling, not four anatomical modules, and the biological substrate blends them continuously — a reaching movement models world-geometry and self-kinematics at once. The full exposition is in Gruber (2026a). What matters here is one structural fact about the explicit self-model: it takes the system's own modelling as its object, and that self-interaction is the second-order computation the argument turns on.

## 3. Free modelling as a necessary condition, and the integration that constitutes experience

### 3.1 Consciousness requires free modelling

A system that models its own modelling can take any of its own states, its modelling included, as a further object, and the depth of self-reference a situation will demand is not known in advance. A computation of a fixed type settles that depth beforehand. What the system needs instead is open-ended computation, whose reach is limited by the resources available at the time and by no ceiling built in ahead of them. This is weaker than Turing-universality in the strict sense, which no finite system has.

Where can such computation run? Universal computation carries no criticality condition: it is proved for rate networks (Siegelmann & Sontag, 1995) and spiking networks (Maass, 1996) with no condition on the operating point, and in echo-state models memory and computation co-peak on the ordered side of the measured edge of chaos and collapse at it (Gruber, 2026b). What consciousness requires is more specific than the capability. It is free modelling: open-ended computation that the system itself recruits for its own world- and self-models, with no depth of self-reference fixed in advance. The condition has three parts (Gruber, 2026a, Section 3.7.3): the substrate is capable of open-ended computation, it actually performs it, and it does so in the service of its own self-modelling, the recruitment sustained by its own operation whatever first installed it. Stated at the budget every organism has, the requirement reads: a feedforward model of matched capacity, trained on the system's own history, falls behind the system on novel input as compositional depth grows (Gruber, 2026a, Table 1b).

In cortex free modelling usually shows as dynamics held near the edge of chaos, where the storage and the transmission of information are both supported (Langton, 1990; Bertschinger & Natschläger, 2004) and the waking set-point sits slightly below the branching transition (Priesemann et al., 2014) and is homeostatically maintained (Ma et al., 2019). The relation runs from free modelling to that signature. A human brain engaged in conscious cognition recruits far more tissue than the modelling task needs and keeps survival work running alongside, and that recruitment, with the energy management it requires, is a plausible reason the signature becomes measurable at all. A smaller or differently built system meeting the same requirement may leave no such trace, and whether some human conscious processing runs subcritically part of the time is open.

### 3.2 The signature across state transitions

The near-critical signature has been measured across state transitions independently of any theory, and two notions of criticality have to be kept apart. *Avalanche criticality* concerns a branching process: cortical activity propagates in cascades whose sizes follow a power law (Beggs & Plenz, 2003), and a branching ratio separates activity that dies out from activity that runs away. *Edge-of-chaos criticality* concerns where the collective dynamics sit relative to the boundary between ordered and chaotic evolution. In behaving animals the waking avalanche set-point is slightly subcritical (Priesemann et al., 2014) and is homeostatically restored over about two days when perturbed (Ma et al., 2019).

The link to consciousness runs through the edge-of-chaos distance. The distance of slow cortical electrodynamics from the edge tracks conscious level across anaesthesia, seizures, psychedelic states and disorders of consciousness (Toker et al., 2022). Waking sits near the edge on its chaotic side; propofol drives the slow dynamics deeper into the chaotic phase, generalised seizures carry them across the edge into the periodic phase, and LSD brings them closer to the edge while experience persists in altered form and signal diversity rises (Toker et al., 2022; Schartner et al., 2017). Waking cortex is thus slightly subcritical by its branching ratio and slightly chaotic by its slow electrodynamics at once. Recent syntheses treat criticality as a candidate unifying set-point of brain function (Hengen & Shew, 2025) and as a common substrate for several theories of consciousness (Algom & Shriki, 2026).

The branching measure cannot separate conscious from unconscious cortex on its own: deep slow-wave sleep sits if anything closer to the branching critical point than waking (Priesemann et al., 2013), and sustained wakefulness progressively disrupts avalanche signatures that sleep restores (Meisel et al., 2013). On the present account a near-critical cortex in deep sleep runs modelling that never becomes experience. Near-criticality also fails as a sufficient marker outside the brain: a reservoir of randomly coupled units tuned to the edge of chaos is near-critical, and so is a fluid at the onset of turbulence, while neither models a world or itself (Gruber, 2026c).

### 3.3 The integration that constitutes experience

Free modelling says what the second-order computation needs. What that computation *is*, and why running it amounts to experience, is the theory's constitutive claim, and it is separate from the necessity claim.

Base, first-order computation is the modelling of world and self — perceiving, acting, predicting. The implicit models hold learned structure stored in the substrate's connectivity and evaluated by substrate processing, not itself represented; the explicit models generate a transient, structured scene and self. Much of this proceeds unconsciously. Conscious experience arises through a specific further operation on that first-order computation. Information carried implicitly — in the substrate's own learned dynamics — is integrated into the explicit models, and the integrated content is fed to the explicit self-model. The self-model then does something peculiar: it *confuses itself with the being it occupies*. It does not represent itself as a model among the system's models; it takes itself to be the system — the one perceiving, the one acting, the subject the scene is *for*. Metzinger (2003) calls this the transparency of the self-model, and it continues Dennett's (1991) treatment of the self as a construct the system's own processing produces. That conflation, running on the integrated implicit-to-explicit content, is what it is for the system to have an experience.

This is how a cortex can run heavy modelling, near-critical by its avalanche statistics, and be unconscious. If, in some phase of sleep, cortex deploys its explicit models heavily but does not integrate that content into a self-model that conflates itself with its bearer, the constitutive step is missing while free modelling runs. The theory accommodates the case: free modelling and its signature are present, and the self-conflation that makes the modelling an experience is absent.

The same structure separates a self-*model* from mere self-*monitoring*, and shows why an all-purpose digital computer, however powerful, is not conscious on this account. A system that monitors its own states — a supervisor process reading out a first process — is not thereby closed: the monitor stands outside what it monitors, and the whole arrangement can be described from a vantage point where the supervised process is here and the supervisor there. Closure is the case in which no such external vantage remains within the system: the self-model models the world, the system in the world, and its own modelling of both, so the modelling process is among the things it models (Gruber, 2026a). A universal digital computer can compute anything computable, but the objects it models are supplied to it from outside as inputs and goals; its self-modelling, where it has any, is *heteronomous*, and it fails the third part of the condition in Section 3.1. What it runs is first-order computation over externally supplied objects, never a self-directed modelling that closes back on itself and mistakes itself for the system. Autonomy here is a property of *what is computed* — whether the models are endogenously generated and self-directed — and holds whatever the hardware or its clock. This marks two thresholds (Gruber, 2026a, Section 3.7.3). Free modelling is the computational threshold: it enables the second-order computation without constituting it. The architecture — the integration of implicit into explicit models and the self-model's closure and self-conflation — is the threshold that sets whether, and how deeply, experience is present.

### 3.4 Why the signature co-varies with conscious level

Why, then, is cortex near-critical, and why does the set-point co-vary with conscious level? Optimisation explains the first: critical dynamics maximise dynamic range (Shew et al., 2009), susceptibility and correlation length (Chialvo, 2010), and homeostasis tunes toward them (Ma et al., 2019; Hengen & Shew, 2025). The four-model theory adds a reason for the second that leaves the optimisation account intact: free modelling, the enabling condition of Section 3.1, recruits in a human brain far more tissue than the modelling task needs and keeps survival work running alongside, and that recruitment is the plausible source of the near-critical dynamics across the cortex. When the explicit models are abolished, as under propofol, the recruitment ends and the slow dynamics leave the edge (Toker et al., 2022); when the models return, the signature returns with them. The correlation the literature has recorded is, on this reading, the trace free modelling leaves in one kind of substrate under loads of a certain size, which is why the theory allows it to fail at both margins: a near-critical cortex running unconscious modelling, and a conscious system small or efficient enough to model freely below the signature's threshold.

## 4. Computationalist, not implementationist

The account just given fixes the theory's position on the computationalism–implementationism axis. Consciousness, here, is a computation of a particular self-referential form together with the integration and self-conflation that make it an experience — a fully physical pattern a system enters and sustains, with no extra ingredient added to the physics: no experiential essence, no privileged material. What carries phenomenal character is the *organisation* of the computation, not the medium (Chalmers, 1996). The same second-order computation, integration, and self-conflation, run on different physics, would be the same consciousness. The theory is therefore computationalist and multiply realisable in principle, not implementationist. Criticality does not compromise this commitment. It enters as a trace the required computation usually leaves in spiking cortex, never as a demand that consciousness be built of any particular stuff. This is the distinction between a computational property and a causal-structure-of-the-substrate property; accounts that locate consciousness in the causal structure of the implementing physics face well-known difficulties precisely because they tie experience to substrate detail (Doerig et al., 2019). The four-model theory ties experience to a computation, and the criticality signature is a trace of that computation in one substrate, which can appear in an unconscious cortex and be missing from a conscious system. A different substrate could realise the same computation on different physics and display none of the avalanche statistics of cortex.

The unfolding argument has a version aimed at any account that rests on recurrence. A recurrent network's input-output function can be reproduced by an unfolded feedforward one, so a theory that ties consciousness to recurrence draws a distinction no input-output experiment can test (Doerig et al., 2019). Closure is recurrent, and the reply is the one built into the falsifier of Section 5: the two architectures agree only when resources are unbounded. Unfolding replaces a reused loop with replicated stages. On a model connectome, removing every closed path costs 74.0% of the synapses where relocating closure costs 14.0% (Gruber, 2026b), and an organism runs under a bound at which the unfolded chain falls behind the closed one on novel input as compositional depth grows. The claim concerns efficiency under a resource bound; on an unbounded substrate the unfolded chain reproduces the behaviour. Basri, BarAvi and Hemmo (2026) reject the argument's second premise on separate grounds: feedforward approximation is guaranteed only on compact domains, which leaves open whether it holds for outputs that depend on an open-ended input history.

The 2×2 of model kinds belongs here as the taxonomy of *what closes the loop*, not as a parts list. The four kinds are the extremal corners of a continuous scope-by-mode space — world versus self, implicit versus explicit; consciousness requires that a system model both scopes in both modes and close its explicit self-model back onto the whole. Naming "four" names the corners of the space, not four boxes in the brain, and the biological substrate maintains innumerable overlapping models that blend across the world/self boundary. The architectural minimum is closure over the full space, not a count of modules.

## 5. What would disconfirm the necessity claim

The necessity claim is that consciousness requires free modelling, and its falsifier targets that capability: robust consciousness in a system whose behaviour on novel input is matched, as compositional depth grows, by an unrolled feedforward model of equal capacity trained on the same history (Gruber, 2026a, Table 1b). The probe compares the system with a model matched on parameters, exposure and memory, so it applies now to artificial systems whose weights and dynamics are inspectable and to nervous systems as they become so; a human version needs a comparator matched on exposure as well as capacity, which no behavioural record supplies. Criticality statistics are recorded alongside the probe as the signature's own validation, with the convergence requirements the criticality literature has developed: avalanche exponents near −3/2 (Beggs & Plenz, 2003) estimated with subsampling-invariant methods (Wilting & Priesemann, 2018), detrended-fluctuation exponents of 0.6 to 0.9 (Hardstone et al., 2012), the directional edge-of-chaos margin (Toker et al., 2022), and the power-law caveats of Touboul and Destexhe (2017) and Clauset, Shalizi, and Newman (2009). Where free modelling is abolished, cortex is expected to leave the near-critical band, on the avalanche measures toward subcriticality in most cases. A conscious state in which the explicit models are demonstrably running while the cortex sits far from criticality would show that the signature is more conditional than the human data so far suggest, and would leave the necessity claim standing.

The constitutive claim has a separate test. If the organisation of the explicit models sets the level of consciousness, a perturbation that degrades the explicit self-model while sparing the substrate should move the fidelity of self-modelling and of world-modelling independently, and a scalar account predicts that they covary; in signal-detection terms, metacognitive sensitivity (meta-d′) should be movable while first-order sensitivity (d′) is spared (Maniscalco & Lau, 2012; Fleming & Lau, 2014). Anterior prefrontal lesions, prefrontal theta-burst stimulation and sub-anaesthetic ketamine have each lowered metacognitive sensitivity or efficiency without a detectable first-order change (Fleming et al., 2014; Rounis et al., 2010; Lehmann et al., 2022), though the stimulation result failed a direct replication (Bor et al., 2017) and none of these designs let d′ vary freely, so the orthogonality itself is untested; the decisive design lets d′ vary within subjects (Gruber, 2026c). The state-transition data of Section 3.2 remain post-hoc corroborations shared with the whole criticality literature; the theory's addition is the direction of the relation and the probe that puts the requirement itself at risk.

## 6. A further consequence: two dials and subjective time

Two dimensions describe how much Class 4 dynamics a substrate is doing, and they can vary independently (Gruber, 2026a, Section 3.7). One is *extent* — how much of the cortex is recruited into near-critical dynamics. The other is *complexity* — how rich the computation running on the recruited tissue is, the differentiation of its activity. Extent is roughly an integration axis, complexity roughly a differentiation axis, and a state of the substrate is located by both, never by a single scalar of arousal. Normal waking rides a frontier balancing the two. The dials describe the substrate's state; the level of consciousness is set by the architecture of Section 3.3.

The conscious "now" is, on this account, a constructed frame, since perception is sampled and assembled rather than streamed (VanRullen & Koch, 2003), and its effective clock speed can vary. Driving the complexity dial to an extreme while the frame's construction is altered predicts subjective time-dilation: much structured content is assembled within a brief external interval, which is then lived as far longer. The near-death "life review", a panoramic re-experiencing reported as compressed into seconds (Noyes & Kletti, 1977), has this profile; such reports are phenomenological, and no validated instrument yet measures the time distortion itself.

The two-dial picture supplies a negative control: the generalised tonic-clonic seizure. A generalised seizure produces near-maximal co-activation, yet its dynamics are pathologically organised and low in differentiation, a failure of the self-organised criticality of the waking state. Direct human evidence for that departure exists for both seizure types: focal ictal recordings lose the power-law statistics of the critical regime (Meisel et al., 2012), and in the three generalised seizures so far measured the slow electrodynamics crossed the edge into the periodic phase (Toker et al., 2022), the hypersynchrony route, though extent collapses on either route. Extent, measured as Class 4 involvement, is low during the seizure though co-activation is high, such dynamics cannot host free modelling, and consciousness is lost. A co-activation account expects consciousness where the extent measure expects its loss, and the clinical record follows the loss.

## 7. Convergence with existing frameworks

The field is converging on three ingredients: criticality, integration-and-differentiation, and recursive self-modelling. The four-model theory places them in one account: recursive self-modelling is the second-order computation, integration and differentiation describe the substrate it runs on, and criticality is the trace free modelling usually leaves in cortex. Convergence is not confirmation, and none of the frameworks below tests the theory.

**Integrated World Modeling Theory.** Safron's account unifies predictive-processing, global-workspace, and integrated-information elements under a system that jointly models world and self through active inference, and that integration is its real strength (Safron, 2020). The four-model theory shares the constitutive commitment to joint world-*and*-self modelling and is consistent with the active-inference framing of the modelling process. What it adds is the real/virtual level split — the distinction between the non-phenomenal implicit substrate and the explicit simulation, phenomenal in the self-model's closed loop — and free modelling as the condition under which the closure that binds world- and self-models together can run.

**Recursive and fractal self-modelling.** Bieberich's recurrent fractal neural network frames cortical self-reference as self-similar organisation across scales, formalising the intuition that self-modelling is recursive and nested (Bieberich, 2002). The four-model theory shares the emphasis on recursion depth and self-referential organisation; its graduated ladder of self-modelling — first-, second-, and third-order self-reference — is a specific reading of the recursive-self-modelling intuition the fractal approach expresses in the language of self-similarity. What it adds is the computational condition: the recursion needs free modelling, open-ended computation with no depth of self-reference fixed in advance.

**The Beautiful Loop.** Laukkonen, Friston, and Chandaria give an active-inference account in which consciousness is a closed self-modelling loop with "epistemic depth" — a system inferring its own inferential states (Laukkonen, Friston, and Chandaria, 2025). This is the same structural insight as the four-model theory's second-order "computation upon computation," reached from an active-inference rather than a dynamical-systems starting point. The two are complementary descriptions of closure: one specifies the inferential architecture of the loop, the other the computational condition the loop needs. That two independent formalisms converge on self-referential closure is mutual corroboration of the target, not of either theory's specifics.

**Higher-order theories.** On higher-order theories a state is conscious when it is the object of a suitable higher-order representation (Rosenthal, 2005; Lau & Rosenthal, 2011; Brown et al., 2019). They are the four-model theory's nearest neighbour: both locate consciousness in a system representing its own states, and on the signal-detection test of Section 5 a higher-order theory predicts the same metacognition-specific effect. The four-model theory embeds the second-order representation in the two-by-two of model kinds, makes implicit self-modelling part of the architecture, and takes phenomenal character from the self-model's taking itself for its bearer; separating the two accounts needs experiments on these further commitments.

**Integrated Information Theory and the neural-complexity tradition.** Integrated information theory identifies consciousness with integrated information and develops the proposal with unusual mathematical rigour, formalising both the integration and the differentiation of a system's cause-effect structure (Tononi, 2004; Oizumi, Albantakis, and Tononi, 2014; Albantakis et al., 2023); it stands in a longer tradition of quantifying neural complexity as the balance of integration and differentiation (Tononi, Sporns, and Edelman, 1994). The four-model theory's two dials — extent as an integration axis, complexity as a differentiation axis — sit squarely in this tradition and claim no priority over it. What the theory adds is to treat *both* dials as dimensions of one substrate's Class 4 dynamics and to single out the rare states in which both are unusually high at once (Gruber, 2026a). The theory takes no position in the dispute over integrated information theory's scientific status; it borrows the integration/differentiation structure and locates it dynamically.

**Complexity indices of consciousness.** Perturbational complexity and Lempel-Ziv-based signal-diversity measures operationalise the differentiation dial directly and already discriminate conscious from unconscious states — separating propofol and xenon unresponsiveness from ketamine's preserved but disconnected experience, and tracking the raised diversity of psychedelic states (Casali et al., 2013; Sarasso et al., 2015; Schartner et al., 2017). Their strength is that they are validated and in routine clinical and experimental use. For the four-model theory these indices measure the complexity dial with instruments already in clinical and experimental use; the spatially resolved extent measure has yet to be built.

## 8. Substrate neutrality and limits

The requirement is stated at the level that is substrate-neutral: open-ended computation recruited for autonomous self-modelling. A neural substrate meeting it usually exhibits near-critical dynamics, plausibly because of how much tissue a brain recruits and how it manages the energy of doing so; a digital realisation of the same second-order computation can meet it with none of the avalanche statistics of cortex, because its open-endedness is supplied architecturally; and a small nervous system meeting it may do so below the signature's threshold. A separate treatment develops the substrate-neutral side directly, with mechanism demonstrations of closure at toy scale and the capability-first reading of criticality in model systems, the scaled test of the dependency left open (Gruber, 2026b). The two treatments describe one condition at two grains, and the neural signature is the local, contingent appearance of the condition in one of them.

There is as yet no full mathematical formalisation of the architecture; the free-modelling requirement is operationalised through the novel-input probe of Section 5 but not derived from a closed formal model of the four model kinds, and that derivation is outstanding. A residual bridging commitment remains — the step from the integration and self-conflation to lived phenomenal character — and it is shared by every theory that connects a physical or computational condition to experience; the four-model theory locates the step at the self-model's conflation of itself with its bearer, the condition under which the gap between process and felt state does not arise, and the philosophical burden of that step remains (Chalmers, 1995). A no-go theorem constrains the artificial case. Kleiner and Ludwig (2024) prove that if consciousness is dynamically relevant, so that a theory of consciousness predicts a different time course for a conscious system than the system's own computational description does, then no system on verified hardware is conscious, because verification guarantees that a chip follows its description. The four-model theory rejects the premise. Consciousness here is the self-simulation the system runs; the closed loop is part of the computational description, and the difference it makes shows against other systems at matched resources, never against the system's own reference dynamics. Dynamical relevance is defined relative to a reference theory, and the theorem needs the chip's own verified description as that reference. The four-model theory's test presupposes relevance relative to a different reference, a matched system without the loop: closure is ablated against matched baselines, and consciousness is read from architecture, never as a departure from the system's own dynamics. The other-minds problem bounds any extension to artificial systems: even a system built to the substrate-neutral specification could not be *verified* conscious from outside, a limit shared with every account that permits artificial consciousness (Butlin et al., 2026; Findlay et al., 2024). Biological cognition is realised on strikingly different substrates across taxa — the cortex-free but plainly capable pallium of corvids among them (Güntürkün and Bugnyar, 2016) — so a signature measured in mammalian cortex says little about how a corvid meets the requirement.

## 9. Conclusion

Is there more to consciousness than base computation? Yes, and the more is made of computation: a second-order regime in which computation operates on computation, closed upon a self-model that takes itself for the being it occupies. Free modelling is the enabling condition, and a system without it has no consciousness whatever its dynamics. In cortex free modelling usually shows as near-critical dynamics, so the field's decades-old criticality signature reads as the trace of the enabling condition in one substrate, usually present in human cortex during conscious processing and allowed to be absent in smaller or differently built systems that meet the same requirement. Experience is the integration of implicit into explicit models, fed to a self-model that mistakes itself for its bearer. The optimisation programme explained why criticality is useful; the four-model theory adds what the criticality literature was measuring, and puts the requirement itself at risk: find robust consciousness in a system that a matched feedforward model keeps pace with on novel input of growing compositional depth, and the necessity claim is wrong.

**Figure 1. Free modelling is necessary; near-criticality is its usual cortical signature.** Panel (a): the signature. Brain states are plotted against a horizontal edge-of-chaos axis (periodic/stable ← near-critical → chaotic/unstable) and a vertical axis marking whether the system as a whole is in a conscious state; each state is drawn as a region, since the values vary across individuals and moments. Normal waking sits near the edge, mostly on its chaotic side, with consciousness present; LSD (75 µg) reduces chaoticity and sits closer to the edge, overlapping waking, with consciousness present in altered form; propofol displaces the slow dynamics deeper into the chaotic phase with consciousness absent; the generalised seizure, despite near-maximal raw co-activation, crosses the edge into the periodic phase with consciousness absent (Toker et al., 2022). Toker et al. infer waking's chaotic-side position from the direction of the LSD shift and regard the side as unsettled; on branching-ratio measures awake cortex is slightly subcritical (Wilting & Priesemann, 2018), a different notion of criticality. The relation runs from free modelling to the signature: the band admits unconscious states, and a conscious system outside it stays possible. Within a conscious state much modelling still stays unconscious (panel b). Panel (b): the constitutive step. Where free modelling runs (the enabling condition), information from the implicit models is integrated into the explicit models and fed to the explicit self-model; when that self-model confuses itself with the being it occupies, taking the modelled self to be the subject rather than a model of it, the result is conscious experience. The same free modelling running explicit models whose content does *not* reach a self-conflating self-model (as in some phases of sleep) remains unconscious.

Alt text: A two-panel figure. Panel a plots brain states, each drawn as a region rather than a point, along a horizontal edge-of-chaos axis running from periodic through a near-critical band to chaotic, against a vertical axis marking whether the system as a whole is in a conscious state; waking lies mostly on the chaotic side of the edge and reaches slightly across it, and the LSD state sits closer to the edge and overlaps waking, both with consciousness present; propofol is displaced deeper into the chaotic phase with consciousness absent, and a generalised seizure, despite high co-activation, is placed across the edge in the periodic phase with consciousness absent; an annotation notes that free modelling produces the signature and that the band admits unconscious states. Panel b is a schematic pipeline under the label free modelling: implicit models feed integrated information into explicit models, which feed an explicit self-model; when the self-model conflates itself with the being it occupies, the output is labelled conscious experience, and a parallel branch in which the content does not reach a self-conflating self-model is labelled unconscious modelling.

**Figure 2. Computation upon computation: self-referential closure.** A three-stage schematic distinguishing closure from mere self-monitoring. Panel (a): a simple simulation models an external world, with an outward-pointing modelling arrow only. Panel (b): a monitored simulation adds a separate monitoring process that observes the system's states, but the monitor stands outside what it monitors and is not itself modelled — self-monitoring without closure. Panel (c): a closed self-model in which the system models the world, itself within that world, and its own modelling of both, so the modelling process is among the things being modelled — self-referential closure, second-order computation operating on first-order computation. Only panel (c) is the regime the theory identifies with consciousness.

Alt text: A three-stage schematic of computational architectures. Stage one shows a system modelling an external world with an arrow pointing outward only. Stage two adds a separate monitoring box that observes the system but is not itself modelled, illustrating self-monitoring without closure. Stage three shows a closed loop in which the system's model represents the world, the system within that world, and the modelling process itself, illustrating self-referential closure as second-order computation operating on first-order computation.

**Figure 3. Two dials on one substrate: extent, complexity, and subjective time (schematic).** A schematic plane with edge-of-chaos spatial extent on the horizontal axis (a theoretical percolation-style order parameter — the fraction of cortex within the near-critical band — not yet a validated measurement) and on-cluster dynamical complexity on the vertical axis (how rich the computation on the recruited tissue is). Normal waking rides a frontier balancing the two. The generalised tonic-clonic seizure sits at low extent and low complexity despite high raw co-activation, a departure from criticality toward the periodic phase in the generalised seizures so far measured (Toker et al., 2022), marked unconscious; it shows why co-activation is not extent. Near-death life-review reports sit toward the high-complexity, altered-clock corner where the constructed "now" dilates and subjective time stretches.
