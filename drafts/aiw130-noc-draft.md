# More Than Computation, Made of Computation: Free Computation as a Necessary Condition, and a Self-Model That Takes Itself for Its Bearer

**Matthias Gruber · Georgia Sousouri** *(provisional co-author — pending consent and contribution)*

*Matthias Gruber — Independent researcher; ORCID 0009-0005-9697-1665; correspondence: matthias@matthiasgruber.com*

*Georgia Sousouri — University Children's Hospital Zürich & ETH Zürich (Reto Huber group); affiliation and ORCID to be confirmed*

---

## Abstract

Is there more to consciousness than computation? This paper answers yes, and argues that the extra ingredient is itself computation: a second-order regime in which computation operates on computation, a self-model that takes its own modelling as its object. The account rests on two claims. The first is a necessity: consciousness requires *free* — open-ended — computation, and in neural tissue that requirement appears as near-criticality, dynamics held close to the edge of chaos. The necessity is one-directional and scoped to biological brains — no criticality, no consciousness — and criticality is enabling rather than constitutive: it is necessary, not sufficient, and a near-critical cortex can run heavy unconscious modelling. The second claim is constitutive: conscious experience arises when information from the system's implicit models is integrated into its explicit models and fed to an explicit self-model that confuses itself with the being it occupies, taking the modelled self to be the subject rather than a model of it. That integration and self-conflation, not the presence of criticality, is what makes experience. On the special issue's axis the theory is computationalist: what makes a system conscious is the form of computation it runs, and criticality is the signature that computation leaves in neural tissue, not a demand on the substrate. Across anaesthetic, sleep, and pathological transitions, experience is abolished when cortical dynamics leave the near-critical band and returns when near-criticality is restored — a correlation recorded for two decades and read as an efficiency property. The four-model theory names the computation criticality enables and that constitutes experience, turning that correlation into the expected, one-directional signature of a necessity condition. The paper operationalises the necessity claim through a quantified distance-to-criticality criterion and an interventional test, and shows its consistency with integration, world-modelling, and recursive self-modelling frameworks.

**Keywords:** consciousness; free computation; criticality; edge of chaos; self-model; second-order computation; self-referential closure; altered states; disorders of consciousness

## 1. Introduction

Is there more to consciousness than computation? The answer defended here is yes — and the "more" is itself computation. What a base account leaves out is not a special substance and not a special substrate but a *second-order* regime: computation that operates on computation, a system's model of itself taking its own modelling as an object. Consciousness, on this view, is what a modelling system becomes when it integrates its own implicit models into its explicit ones, feeds the result to a model of itself, and that self-model comes to treat itself as the very being it models — computation on top of computation, the modelling of modelling.

Two claims carry the argument. The first is a condition of possibility: such second-order modelling requires *free*, open-ended computation, and in neural tissue free computation is realised as near-criticality, dynamics poised at the edge of chaos between frozen order and unstructured disorder. The second is constitutive: experience is made by the integration and self-conflation just described, not by the criticality that enables it.

These two claims stand in a definite relation. Free computation — near-criticality, in a brain — is the *enabling* condition, and in biological tissue the necessity runs one way: no criticality, no consciousness. Criticality does not by itself produce experience. A cortex can be near-critical and run heavy modelling that is wholly unconscious; what it then lacks is not free computation but the constitutive step — the integration of implicit into explicit models and the self-model's conflation of itself with its bearer. Criticality is thus the expected *signature* of consciousness, present wherever consciousness is, without being sufficient for it.

The special issue frames its question as computationalism versus implementationism — whether consciousness depends on the computations a system performs or on the physical details of how they are implemented. The four-model theory is computationalist. What makes a system conscious is that it runs the right kind of computation — a self-referential, second-order regime — not that it is built of neurons or any privileged material; the regime is multiply realizable in principle. The theory's central neural evidence is a criticality requirement, and "criticality required" can be misread as an implementationist demand on the substrate. It is not. Criticality is how the required computation shows up in neural tissue — the form an open-ended dynamical computation takes when it runs on spiking cortex — not a stipulation about what the tissue must be made of. A different substrate could realize the same second-order computation on different physics and need not display neuronal-avalanche statistics at all; this positioning is developed in Section 4 and its scope fenced in Section 8.

The field this paper enters has no settled paradigm. A recent adversarial collaboration pitted leading frameworks against one another with results that partly matched integrated information theory's prediction of a posterior hot zone — decodable stimulus content was found there — while failing its prediction of sustained posterior synchrony, and showed weaker-than-predicted prefrontal ignition for global neuronal workspace, confirming neither decisively (Cogitate Consortium, 2025). Surveys describe a proliferation of incompatible accounts with no agreed method for adjudicating among them (Seth and Bayne, 2022). The four-model theory offers a reframe of what the target *is* — a computational regime — and, from that reframe, an account of a fact the search for neural correlates has recorded but not explained: why conscious level co-varies so tightly with cortical criticality.

The full framework — its treatment of the hard problem (Chalmers, 1995), the binding problem, and altered states — is set out in a companion preprint (Gruber, 2026a). This paper develops one part of it: that free computation, seen in the brain as criticality, is a necessary condition for the second-order computation that constitutes experience, and that this necessity yields a testable prediction about when consciousness is present.

## 2. An operational anchor for "consciousness"

The target here is the field's standard operational notion of consciousness, not a definition tailored to the conclusion. It is phenomenal consciousness in the minimal sense: the presence of subjective experience, there being "something it is like" to be a system in a given state (Nagel, 1974). This is distinguished, as standardly, from access consciousness — the availability of information for report and control — because a system can be experientially present without being able to report (Block, 1995). The field individuates conscious states by converging report-based and no-report markers of presence versus absence. Behavioural responsiveness and subjective report anchor the easy cases; where those fail — sleep, anaesthesia, disorders of consciousness — the best-validated stimulus-and-record index is perturbational complexity, the compressibility of the cortical response to a direct perturbation, which separates conscious from unconscious states across sleep, anaesthesia, and brain injury and stratifies unresponsive patients who retain covert experience (Casali et al., 2013; Casarotto et al., 2016). This operational consensus is what the theory takes as given.

The four-model theory adds to this target rather than replacing it. Where the operational programme measures *whether* experience is present, the theory says *what is present when it is*: an ongoing self-model, generated by the system, that models the world, the system within that world, and its own modelling. The architecture is a taxonomy of **two model classes along two dimensions** — scope (world versus self) and mode (implicit versus explicit) — whose crossing yields four model *kinds*: implicit and explicit models of the world, and implicit and explicit models of the self. The implicit kinds are substrate-level, learned, and non-phenomenal; the explicit kinds are generated and transient, and phenomenal where they enter the self-model's closed loop. These are kinds of modelling, not four anatomical modules, and the biological substrate blends them continuously — a reaching movement models world-geometry and self-kinematics at once. The full exposition is given in the companion preprint (Gruber, 2026a). What matters here is one structural fact about the explicit self-model: it takes the system's own modelling as its object, and that self-interaction is the second-order computation the argument turns on.

## 3. Free computation as a necessary condition, and the integration that constitutes experience

### 3.1 Consciousness requires free computation

A system that models its own modelling — that can take any of its own states, including its modelling, as a further object, with no fixed ceiling on the depth of self-reference it may require — cannot be captured by a computation of a fixed finite type, since any such type fixes in advance a modelling depth the process may exceed. What it requires is *free*, open-ended computation: computation whose expressive class is not bounded in advance. This is weaker than Turing-universality, which is neither claimed nor needed; the point is only that no fixed finite ceiling can be imposed ahead of time.

Where, in a dynamical medium, can such computation run? In a system that computes through its own evolving activity rather than through a clocked digital register, open-ended computation is supported at — and in the relevant limit confined to — the edge of chaos, for an information-theoretic reason. Deep in the ordered regime, dynamics freeze into fixed points or short cycles: information is stored but not transported, so no new structured relationship can be composed across the system. Deep in the chaotic regime, dynamics decorrelate almost immediately: information is transported but not stored, so no relationship persists long enough to be operated on. Open-ended computation needs both capacities jointly — active information storage and information transfer — and they are co-maximised only near the critical point, which is why edge-of-chaos dynamics support an open-ended, self-referential computation that ordered and chaotic dynamics cannot (Langton, 1990; Bertschinger and Natschläger, 2004). A system can reach and hold this regime by its own internal dynamics rather than by external fine-tuning (Bak, Tang, and Wiesenfeld, 1987), realising it — in cortex — as a driven, slightly-subcritical variant. The point invoked here is structural, not generic: not that criticality is optimal for information processing at large, but that a *dynamical* realisation of open-ended, self-referential computation must occupy this regime, because the two capacities such a computation needs are jointly available nowhere else.

This gives the necessity, scoped to neural tissue. The second-order computation that consciousness requires can be carried, in a brain, only near the edge of chaos, so a cortex displaced far from criticality cannot carry it: no criticality, no consciousness. The necessity runs one way. That a cortex is near-critical does not entail that the second-order computation is running on it — a near-critical substrate can carry first-order modelling that never closes into experience. Criticality is the enabling substrate, not the thing enabled.

### 3.2 Criticality tracks consciousness across state transitions

The prediction that consciousness requires near-criticality meets evidence already assembled independently of any theory. Two distinct notions of criticality are in play, and they must be kept apart. *Avalanche criticality* concerns a branching process: cortical activity propagates as cascades whose sizes follow a power law, first characterised in neocortical circuits and interpreted as self-organised criticality (Beggs and Plenz, 2003), with a branching-ratio control parameter that is subcritical when activity dies out and supercritical when it runs away. *Dynamical*, or edge-of-chaos, criticality concerns the position of the collective dynamics relative to the boundary between ordered and chaotic evolution. The two are related but not identical, and they can move apart across brain states. In behaving animals the waking avalanche set-point is slightly subcritical, held a controlled distance below the branching transition (Priesemann et al., 2014); what cortex realises is self-organisation *toward*, not exactly at, the critical point, and the set-point is actively maintained — when perturbed away from it, homeostatic mechanisms restore it over hours in vivo (Ma et al., 2019).

The link to consciousness specifically comes from state-transition data, and it is the *edge-of-chaos distance* that carries it. The distance of slow cortical electrodynamics from the edge of chaos tracks conscious level across anaesthesia, seizures, psychedelic states and disorders of consciousness: the dynamics sit near the critical boundary when experience is present and are displaced from it when experience is abolished (Toker et al., 2022). The displacement is not confined to one side. Propofol anaesthesia moves the dynamics toward the ordered, stable side; ketamine and classical psychedelics move them the other way, closer to the edge from the slightly-ordered waking baseline, yet preserve experience in altered form and independently raise signal diversity (Toker et al., 2022; Sarasso et al., 2015; Schartner et al., 2017). What abolishes experience is a departure large enough to leave the near-critical band: the psychedelic shift stays near the band and alters experience rather than abolishing it, whereas the ordered-side departure of anaesthesia leaves the band and abolishes it. The claim is keyed to distance from the critical point, not to a single side. Recent syntheses treat criticality as a candidate unifying set-point of brain function (Hengen and Shew, 2025) and as a common substrate proposed to underlie multiple theories of consciousness (Algom and Shriki, 2026).

Read for the necessity claim, this evidence says one thing: wherever experience is present the cortex is near-critical, and leaving the near-critical band abolishes experience. It does not say that near-criticality guarantees experience, and it should not be read that way — the branching side-label alone cannot even separate conscious from unconscious cortex. Measured by avalanche statistics, deep slow-wave sleep is not further into the subcritical regime than waking but if anything closer to the branching critical point, with larger avalanches (Priesemann et al., 2013), and sustained wakefulness *depletes* avalanche-criticality signatures that sleep restores (Meisel et al., 2013). The edge-of-chaos distance separates the states the branching label cannot, and the theory commits to it, treating the avalanche family as a related signature that need not co-move with conscious state. So stated, the correlation is *shared*: it belongs to the whole criticality literature and is not, at this stage, specific to the theory advanced here. The theory's contribution is the reason behind it.

### 3.3 The integration that constitutes experience

Necessity is not constitution. Criticality tells us where, in a brain, the second-order computation *can* run; it does not tell us what that computation *is*, or why running it amounts to experience. This is the theory's constitutive claim, and it is separate from the criticality requirement.

Base, first-order computation is the modelling of world and self — perceiving, acting, predicting. The implicit models hold learned structure stored in the substrate's connectivity and evaluated by substrate processing, not itself represented; the explicit models generate a transient, structured scene and self. Much of this proceeds unconsciously. Conscious experience arises through a specific further operation on that first-order computation. Information carried implicitly — in the substrate's own learned dynamics — is integrated into the explicit models, and the integrated content is fed to the explicit self-model. The self-model then does something peculiar: it *confuses itself with the being it occupies*. It does not represent itself as a model among the system's models; it takes itself to be the system — the one perceiving, the one acting, the subject the scene is *for*. That conflation, running on the integrated implicit-to-explicit content, is what it is for the system to have an experience. Experience is constituted by the integration and the self-conflation, not by the criticality that lets the computation run.

This is why a near-critical cortex can be unconscious. If, in some phase of sleep, cortex deploys its explicit models heavily — running rich unconscious modelling — but does not integrate that content into a self-model that conflates itself with its bearer, the constitutive step is missing though the enabling substrate is present. The state is near-critical and unconscious, and the theory expects exactly this: criticality is necessary for the computation but does not compel the self-conflation that makes the computation an experience. The necessity is one-directional for precisely this reason.

The same structure separates a self-*model* from mere self-*monitoring*, and shows why an all-purpose digital computer, however powerful, is not conscious on this account. A system that monitors its own states — a supervisor process reading out a first process — is not thereby closed: the monitor stands outside what it monitors, and the whole arrangement can be described from a vantage point where the supervised process is here and the supervisor there. Closure is the case in which no such external vantage remains within the system: the self-model models the world, the system in the world, and its own modelling of both, so the modelling process is among the things it models (Gruber, 2026a). A universal digital computer can compute anything computable, but the objects it models are supplied to it from outside as inputs and goals; its self-modelling, where it has any, is *heteronomous*. What it runs is first-order computation over externally supplied objects, not a self-directed modelling that closes back on itself and mistakes itself for the system. Autonomy here is a property of *what is computed* — whether the models are endogenously generated and self-directed — not of the hardware or its clock. This marks two distinct thresholds. Criticality, in a brain, is the *substrate* threshold: it enables the second-order computation but does not constitute it. The architecture — the integration of implicit into explicit models and the self-model's closure and self-conflation — is the threshold that sets whether, and how deeply, experience is present. The substrate enables the computation; the architecture makes it an experience.

### 3.4 From efficiency property to expected signature

Why, then, is the cortex critical? The standard answer is optimisation, and it is largely correct: critical dynamics maximise dynamic range (Shew et al., 2009), peak susceptibility and correlation length so that small inputs propagate and distant regions co-vary (Chialvo, 2010), and are actively tuned toward criticality by homeostasis (Ma et al., 2019; Hengen and Shew, 2025). Criticality is useful, and the machinery that maintains it is real. What the optimisation account does not explain is why the maintained set-point should co-vary with *conscious level* specifically, rather than with cortical metabolic rate, mean firing rate, or information throughput — why criticality tracks consciousness rather than a processing benefit.

The four-model theory supplies the missing reason without contradicting the optimisation account: criticality co-varies with consciousness because it is the necessary substrate for the second-order computation established in Section 3.1 — the computation that, with the integration and self-conflation of Section 3.3, constitutes experience. Wherever that computation runs, the cortex must be near-critical; where the cortex leaves the near-critical band, the computation cannot run and experience goes with it. The correlation the literature has recorded for two decades is, on this reading, the expected signature of a necessity condition — not a coincidence and not merely an efficiency property, but the fingerprint left by the fact that the computation which makes experience can be carried, in a brain, only at the edge of chaos. It was read as efficiency because it announces no measurable ability: a subject at criticality is not thereby faster or higher-scoring on any task, so the signature presents as a maintained set-point rather than as a cognitive advantage, and its tie to experience went unremarked.

## 4. Computationalist, not implementationist

The account just given fixes the theory's position on the special issue's axis. Consciousness, here, is a computation of a particular self-referential form together with the integration and self-conflation that make it an experience — a fully physical pattern a system enters and sustains, with no extra ingredient added to the physics: no experiential essence, no privileged material. What carries phenomenal character is the *organisation* of the computation, not the medium (Chalmers, 1996). The same second-order computation, integration, and self-conflation, run on different physics, would be the same consciousness. The theory is therefore computationalist and multiply realizable in principle, not implementationist.

Criticality does not compromise this commitment. It enters as the form the required computation takes in neural tissue — the way an open-ended dynamical computation is bound to reveal itself when it runs on spiking cortex — not as a demand that consciousness be built of any particular stuff. This is the distinction between a computational property and a causal-structure-of-the-substrate property; accounts that locate consciousness in the causal structure of the implementing physics face well-known difficulties precisely because they tie experience to substrate detail (Doerig et al., 2019). The four-model theory ties experience to a computation and treats the neural criticality signature as evidence that the computation is running, not as a condition on the hardware. A different substrate could realize the same computation on different physics and display none of the avalanche statistics of cortex.

The 2×2 of model kinds belongs here as the taxonomy of *what closes the loop*, not as a parts list. The four kinds are the extremal corners of a continuous scope-by-mode space — world versus self, implicit versus explicit; consciousness requires that a system model both scopes in both modes and close its explicit self-model back onto the whole. Naming "four" names the corners of the space, not four boxes in the brain, and the biological substrate maintains innumerable overlapping models that blend across the world/self boundary. The architectural minimum is closure over the full space, not a count of modules.

## 5. Operationalising the necessity claim: what would disconfirm it

The necessity claim — in biological brains, consciousness requires near-criticality — is stated here so that a specified result would defeat it. It is not protected by generality: a single class of observation would break it.

**Measurement: convergence across independent methods.** Criticality in neural tissue is not read off any single statistic. The commitment is to convergence across *methodologically independent* families, not restatements of one measurement. The first family is avalanche/branching statistics: a branching ratio approaching unity together with a power-law avalanche-size distribution near the mean-field exponent of −3/2 (Beggs and Plenz, 2003). These two are views of one branching process — biased in the same direction by subsampling and thresholding of the same event-detected data (Touboul and Destexhe, 2017) — so subsampling-invariant estimators are required (Priesemann et al., 2014), and the operational criterion is proximity to the canonical values within measurement tolerance, since reported exponents scatter with modality and analysis choices (O'Byrne and Jerbi, 2022). The second family is the long-range temporal correlation of the oscillatory envelope, a detrended-fluctuation-analysis scaling exponent in the range characteristic of criticality — roughly 0.6 to 0.9 (Hardstone et al., 2012); it uses different data and a different estimator and so fails, when it fails, for different reasons. A third and more independent handle is the edge-of-chaos distance of the slow electrodynamics, which — unlike the avalanche family — is directional and is the measure shown to track conscious level (Toker et al., 2022).

**Primary measure and the direction problem.** For state discrimination the primary measure is the edge-of-chaos distance-to-criticality, because it both tracks consciousness across states and carries *direction* — whether a departure is toward the ordered or the disordered side (Toker et al., 2022). Direction matters because the detrended-fluctuation exponent, though the most reliably estimable tissue-level signature from scalp or intracranial recordings without single-unit resolution (Hardstone et al., 2012), indexes distance to criticality but not its side: the exponent is maximal near critical excitation–inhibition balance and falls on *both* flanks. It is therefore retained as the primary *tissue-level* signature — scalable and artefact-resistant — while a directional statistic (the edge-of-chaos margin, or the branching ratio where spike-resolution data permit) fixes sub- versus supercritical departure. A quantitative criterion, stated in advance, fixes what "near-critical" and "demonstrably off-critical" mean, so the disconfirmer cannot be retuned after the fact: on the edge-of-chaos margin, the near-critical band is calibrated so that the waking set-point — slightly subcritical, σ≈0.98 (Priesemann et al., 2014) — falls inside it, and a state counts as demonstrably off-critical when its margin lies beyond that band by a pre-set multiple of the between-subject waking variance. The band is set once and applied identically to all states.

**The disconfirmer.** The claim put up for test is a necessity, scoped to biological cortex: consciousness requires near-critical cortical dynamics. It is disconfirmed by one observation — **robust, reportable consciousness while the cortical edge-of-chaos margin lies demonstrably outside the pre-set near-critical band on the primary measure** (and, where the data permit, on the avalanche and long-range-correlation signatures too). A conscious cortex that is demonstrably off-critical breaks the necessity, and the theory has no spare parameter to absorb it. The test has an interventional form that strengthens it beyond correlation: across anaesthetic, sleep, and pathological transitions, driving cortical dynamics out of the near-critical band should abolish consciousness, and restoring near-criticality should return it, with the loss and the return tracking the distance-to-criticality measure rather than any independent processing variable. A dissociation in which consciousness survives the loss of near-criticality, or fails to return when near-criticality is restored, would count against the claim.

**What does not disconfirm it.** The converse case — a near-critical cortex that is unconscious — is predicted, not excluded. Because criticality is necessary and not sufficient, a cortex may sit at the edge of chaos while running unconscious modelling that never integrates into a self-conflating self-model, as some phases of sleep may. The necessity runs one way; only the off-critical-yet-conscious case can break it. Spatial *extent* — the fraction of cortex within the near-critical band, developed as a construct in Section 6 — would sharpen the picture, but there is at present no validated method returning a spatially-resolved edge-of-chaos margin across human cortex, so the load-bearing falsification rests on the whole-cortex signature families and the edge-of-chaos margin, which are measurable now.

**The false-positive caveat.** Power laws alone are not criticality: heavy-tailed distributions and apparent scaling can arise from mechanisms unrelated to a critical point, a failure mode the literature documents carefully (Touboul and Destexhe, 2017; Clauset, Shalizi, and Newman, 2009), and reviews warn against reading criticality off a single power-law fit (O'Byrne and Jerbi, 2022). Convergence across the independent families above is the answer to this hazard: a spurious power law in the avalanche family does not by itself satisfy the criterion, which also requires the independent envelope-correlation signature and the directional edge-of-chaos margin.

**Shared evidence and the specific claim.** The state-transition data of Section 3.2 are post-hoc corroborations: they already exist, they are consistent with the necessity claim, and they are shared with the whole criticality literature. What the four-model theory adds is not the correlation but its explanation — that near-criticality is required because it is the enabling substrate for the second-order computation that constitutes experience — and the interventional necessity test above is what puts that explanation's central commitment at risk.

## 6. A further consequence: two dials and subjective time

The architecture yields consequences beyond the necessity claim; one is given here to show its specificity. The edge-of-chaos substrate carries two dials that vary independently. One is *extent* — how much of the cortex is recruited into near-critical dynamics. The other is *complexity* — how rich the computation running on the recruited tissue is, the differentiation of its activity. Extent is roughly an integration axis, complexity roughly a differentiation axis; because they are independent, a state is characterised by its position on both, not by a single scalar of arousal. Normal waking rides a frontier balancing the two.

The conscious "now" is, on this account, a constructed frame rather than a direct read-out of physical time — perception is sampled and assembled, not continuously streamed (VanRullen and Koch, 2003) — and its effective clock speed is not fixed. When the complexity dial is driven to an extreme while the frame's construction is altered, the architecture predicts subjective time-dilation: a large quantity of structured content is assembled and indexed within what external clocks register as a brief interval, so the interval is *lived* as far longer. This is the profile of the near-death "life review," in which survivors of acute danger report a panoramic, richly detailed re-experiencing compressed into seconds (Noyes and Kletti, 1977), and of deep-absorption reports of "eternity in a moment." These last are phenomenological reports, not instrument-validated measurements; what is measurable with a validated instrument is the ego-dissolution component that often accompanies such states (Nour et al., 2016), not the time-distortion itself. Consistent with the constitutive account, states of deep meditative absorption have been reported to shift cortical dynamics *toward* criticality (Mago et al., 2025, preprint).

The two-dial picture supplies a negative control: the generalised tonic-clonic seizure. A generalised seizure produces near-maximal co-activation — vast swathes of cortex firing together — yet its slow dynamics become hypersynchronous and *periodic*, a departure toward the ordered, hyper-stable side of the edge of chaos rather than an intensification of near-critical computation (Toker et al., 2022). In this it joins deep anaesthesia rather than opposing it: both abolish consciousness by leaving the near-critical band toward stability, the seizure into periodic lockstep and anaesthesia into quiescence. The seizure is the sharper case only because it does so at near-maximal co-activation, which is exactly what separates raw co-activation from proximity to criticality — a co-activation account expects consciousness where a distance-to-criticality account expects its loss, and the data follow criticality. Adaptive self-organised criticality breaks down as the cortex approaches seizure, its long-range temporal correlations collapsing (Meisel et al., 2012): co-activation is high while the dynamics have left the near-critical band, and consciousness is abolished — as a picture that keys consciousness to distance-from-criticality, not co-activation, requires. Its full treatment, with the other altered states, is in the companion preprint (Gruber, 2026a).

## 7. Convergence with existing frameworks

The field is independently converging on three ingredients as the substrate of consciousness: criticality, integration-and-differentiation, and recursive self-modelling. The four-model theory is consistent with this convergence and adds one claim — that these are enabling conditions for a single thing, the second-order self-referential computation that, with the integration and self-conflation of Section 3, constitutes experience. None of the frameworks below is claimed to *test* the theory; convergence is not confirmation.

**Integrated World Modeling Theory.** Safron's account unifies predictive-processing, global-workspace, and integrated-information elements under a system that jointly models world and self through active inference, and that integration is its real strength (Safron, 2020). The four-model theory shares the constitutive commitment to joint world-*and*-self modelling and is consistent with the active-inference framing of the modelling process. What it adds is the real/virtual level split — the distinction between the non-phenomenal implicit substrate and the explicit simulation, phenomenal in the self-model's closed loop — and the identification of criticality as the enabling dynamical regime for the closure that binds world- and self-models together.

**Recursive and fractal self-modelling.** Bieberich's recurrent fractal neural network frames cortical self-reference as self-similar organisation across scales, formalising the intuition that self-modelling is recursive and nested (Bieberich, 2002). The four-model theory shares the emphasis on recursion depth and self-referential organisation; its graduated ladder of self-modelling — first-, second-, and third-order self-reference — is a specific reading of the recursive-self-modelling intuition the fractal approach expresses in the language of self-similarity. What it adds is the dynamical grounding: the recursion must be carried at criticality, which is where an unbounded self-referential process can actually run in a dynamical substrate.

**The Beautiful Loop.** Laukkonen, Friston, and Chandaria give an active-inference account in which consciousness is a closed self-modelling loop with "epistemic depth" — a system inferring its own inferential states (Laukkonen, Friston, and Chandaria, 2025). This is the same structural insight as the four-model theory's second-order "computation upon computation," reached from an active-inference rather than a dynamical-systems starting point. The two are complementary descriptions of closure: one specifies the inferential architecture of the loop, the other the dynamical regime the loop must occupy. That two independent formalisms converge on self-referential closure is mutual corroboration of the target, not of either theory's specifics.

**Integrated Information Theory and the neural-complexity tradition.** Integrated information theory identifies consciousness with integrated information and develops the proposal with unusual mathematical rigour, formalising both the integration and the differentiation of a system's cause-effect structure (Tononi, 2004; Oizumi, Albantakis, and Tononi, 2014; Albantakis et al., 2023); it stands in a longer tradition of quantifying neural complexity as the balance of integration and differentiation (Tononi, Sporns, and Edelman, 1994). The four-model theory's two dials — extent as an integration axis, complexity as a differentiation axis — sit squarely in this tradition and claim no priority over it. What the theory adds is to place *both* dials on one edge-of-chaos substrate and to isolate the rare corner in which they are jointly maximised, which is where the second-order computation runs. The theory takes no position in the dispute over integrated information theory's scientific status; it borrows the integration/differentiation structure and locates it dynamically.

**Complexity indices of consciousness.** Perturbational complexity and Lempel-Ziv-based signal-diversity measures operationalise the differentiation dial directly and already discriminate conscious from unconscious states — separating propofol and xenon unresponsiveness from ketamine's preserved but disconnected experience, and tracking the raised diversity of psychedelic states (Casali et al., 2013; Sarasso et al., 2015; Schartner et al., 2017). Their strength is that they are validated and in routine clinical and experimental use. For the four-model theory this matters twice over: these indices measure its complexity dial, and their existing deployment means much of the theory's test can be run with instruments already in use — the tissue-level criticality signatures and these complexity measures are standard, so the integration with the empirical programme does not depend on the author's own laboratory, even though the spatially-resolved extent measure of Section 6 remains to be built.

## 8. Scope, limits, and the substrate-neutral requirement

The necessity claim is fenced deliberately. "No criticality, no consciousness" is scoped to **biological neural tissue**. Criticality is the *form* the required open-ended computation takes in a neural substrate — a non-universal signature of that computation running on spiking cortex, not a substrate-neutral necessity. The substrate-neutral requirement is stated at a different level: open-ended computation deployed for autonomous self-modelling. A neural substrate meeting that requirement will, as a matter of its physics, exhibit neuronal-avalanche statistics and near-critical dynamics; a different substrate meeting the same requirement need not. A digital realization of the same second-order computation could satisfy the substrate-neutral condition while showing none of the avalanche signatures, because its open-endedness would be supplied architecturally rather than won from dynamics. The biological claim is therefore not asserted as substrate-neutral: it would be false at that level.

A companion treatment develops the substrate-neutral side directly, testing closure and open-ended computation as enabling conditions for world-modelling in an artificial architecture, where the necessary condition is open-ended computation deployed for autonomous self-modelling rather than any biology-specific criticality signature (Gruber, 2026b). The two papers state one condition seen from two sides: in neural tissue it appears as criticality; in a substrate-neutral setting it appears as open-ended computation. Keeping the biological claim scoped is what holds the two accounts consistent — the neural criticality requirement is the local, non-universal appearance of the general requirement, not a competitor to it.

The limits are real. There is as yet no full mathematical formalization of the architecture; the criticality requirement is specified through measurable dynamical signatures but not derived from a closed formal model of the four model kinds, and that derivation is outstanding. A residual bridging commitment remains — the step from the integration-and-self-conflation at criticality to lived phenomenal character — and it is shared by every theory that connects a physical or computational condition to experience; the four-model theory locates the step precisely (the self-model's conflation of itself with its bearer is the condition under which the gap between process and felt state does not arise) but does not claim to have eliminated the philosophical burden (Chalmers, 1995). The other-minds problem bounds any extension to artificial systems: even a system built to the substrate-neutral specification could not be *verified* conscious from outside, a limit shared with every account that permits artificial consciousness (Butlin et al., 2026; Findlay et al., 2024). And the power-law-detection caveats of Section 5 apply in full. That biological cognition is realized on strikingly different substrates across taxa — the cortex-free but plainly capable pallium of corvids among them (Güntürkün and Bugnyar, 2016) — is a reminder that the neural criticality signature is one substrate's way of meeting a general requirement, not the requirement itself.

## 9. Conclusion

Is there more to consciousness than base computation? Yes — and the more is made of computation: a second-order regime in which computation operates on computation, integrated into and closed upon a self-model that confuses itself with the being it occupies. Two claims carry the account. First, a necessity: this second-order computation requires free, open-ended computation, and in a brain that requirement is met only near the edge of chaos — no criticality, no consciousness. The necessity runs one way; a near-critical cortex can still run unconscious modelling, because criticality enables the computation without constituting the experience. Second, the constitutive claim: experience is made by the integration of implicit into explicit models and the self-model's conflation of itself with its bearer, not by the criticality that lets the computation run. Read through these two claims, the field's decades-old criticality signature stops being a coincidence or a mere efficiency property and becomes the expected fingerprint of a necessity condition: the computation that constitutes experience can be carried, in neural tissue, only at criticality, so wherever consciousness is, criticality is. The optimisation programme explained why criticality is *useful*; the four-model theory adds why it is *necessary* — it is the enabling substrate for the second-order self-modelling that experience is made of. The necessity is testable: convergent signatures from methodologically independent measures, a quantified distance-to-criticality criterion, and an interventional prediction — abolish near-criticality and consciousness goes; restore it and consciousness returns; find consciousness in a demonstrably off-critical cortex and the claim is wrong.
