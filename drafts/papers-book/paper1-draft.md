# Limits Without an Outside: Why the Limits of Embedded Description Travel as Refutations

**Matthias Gruber**\
Independent researcher · ORCID 0009-0005-9697-1665 · research@matthiasgruber.com

2026-10-20 · Preprint, version 1

## Abstract

Every limitative thesis about description appears to be stated from the standpoint it denies. Priest accepts the contradiction as true and Nagel leaves a tension; a third option keeps classical consistency. For any describer that is a proper part of what it describes, two limits hold unconditionally: no perspective holds its own current generation, as a whole, as content (a limit on the act in progress, near-analytic given the frame/content distinction and not a diagonal argument), and no part can certify its own description of the whole complete. Three further limits (Kolmogorov–Chaitin counting, computational irreducibility, Gödel's theorems) hold only under stated conditions. All five share one form: the generator can appear in what it generates, but not completely. The limits are not offered as positive truths held from nowhere. They are held as refutations: impossibility claims whose negations are inconsistent with positional input, the input every claimant presupposes in claiming, namely being embedded and having a frame, read through the account's notions of frame and certification. A claimant that admits that input is contradicted; one that refuses it is left with a claim it cannot assert. Such refutations travel where positive claims do not: falsity relative to admitted input needs no comparison with the world, is permanent, as a fact about the set, once exhibited, and is inherited by every successor that keeps the input. The Duhem–Quine point about which member of a tested set is false is conceded; holism leaves the inconsistency of the set standing. The dialetheist who accepts the exhibited contradiction as true is not reached.

**Keywords:** embedded observers; limits of thought; self-reference; self-refutation; falsification; Duhem–Quine thesis

## 1. Introduction

Limitative theses about thought and description share a difficulty. Nagel (1986) holds that the view from nowhere can be approached and never reached; Putnam (1981) that there is no God's Eye point of view "that we can know or usefully imagine" (p. 50); Priest (1995) that the limits of thought are both crossed and not crossed. Each claim seems to be made from the place it declares out of reach. Burnyeat (1976) reconstructs the turning-around argument, the *peritrope*, by which Protagorean relativism is made to refute itself, and Mackie (1964) classifies the ways a claim can undermine its own assertion. Priest (1994, 1995) takes the difficulty as a datum: at the limits of thought a totality and the operation that carries one beyond it produce a true contradiction, and the limit is crossed and not crossed at once. Nagel keeps the tension and declines the contradiction. Moore (1997) takes a different route: absolute representations are possible, and what cannot be stated about the limits of perspectival representation is known ineffably.

There is a third option, and it keeps classical consistency without anything ineffable. The limits on a description held by a proper part of what it describes can be stated without claiming a view from outside, because they are not positive truths about all perspectives. They are refutations. Each limit is an impossibility claim, and the support for it is that its negation, asserted by any perspective whatever, contradicts input that perspective presupposes in making claims at all, and admits once it states its presuppositions: that it is embedded in what it describes and that it holds its content within a frame. It reaches another perspective as a contradiction that perspective can check for itself.

The limits themselves are established: the self-prediction results of Popper (1950), MacKay (1960) and, in measurement form, Breuer (1995); Wolpert's (2008) law-independent impossibility results for inference devices embedded in the universe they infer about; Ryle's (1949) systematically elusive "I" that no higher-order commentary can include; the unification of Cantor, Russell, Gödel and Tarski as one fixed-point argument (Lawvere 1969), extended to Turing (Yanofsky 2003) and to embedded observers (Szangolies 2018); and the endophysics programme (Svozil 1993). What follows concerns what these limits license epistemically. First, a taxonomy: two of the limits hold for any proper part of any whole, with no assumption about the whole beyond that, while the better-known three (capacity, irreducibility, incompleteness) hold only where the world or the system meets a stated condition. Second, an account of why the limits survive their own application: they travel as refutations, and refutations, unlike positive claims, need no outside. Third, a formulation of falsity without truth, falsity as inconsistency between a reference system and the input it admits, inherited by every successor that keeps the input, which concedes the Duhem–Quine point about which member of a tested set is false (Quine 1951; Lakatos 1970) and states what holism leaves standing, without redefining truth.

The argument does not depend on any theory of mind. The notion of a reference system is taken from an account of knowledge on which knowledge is information assigned to a reference system and a reference system is a model (Gruber 2015, which equates the two for its purposes, as is done here); nothing below needs more than "a describer holds a model and admits input". The limits apply on the same terms to a brain describing a world that contains the brain, to a formal theory describing a structure in which its own proofs can be coded, and to a machine describing the universe that runs it.[^1]

## 2. Embedded describers

Let W be a whole and D a proper part of W that describes W. W is the maximal whole D describes, everything D admits as existing; for descriptions of lesser wholes the limits do not apply, and a describer outside such a whole can see about it what its parts cannot (Section 4). Four terms are needed.

**Frame and content.** D holds descriptions as content. Whatever D uses to hold, distinguish and operate on content is its frame. The distinction is relational: a structure that is content for one holder may be frame for another, and a part of D's frame may be represented as content by D itself. Within one perspective at one time, the frame is what does the distinguishing and the content is what is distinguished. A content item is complete at the time it is held: to distinguish an item is to operate on a product, and what the frame operates on at *t* is whatever its producing has delivered by *t*; the producing itself, as it goes on at *t*, is not yet a product and so not yet an item the frame distinguishes. This clause is part of the notion of a frame used here, and it is the premise L1 rests on (Section 3); a claimant who rejects it, holding that content can be unfinished at the time it is held, is not reached by L1 (Section 8). A perspective is a describer with a frame and content. Anything that distinguishes something from something else does so by a frame, so anything that distinguishes anything is a perspective. A supposed view from outside everything, which would hold every structure and distinguish nothing, has no frame, describes nothing, and is not a perspective, so the limits below make no claim about it.

**Generation.** The generation of D's content at a time is the frame-side process by which that content is produced and held at that time. D's current generation is the generation of the content D now holds. Parts of a generation can appear as content: a system can hold a model of itself, a formal system can code its own proof relation, an organism can represent its own body. The question is whether the generation can appear as content whole.

**Admitted input.** D's admitted input is the set of items D accepts as premises for its claims and uses in drawing them: observations, axioms, rules of inference, and facts about its own position. Input is admitted, not given. Which items D admits is up to D, and D can withdraw an item; anything D uses in a check is thereby admitted input. Two items D cannot withdraw while remaining a describer that makes claims about W: that D is a proper part of W, and that D holds its claims within a frame, which follows from making distinctions at all. Call these D's positional input. Positional input is admitted in a second sense as well: not accepted as a premise but presupposed by any claim about W. Section 6 separates the claimant that admits its position in the first sense, whom the refutations reach as a contradiction, from one that only presupposes it, whom they reach as an operational inconsistency in Mackie's (1964) sense.

**Completeness and certification.** A description $\Delta$ held by D is complete if, together with the procedures D can run, it fixes every fact about W that it claims to cover. D certifies $\Delta$ complete if D establishes, from its admitted input and by procedures it can run, that nothing $\Delta$ claims to cover remains unfixed. Certification may rest on admitted items, with one exception: an admitted claim about the complement of the input, that there is nothing the input does not report, is not a ground of certification but a restatement of what was to be certified. Every other admitted item can in principle be checked against further input; that one can be checked only against what lies outside all of it. Completeness is a property $\Delta$ may have; certification is an act of D. The two come apart, and L2 concerns the act, for descriptions whose claimed scope is W entire; a description of restricted claimed scope can be certified complete, which is one of the positive verdicts Section 5 leaves available.

A reference system in the sense of Section 1 is a model, and a model held by a proper part is a $\Delta$ in the sense above.

## 3. Two limits without conditions

**L1. No perspective holds its own current generation, as a whole, as content.**

The argument concerns the act now in progress, and it concerns what a content item is, not what it represents. Suppose D at time *t* holds a content item *c*, and suppose *c* is, or has as a part, D's current generation G*t* as a whole. The holding of *c* at *t* is part of G*t*, and at *t* it is under way: it is the producing of *c*, not a product. By the frame/content relation of Section 2, *c* is complete at *t*: whatever *c* is or has as a part is, at *t*, a finished item. So the holding of *c*, unfinished at *t*, is neither *c* nor a part of *c*; and since the holding is part of G*t*, G*t* as a whole is neither *c* nor a part of *c*. What D can hold is a model of its generation, a part of it or a past state of it, including a complete one. What it cannot hold, at the time of holding, is the generation doing the holding.

Without the two restrictions, to the act in progress and to what content is as against what it represents, L1 would be false. Kleene's (1938) recursion theorem gives, for any computable transformation *f* of programs, a program *e* with $\varphi_e = \varphi_{f(e)}$; a corollary is a program that prints its own text, a quine, whose output completely describes the program, including the instruction that produces the output. Complete self-description, production included, is possible, and L1 does not deny it; nor does it deny that a finished item can fix an act still in progress, as a program text together with its current state fixes the rest of the run. What a quine's output cannot be, or have as a part, is the run that produces it. The regress usually offered for L1 (a representation of the holding needs a further act to hold it, and so on) needs a further premise, that a representation of an act is produced by an act distinct from the one represented, and the recursion theorem shows that premise false for completed descriptions. The argument for L1 given above does not use that premise.

L1 is near-analytic. Given the frame/content relation of Section 2, it says that the holding is not among what is held at the time of holding, which is close to what Section 2 means by a frame; Ryle (1949, ch. 6) gives the experiential form, a higher-order commentary on one's present act that cannot include that commentary. Nothing in L1 uses counting or the size of part, whole or frame. It is used twice: it closes the last route of L2, and its negation, asserted by any perspective, is inconsistent with that perspective having a frame (Section 6).

L1 is not a diagonal argument, and the diagonal is where the formal versions of self-reference live. Lawvere (1969) showed that if an object A represents, point-surjectively, all maps from A to Y, then every endomap of Y has a fixed point; contrapositively, where Y has a fixed-point-free endomap, such as negation on truth values, no A represents its own predicates completely. Yanofsky (2003) shows that Cantor, Russell, the liar, Gödel, Tarski and Turing are instances. Each derives a contradiction from the assumption of complete self-representation together with an operation that has no fixed point. L1 assumes no such operation. Supposing its negation yields no diagonal contradiction, only a conflict with the frame/content relation itself: an item still being produced would be a finished one. The limit sharpens into a diagonal contradiction when D can act against its own content, for instance read a prediction of its next output and produce a different one. That is the self-prediction argument of Popper (1950) and MacKay (1960), and it has Lawvere's form. It also needs the extra capacity, which makes it a conditional result about describers that can negate their own output, where L1 holds for any describer at all.

**L2. No part can certify its own description of the whole complete.**

Let $\Delta$ be D's description of W. $\Delta$ may be complete. For a whole generated by a short rule from a short initial condition, a part can hold the rule and the condition, and that is a complete description in the sense of Section 2. The limit is on the certification. To certify $\Delta$ complete, D must establish that W contains nothing $\Delta$ leaves unfixed, and whatever D uses in the check is admitted input (Section 2), so D has access to W only through $\Delta$ itself and its admitted input. A check against $\Delta$ alone is circular. A check against the admitted input shows at most that $\Delta$ fixes everything the input reports. The input may itself contain the claim that it reports everything there is, as when D admits that W is generated by a rule from an initial condition and nothing else; but that is the one admitted item certification may not rest on (Section 2), since checking it needs access to the complement of the input, and certifying it is the original task over again. The second route therefore either begs the question or hands it on. The remaining route is to hold W itself beside $\Delta$ and compare. That is holding W as content, including D's own current generation, which L1 excludes; and even setting L1 aside, what D would then hold is a description larger than $\Delta$, for which the question returns. A check for completeness made from inside either exceeds the description or rests on the one admitted claim that cannot ground it, and neither is certification.

L2 is a specialized form of Albert's (1968) trilemma: the attempt at a final justification ends in regress, circularity or a stop taken on authority. Applied to the single claim "my description is complete", the regress is holding the remainder, the circularity is proving from the input that the input is all there is, and the stop is declaring it so. The trilemma's third horn is disputed: foundationalists deny that every stop is dogmatic, infinitists accept the regress (Klein 1999), coherentists deny that the circle is vicious. Here the horn is closed by the definition of certification in Section 2, which does not count a stop as a certification, and L2 is to that extent a consequence of what "certify" means for a part. The nearest formal analogue is Gödel's second theorem: a system of the kind it governs cannot certify from inside a global property of itself on which the standing of all its claims depends. L2 does not need arithmetic, however, and holds for a system too weak to state anything about itself, because whatever the input contains, the claim that it is all there is comes admitted and not certified.

Both hold in a world that is small, compressible and reducible, for a describer that can store the whole's rule and run it ahead of the whole. That is the ground for calling them unconditional: they depend on nothing about W beyond D's being a proper part of it with a frame, not on its compressibility, its computation, its arithmetic strength or its finiteness.

## 4. Three limits with conditions

The better-known limits each carry a condition on the world or on the system.

**Capacity.** Two assumptions enter here that L1 and L2 do not use: that W is finite, so that a proper part has fewer degrees of freedom than the whole, and that W's state is incompressible. Kolmogorov (1968) and Chaitin (1966) defined the complexity of a finite string as the length of the shortest program that produces it, and the definition comes with a counting fact: there are 2^*n* binary strings of length *n* and fewer than 2^(*n*−*c*) programs shorter than *n* − *c* bits, so fewer than a fraction 2^−*c* of the strings have complexity below *n* − *c*, and most strings cannot be compressed by more than a few bits. If the state of W is incompressible, or compressible only to a length above D's capacity, D holds no complete description of that state. Whether a given W is incompressible is a fact about W, and where W compresses to fit D, capacity sets no limit. What D then holds is W's generator, the rule plus the initial condition, and whether holding the generator gives D the state is the next limit's question.

**Irreducibility.** Wolfram (2002) calls a computation irreducible when the only way to learn its state after *t* steps is to run *t* steps. A closed form, in this sense, is a shortcut whose cost grows more slowly than the simulation it replaces; every finite deterministic system is eventually periodic and has a lookup table, which is not one. Elementary cellular automata show the distinction. Rule 90 is additive: any cell at any time is given by a binomial coefficient modulo 2, so a part holding the formula computes it without running the intervening steps. Rule 30 has no known closed form, has served as a pseudorandom generator since Wolfram (1986) proposed it as one, and its irreducibility is a conjecture. Rule 110 is universal (Cook 2004), and a universal system admits no general shortcut to its eventual behaviour, because Turing (1936) showed that no procedure decides, for every machine and input, whether the computation finishes. Turing rules out a general procedure for the outcome, not a rendering of each step that beats the original by a constant factor, and he does not reach a finite D at all, whose eventual behaviour is decidable in principle; for a finite W the question shifts from impossibility to size, a lookup table larger than the system, which returns the matter to capacity. What holds for a finite D is weaker: wherever a describer in W actually performs irreducible computation, W's trajectory has no closed form, since every part's state is a function of the whole's and would inherit one, so a D that holds W's exact rule obtains W's future only by running the rule; and if D, as a proper part, has fewer computational resources than W, a further premise, it runs behind. The claim concerns exact prediction of the microstate; coarse-grained descriptions of many automata, Rule 110 among them, are predictable (Israeli and Goldenfeld 2004, 2006). The condition is on W's history, and whether any describer in W does irreducible work is, for Rule 30 and for most actual systems, a conjecture. Culik and Yu (1988) proved that sorting an arbitrary cellular automaton into a formalization of Wolfram's behavioural classes is undecidable, so whether the irreducibility limit applies to a given rule has no general decision procedure.

**Incompleteness.** Take a formal system in the sense Gödel (1931) studied: a fixed alphabet; axioms and rules such that whether a string is a proof is mechanically checkable; and enough expressive power to represent the recursive functions, for which Robinson's arithmetic Q suffices. If such a system is consistent, there is, in Rosser's (1936) form, a sentence of its language it neither proves nor refutes; and if it also proves enough about its own proof relation, it does not prove the natural arithmetical statement of its own consistency, though a stronger system can prove it for it: one system can see about another what the other cannot see about itself. Drop any condition and the theorem is silent. An inconsistent system proves everything, its own consistency included. A system whose axioms are all the true arithmetical sentences is complete but not mechanically axiomatized. Arithmetic with addition alone (Presburger 1929) and arithmetic with multiplication alone, Skolem arithmetic (Mostowski 1952), are each complete and decidable; the theorem needs both operations together.

**The common form.** Gödel's mechanism shows what the five limits share: he coded the system's syntax into numbers so that the system's proof relation is represented inside the system, exactly. The generator appears in what it generates, as content. What fails is complete and consistent appearance, and that is the sharp form of the principle: the generator can appear in what it generates, but not completely, if what it generates is to remain consistent. Priest's inclosure schema (1994, 1995) is the same structure read philosophically: a totality $\Omega$ and an operation $\delta$ that takes any part of $\Omega$ to something in $\Omega$ outside that part, so that $\delta(\Omega)$ is both in and out. The two unconditional limits fit the principle in its unsharpened form. L1: the generation appears as content, even completely, but only as a finished state; the generating does not appear in its own product. L2: a description can agree with everything admitted; what cannot appear in it is the certificate that nothing lies outside it. Capacity and irreducibility are the same failure located in the world's state and the world's computation: the whole fails to fit inside the part that would describe it, by size or by time.

The taxonomy has two axes. The first is conditionality, what must be assumed about W or the system beyond proper parthood with a frame: nothing for L1 and L2; a kinematic premise for Breuer's (1995) result, that W's admissible states include, for each state of D, distinct states agreeing on D and differing outside it; finiteness and incompressibility for capacity; irreducible work for irreducibility; arithmetic strength for incompleteness; the capacity to negate one's own output for the self-prediction results of Popper (1950), MacKay (1960) and Wolpert (2008). The second is mechanism: the frame/content relation and the definition of certification for L1 and L2; non-injectivity of restriction for Breuer; counting for capacity; diagonal for Gödel, Turing and the self-prediction results. The axes do not coincide: the conditional results come with three mechanisms, and Breuer's premise holds for infinite W, where a proper part can have as many states as the whole and counting fails, so it is not finiteness under another name.

## 5. Falsity without truth

"Falsity" below means inconsistency between a system's claims and the input it admits; it is falsity of the claims on the condition that the input is true, and nothing more. "Truth as match" means a verdict of correspondence certified from a position outside all models, and its unavailability is epistemic: what is at issue is certification, not the concept of truth.

Any perspective can establish two kinds of thing about another's model: that it fits input, and that it contradicts input. The first does not add up to truth. The second needs no outside.

**Why truth as match is unavailable.** A claim of the form "this model matches the world" would be established by setting the model beside the world and comparing them from a position that is not itself a model, and no perspective occupies such a position: whatever D uses in the comparison is admitted input (Section 2), held as content within D's frame, so the comparison sets the model beside what D holds of the world; and whether what D holds is all there is to hold is the totality claim L2 leaves admitted and uncertified (Section 3). Positive verdicts as such remain available, in a local sense: "this model agrees with the input I admit on the consequences I have drawn and checked" is a finite verdict, checkable and no harder to check than its negation. Fit in this sense is weaker than consistency with the input, the global verdict that the next paragraphs show has no certificate from inside for systems Gödel's second theorem governs; and what is unavailable in either case is the step from fit to match.

**Why falsity needs no outside.** A reference system can be shown inconsistent with input it itself admits; both terms of the relation are inside. Establishing the verdict, relative to that input, takes one exhibited contradiction: a consequence the system draws that the input it accepts denies. Once exhibited, anyone with access to the system's claims and its input can check it, whatever their own model of the world. The check compares a system only with itself and its own input, and so needs no agreement about what the non-logical symbols refer to, given agreement on the logical vocabulary and on which claims are the system's own. For non-formal perspectives the last item is a matter of interpretation, and an interpreter who minimizes the contradictions she attributes (Davidson 1973) will contest it.

For formal systems the asymmetry is sharp. For an effectively axiomatized first-order theory, inconsistency is semi-decidable: if the theory is inconsistent, a finite derivation of a contradiction exists, can be found by enumeration, and can be checked step by step; if the theory is consistent, no finite search returns that answer. Inconsistency is therefore detectable, and permanent once exhibited, which is not the same as decidable. For propositional systems, or any system with a decidable consequence relation, the search terminates either way; in general it does not. Consistency, the positive verdict, has no certificate from inside for systems of the kind Gödel's second theorem governs; the negative verdict comes with a certificate any checker can verify.

**Inheritance.** The principle that every property determines a set, applied without restriction, yields Russell's contradiction: the set of all sets that are not members of themselves is a member of itself if and only if it is not. Every classical set theory built since keeps the input that generated the contradiction, the ordinary rules of logic and the notion of membership, and restricts the principle to avoid it; the paraconsistent set theories keep the principle and drop the logic instead (Weber 2010), which is the other way of dropping a member of an inconsistent set. The refutation of unrestricted comprehension has passed into every classical theory, none of which proves its own consistency from inside: mathematics knows, with security relative to classical logic and the membership notion every later theory keeps, one thing about sets that is false, and holds its positive theory of sets on trust.

Arithmetic gives a plainer case. In 1644 Mersenne listed 2^67 − 1 among the primes (Mersenne 1644). Lucas showed it composite in 1876 by his primality test (Lucas 1878), without exhibiting a factor; in 1903 Cole produced the factors, 193,707,721 × 761,838,257,287 (Cole 1903). The refutation required no agreement about what numbers are and no standpoint outside arithmetic, only that a reader be able to multiply. Every perspective that admits the rules of multiplication receives the same verdict, and every later system that keeps them keeps it.

A successor framework that keeps the input inherits the verdict that the predecessor contradicted it, and no guarantee of its own correctness; a schematic history shows the two halves. A community holds the earth flat, on the evidence that the ground looks flat and the horizon looks like an edge. Ships disappear hull first; the earth's shadow on the moon is round at every angle; travellers going south watch new stars rise. None of these needs geometry beyond what the flat-earth claim already used, together with two auxiliaries the community already admits, that light travels in straight lines and that the shadow on the moon is the earth's; the community's own framework, applied to its own new observations, contradicts the claim. The replacement, "roughly a ball", is the best present fit, open to a successor nobody yet suspects. Any successor has to reproduce the hull-down ships and the round shadow or drop an auxiliary, visibly; every framework that keeps the observations and the auxiliaries inherits the verdict that the earth is not flat, and none is obliged to inherit the verdict that it is a ball.

**Duhem–Quine.** Quine (1951) and Lakatos (1970) object to any conclusive falsification: a hypothesis is tested only together with auxiliary assumptions, so a failed prediction can be absorbed by adjusting an auxiliary, and historically refutations are reversed. The account accepts this about localization. What the exhibited contradiction establishes is that the set consisting of the system's claims and its admitted input is inconsistent; which member of the set is false is not settled by the contradiction. Inheritance is monotonicity: a superset of an inconsistent set is inconsistent, so every successor that keeps the whole set keeps the contradiction, and a consistent successor has dropped a member. And the drop is visible: a system can refuse an observation, reinterpret an instrument, or add an auxiliary that turns the contradiction into a fit, and the refusing system has then changed its input, which every perspective keeping the input can see. Which drops are acceptable is Lakatos's (1970) question, and his distinction between progressive and degenerating problem-shifts is a theory of it. Every drop is visible to those who keep the member.

Two senses of "absolute" have to be kept apart. As a relation, falsity is not a consensus property: the contradiction between a flat earth and a round shadow was available to anyone who admitted the observation before anyone did. Epistemically, verdicts of falsity are revisable, because observations can be wrong and frameworks misapplied; one exhibited contradiction establishes the verdict relative to the input, and what accumulates afterwards is security against the input being withdrawn. Keeping absolute falsity in the relational sense while giving up absolute truth is consistent, because a falsity verdict is never read off an absolute truth.

**Popper.** The logical half of this is Popper's. He derived the asymmetry from the form of universal statements, no finite set of observations verifies a law while one accepted counter-instance contradicts it, and made falsifiability a methodological rule (Popper 1959). Miller (1994, ch. 3) takes the strongest line: nothing is justified, falsity verdicts included, and criticism proceeds without justification. The verdict needs nothing beyond the derivation, as Miller holds; security against withdrawal of input is a separate matter, which Miller would not call justification either. Beyond Popper, the asymmetry is located here (what is unavailable from inside is truth as match, not positive verdicts of fit), stated for any reference system, an organism's model of its surroundings as much as a scientific theory (Popper 1972 extended it to organisms' expectations), and extended by inheritance: the refutation is a fact about the predecessor and the input that stays one for every successor keeping the input. Verisimilitude is not reintroduced, which Tichý (1974) and Miller (1974) showed Popper's own measure could not supply: it makes no claim about distance from truth, only about inconsistency with kept input.

Popper also conceded that the basic statements that do the refuting are accepted by decision, and compared the empirical basis of science to piles driven into a swamp (Popper 1959). "Admitted input" has the same feature; what differs is that the cost of refusing an input is visible from every perspective that keeps it.

**The scope condition.** Transfer has one condition, on logic rather than on the world: for both perspectives, an exhibited contradiction between a system's claims and its admitted input must count as a failure of the claims. Classical logic has that rule, since from a contradiction anything follows. Paraconsistent logics block explosion. Most of them still treat a contradiction as a defect to be removed (Carnielli and Coniglio 2016), and a perspective reasoning in such a logic is reached by the refutation as a classical one is; what differs is how much of the system the contradiction brings down. The exemption belongs to dialetheism (Priest 1995, 2006), the view that some contradictions are true. A dialetheist who accepts the particular contradiction exhibited as true is not refuted by it, and is reached by every refutation whose contradiction she does not accept.

## 6. The limits as refutations

The core limits are claimed for every perspective, and the conditional limits for every perspective that meets their conditions. On the account of Section 5, a positive claim passes between perspectives only as a fit to admitted input; if the limit claims were positive truths about all perspectives, held from none, they would be what Section 5 says no one holds, and the difficulty of Section 1 would stand.

They have a different form. Each is an impossibility claim, and support for a claim of that form comes from showing that its negation fails; here the negation fails from inside every perspective that might assert it, against that perspective's positional input.

A perspective that claims to hold its own current generation as content claims that an item it now distinguishes is, or has as a part, the distinguishing now under way; what a frame distinguishes at a time is finished at that time (Section 2), so the claim contradicts its admission that it has a frame (L1). The claim so refuted is rarely asserted, and the refutation's role is structural: it closes the last route of L2 and shows what having a frame commits every claimant to. A perspective that claims to have certified its description complete claims either to have held the remainder, which is more than its description, or to have certified from its own input that there is none, which is the one admitted item certification cannot rest on (L2). A perspective that claims the whole's state, where the whole is finite and its state incompressible, claims more degrees of freedom than a proper part has, and the claim fails by counting. A perspective that claims the whole's future from the whole's generator, wherever the whole does irreducible work, claims to obtain that future ahead of the whole, which together with the condition contradicts its being a proper part with fewer resources. A formal system of the kind Section 4 describes that proves its own consistency is thereby inconsistent, which is Gödel's second theorem read as a refutation of the claim to have certified consistency from inside. In the first two cases the contradiction lies between the claim and input the claimant cannot drop without ceasing to be the kind of thing that makes claims: being embedded and having a frame. In the last three it lies between the claim and that input together with the condition the limit states. In the first two, positional input is read through Section 2: the frame/content relation, with its clause that held content is finished at the time it is held, and the notion of certification, which does not count a stop as a certificate. A claimant that rejects either notion is not contradicted by the corresponding limit; the refutations reach those that share the notions, which is weaker than reaching every claimant.

The negation of each limit, together with the positional input of whoever asserts it, is inconsistent by the arguments of Section 3. There are two tiers, and Mackie's (1964) analysis supplies the terms. Where the claimant admits its positional input as a premise, the first sense of "admitted" in Section 2, the inconsistency is logical: content and admitted position together entail a contradiction, and the negation of the limit is false relative to the claimant's input. Where the claimant only presupposes its position in claiming and will not admit it, the inconsistency is operational in Mackie's sense: the assertion presupposes what its content denies, the claim is unassertable from where it is made, and nothing follows about its truth. The limits reach the first claimant as falsity and the second as unassertability, and a claimant passes from the second tier to the first by stating its presuppositions; a claimant that will not state them stays in the second tier (Section 8). The limit claims are not asserted as positive truths from nowhere, so the turning-around that Burnyeat (1976) reconstructs for Protagoras does not turn on them: there is no "true for all perspectives" in them to cash out. On Burnyeat's reconstruction the *peritrope* turns on the relativist's commitment to truth simpliciter; the limit claims carry no such commitment, and the notion they rely on is inconsistency relative to kept input, stable in the relational sense of Section 5.

Who is reached. Any perspective that distinguishes anything has a frame, and the refutation reaches it. The view from outside everything, which would hold every structure and draw no distinction, is not a perspective (Section 2), and no claim is made about it. The limits carry a refutation's resistance, not immunity: the input they rest on, being embedded and having a frame, is what a perspective would have to withdraw to escape them, and withdrawing it leaves nothing that distinguishes. The dialetheist who accepts the exhibited contradiction as true is the one exemption (Section 8).

The thesis "there is no outside" splits accordingly. As a refutation it travels: any perspective that claims to be outside while admitting it is part of the world contradicts itself, and the contradiction is checkable by anyone with access to the claim and the admission. As a positive picture of how things are, a world of embedded perspectives with nothing beyond them, it is one more claim held from inside, exposed to every competitor that fits the input better. The thesis that two limits hold for every embedded describer splits the same way. As a positive thesis it is held from inside and fits the input the author admits; as a refutation it is the schema of Sections 3 and 6, which any other perspective can run on itself with its own positional input. That refutations travel is likewise a thesis held here as a fit; what travels is each particular refutation.

## 7. Neighbours

**Priest.** The inclosure schema is the closest structural relative of the common form in Section 4, and Priest's conclusion is the opposite of Section 6. Where a totality and its diagonaliser produce a contradiction at the limit, Priest (1995) reads the contradiction as true: the limit is transcended and closed at once. Here the same contradiction is read as the mark of a failed claim, the claim to completeness, and classical consistency is kept. The scope condition of Section 5 states where the present account stops, and Priest's position is on the far side of it. The disagreement is about logic and is located here: Priest's true contradictions are the inclosure contradictions, which are the contradictions the limit claims exhibit.

**Lawvere, Yanofsky, Szangolies.** Szangolies (2018) applies Lawvere's theorem to observers embedded in the systems they measure and derives epistemic horizons from it; the sharp version of the common form in Section 4 is his, Lawvere's (1969) and Yanofsky's (2003). New here are the separation of L1 and L2, which do not have diagonal form, from the limits that do, and the two axes of Section 4: the diagonal results need an operation without a fixed point, which the system or describer must supply; L1, L2 and Breuer's result use none.

**Popper 1950, MacKay, Breuer, Wolpert.** The line from Popper's (1950) predictor that cannot predict its own future states, through MacKay's (1960) agent that cannot be bound to accept a complete prediction of its own choice, to Breuer's (1995) observer that cannot distinguish all present states of a system containing it and Wolpert's (2008) inference devices, consists of representational neighbours of L1: each limits what a describer can represent of itself, by a mechanism L1 does not use, and under its condition reaches further than L1 does. Breuer's argument is independent of the dynamics and uses no counting: it assumes that D is a proper subsystem in the kinematic sense, so that distinct states of W agree on D, and concludes that no state of D distinguishes all states of W. Capacity cannot borrow the premise, because a description may fix the complement's state by procedures D runs from a content shorter than the state, which is why capacity needs incompressibility and Breuer's argument, which sets the dynamics aside, does not. L1 reaches a related conclusion from the act in progress, with no premise about states. Wolpert's results concern an inference device's capacity to answer arbitrary questions about the universe containing it, independent of the physical laws; the central theorem is diagonal, the device is asked about a function that negates its own answer, so on the first axis of Section 4 it is conditional on the device's being askable such a question, though on no law. The two axes are what separate results of these kinds from one another and from the irreducibility and arithmetic results they are usually listed beside (Svozil 1993; Wolpert 2008; Gruber 2026a, §6.4).

**Nagel, Putnam, Moore, Grim.** Nagel (1986) states the problem in its canonical form: objectivity is reached by stepping back from a view and including it in a more detached one, and the process cannot be completed. Nagel offers no proof of a limit. L1 is related and narrower: it says why the detaching act is never among what it detaches, and nothing about whether the view, as a finished state, can be included. Putnam (1981) shares the premise that there is no God's-eye view and draws a different conclusion, redefining truth as idealized rational acceptability. Section 5 does not redefine truth. Putnam denies the coherence of the standpoint; the present account denies access to it, leaves its coherence open, and keeps falsity relative to admitted input without idealization. Moore (1997) holds that absolute representations, representations not perspectival in content, are possible. The present account does not deny it, since $\Delta$ may be complete (Section 3); it denies that a part can certify one as such. The disagreement is over the limits: Moore holds they are known and not sayable; here they are said, as refutations, and their negations are logically inconsistent for a claimant that admits its position (Section 6). Grim (1991) shows by Cantorian argument that there is no set of all truths and no omniscient knower, which anticipates L2 at the level of totalities; L2 is a limit on an act of certification by a part and needs no totality.

**Bartley and performative contradiction.** Bartley's (1962) pancritical rationalism holds every position, rationalism included, open to criticism, claims no positive justification for any, and survives its own application for that reason. The refutation form of Section 6 follows that precedent, with one difference: Bartley's criticisability is a stance a position holds open, while the refutations in Section 6 are forced by input the claimant cannot drop. Transcendental pragmatics (Apel 1975) uses the same mechanism, that the denial of a presupposition of argument commits a performative contradiction, to secure positive ultimate foundations. Albert's trilemma and Apel's retorsion are the two poles of the Letztbegründung debate: Albert (1975) replied that the retorsion yields no positive foundation, and Kuhlmann (1985) defended Apel's side against fallibilism. The limits as refutations use Apel's mechanism and accept Albert's verdict on its yield; why the negative result travels is the subject of Section 5.

## 8. Objections

**Admitted input is chosen, so the refutations are relative.** They are, and two things keep this from relativism. Withdrawal is visible: every perspective that keeps the input sees that the withdrawing system has dropped it (Section 5). And positional input cannot be withdrawn while the system goes on making claims about the whole: a describer can stop describing, but it cannot describe and deny that it is a part with a frame doing the describing. The limits rest on positional input only, read through the notions of frame and certification in Section 2, and so reach every claimant that admits those notions and the steps of Section 3, where the refutation of a hypothesis reaches only those who keep its observations. A claimant that rejects the notions, for instance by holding that content can be unfinished when held, or by counting a declared stop as a certificate, owes an account of what its frame is or what its certificate establishes; it is not contradicted by L1 or L2.

**A perspective might deny being embedded.** Such a perspective claims to describe the whole from no part of it, where the whole is everything it admits as existing (Section 2). Either it draws distinctions or it does not. If it does, it has a frame, and the frame is somewhere among what it admits; the denial is made from a position it denies having, which is self-refutation of Mackie's operational kind, the second tier of Section 6, and yields an unassertable claim rather than a false one. If it does not, it describes nothing, and there is no claim to refute.

**A perspective might be the whole.** A frame shows that a describer is somewhere among what it admits, which makes it a part of W, not yet a proper part. A solipsist, or an absolute idealist, may admit D = W: nothing exists beyond the describer. L1 does not need proper parthood; it concerns the act in progress and holds for any describer with a frame, so it reaches this claimant too. L2's argument survives the case. For D = W, certifying by comparison means holding D itself, its current generation included, beside $\Delta$, which L1 excludes; and the claim that the input reports everything there is remains an admitted totality claim, which cannot ground certification. Capacity and irreducibility, which compare a part with a larger whole, do not apply.

**Dialetheists and paraconsistent reasoners escape.** Paraconsistent reasoners do not. A logic that blocks explosion still lets its user treat a contradiction as a defect, and most do (Section 5), so such a perspective has a defect to remove like any other. A dialetheist who accepts the exhibited contradiction as true is not refuted by it. Nothing follows for refutations whose contradictions she does not accept, and for Priest those are almost all of them: he keeps classical reasoning for consistent domains (Priest 2006) and would accept Cole's refutation of Mersenne. The exemption falls where the inclosure contradictions fall, on the limit claims themselves, and the account has no argument against him there.

**This is Popper.** The logical asymmetry is Popper's (Section 5), and his question was method; his extension of the asymmetry to organisms (Popper 1972) stayed within it. Bartley (1962) and Miller (1994) turn critical rationalism on itself, and Bartley holds his own position open to criticism; to our knowledge neither applies the asymmetry to the limitative theses, locates what is lost, truth as match, against what survives, positive verdicts of fit, or states what holism leaves standing once the Duhem–Quine objection is conceded.

**L1 is a verbal point about "current".** The time index is what the argument runs on. Without it, L1 would say that no system holds a complete description of its own production, and Kleene's recursion theorem refutes that. With it, L1 says that the act doing the holding at *t* is not among the completed items held at *t*: not that a system cannot model itself, nor that the model cannot be complete once finished, but that the model cannot be, or have as a part, the act producing it. Gödel's arithmetization shows how much self-representation is possible, L1 what it stops short of, and the common form of Section 4 states both halves.

## 9. Conclusion

Two limits hold for any describer that is a proper part of what it describes, with no assumption about the whole beyond that; three more hold under conditions on the world's compressibility, its computation and a formal system's strength. All five say that the generator can appear in what it generates, but not completely.

As positive truths about every perspective, the limits would be held from the outside they deny. They are held as refutations: the negation of each contradicts positional input, read through the notions of frame and certification, and a contradiction between a system and its admitted input is the one thing that passes between perspectives without a shared ontology or a view from nowhere: it is detectable from inside, permanent as a fact about the set once exhibited, and inherited by every successor that keeps the input. That is why falsity survives the loss of truth as match and why holism leaves it standing: what is refuted is a set kept entire, and dropping a member is visible to everyone who keeps it.

Mathematics and logic are the instruments embedded describers have for the two things that do travel, a derivation anyone who accepts the rules can check and a contradiction anyone who follows the steps can find. Cole's factorization of 2^67 − 1 refutes, for every reader who can multiply, the claim that the number is prime, and says nothing about what a number is.
