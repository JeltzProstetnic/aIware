### 8.9 Prediction 8: Disrupting the Self-Model Lowers Pain Unpleasantness More Than Pain Intensity

**Statement.** When the explicit self-model is disrupted, pain unpleasantness falls more than pain intensity, and the size of that gap scales with measured self-model disruption, not with drug dose or with expectancy.

**Mechanism.** *Derivation: Principle 3 → qualia as the way the explicit self-model registers its own states (Section 3.4.2) and the simulation as the place where hedonic valence is registered (Section 4.2.1) → prediction. The intensity of a pain is content generated from the nociceptive implicit models. Its unpleasantness is that content registered by the explicit self-model as bad for the self it models. Disrupting the explicit self-model therefore removes the bearer the pain is bad for faster than it removes the sensation.* The claim is relative. A self-model disruption can also change the sensory dimension (Röder et al., 2007; Riegner et al., 2023), so the theory predicts the difference between the two drops, not an intensity that stays fixed.

**Testability.** Randomized, double-blind, within-subject crossover in healthy volunteers: placebo, a dose of psilocybin below the range that produces ego dissolution and a dose within it, with an active placebo matched for expectancy. Tonic heat or cold-pressor pain at calibrated intensity is delivered at peak effect, with separate ratings of intensity and unpleasantness after each stimulus (Rainville et al., 1999). Self-model disruption is measured at the same time point with the Ego-Dissolution Inventory (Nour et al., 2016), the 5D-ASC (Studerus et al., 2010) and the depersonalization scale of the CADSS; expectancy is measured before dosing. The dependent variable is the within-subject gap, the drop in unpleasantness minus the drop in intensity, which is the measure Lee et al. (2013) used for THC. It is regressed on ego dissolution with plasma level and expectancy as covariates. A second design uses no drug. Contact athletes already find pain less bothersome within months while their intensity ratings change only after years (Thornton et al., 2017, 2019), and a meta-analysis puts the athletes' unpleasantness effect above their intensity effect, though on five studies and not significantly (Thornton et al., 2024). That pattern confounds exposure with appraisal. Four groups matched on training hours separate them: a contact martial art with formal mental training (meditation before and after practice), a contact art without it, a non-contact practice with the same mental training, and non-athletes. At calibrated equal intensity, the theory predicts the largest gap in the first group, tracking its depersonalization and self-referential processing scores, while threshold and intensity differences follow exposure, not mental training. An existing dataset comes close: the Maastricht psilocybin trial in fibromyalgia (NCT06368492) records pain tolerance, ego dissolution, 5D-ASC, CADSS and expectancy, but registers only one pain rating.

**Falsification.** If affect and intensity fall together at doses that dissolve the self-model, or the gap is fully explained by dose or expectancy with ego dissolution adding nothing once both are covaried, or ego dissolution predicts the intensity drop rather than the gap, the account of valence as registered by the explicit self-model is wrong.

**Distinguishing power.** The dissociation of the two pain dimensions is established and is not the theory's (Rainville et al., 1997; Perlman et al., 2010; Lee et al., 2013), and Gerrans (2020, 2024) already argues, in active-inference terms, that pain suffering is anchored by a self-model. The coarse prediction is therefore shared. A precision account without a self-model term predicts that matched expectancy produces a matched gap; the theory predicts that self-model disruption explains the gap after expectancy is covaried out, and that the gap appears with ego dissolution rather than with dose. The closest existing test points the other way for one half: in mindfulness, thalamus–precuneus decoupling tracked the drop in intensity, not in unpleasantness (Riegner et al., 2023). Uniqueness: shared in part.
