# Why the Hard Problem Looks Unbridgeable from Inside: Virtual Qualia and Self-Referential Closure

**Matthias Gruber**\
Independent researcher · ORCID 0009-0005-9697-1665 · research@matthiasgruber.com

2026-10-30 · Preprint, version 3

## Abstract

This paper defends one part of the Four-Model Theory of consciousness (Gruber, 2015; 2026): the claim that phenomenal properties belong to a second, computational level that a recurrent substrate hosts, and that they arise where the modelling on that level closes on itself, with the self-model and the world-model each entering the other's update rule. Two consequences are argued. First, the standard formulation of the Hard Problem asks why substrate processing feels like something, and on this account substrate processing does not; phenomenal character is a property of the running self-simulation, as a sum is a property of a running spreadsheet and not of its transistors. Second, closure produces an asymmetry between what an outside description captures (the structure of the self-model) and what it does not (the perspective the self-model constitutes), and the explicit self-model is structurally opaque to the substrate that generates it. Together these explain why the gap looks unbridgeable from inside. The identification of phenomenality with closure is asserted and defended by inference to the best explanation, not derived. The paper engages strong illusionism, the meta-problem's debunking worry, zombie conceivability and the master argument against phenomenal concepts, and states two empirical consequences with their refutation conditions.

**Keywords:** hard problem, meta-problem, self-model, self-referential closure, illusionism, type-B physicalism, phenomenal concepts

## 1. Introduction

Chalmers (1995) separated the functions of consciousness, which mechanistic explanation can in principle reach, from the fact that there is something it is like to undergo them (Nagel, 1974). Three broad responses dominate. Realists who take the problem at face value add something to physics, as property dualism and Russellian views do (Chalmers, 1996; 2003). Illusionists deny that phenomenal properties exist and redirect the explanatory effort to the judgements that they do (Dennett, 1991; Frankish, 2016). Type-B physicalists hold that phenomenal facts are physical facts, and that the gap is a feature of how phenomenal concepts present their referents rather than a gap in nature (Loar, 1990; Papineau, 2002; Balog, 2012).

The position defended here is a type-B physicalism with two specific additions. The first is an ontology: phenomenal properties are real and physical, and they belong to a computational level that a recurrent substrate generates, not to the substrate's own vocabulary. The second is a mechanism: the computational level is phenomenal where its modelling is self-referentially closed, and the same closure makes the system's own substrate opaque to it. The first addition says where to look for phenomenal properties. The second says why, looking from inside, the search seems doomed.

Both additions come from a larger framework, the Four-Model Theory (FMT), first formulated in German by Gruber (2015) and stated in full in a preprint (Gruber, 2026). That framework also addresses binding, altered states, clinical dissociations and animal consciousness. None of that is defended here. This paper isolates the part that bears on the Hard Problem and the meta-problem (Chalmers, 2018), because that part can be assessed on its own.

FMT identifies phenomenality with self-referential closure. The identity is asserted, not derived. What the theory offers in its favour is an argument that closure is the kind of structure for which the Hard Problem's framing assumptions fail, an explanation of why the problem nonetheless looks unbridgeable from inside, and empirical consequences that could count against the architecture. Every theory of consciousness carries a bridging commitment of this kind; the question is which commitment explains more for less.

The account belongs to the self-modelling tradition, which holds that consciousness is constituted by, and not merely correlated with, a system's model of itself. Metzinger (2003) set out a self-model theory of subjectivity within this tradition. Damasio (1999) grounded consciousness in progressively elaborated self-representations; Hofstadter (2007) identified the self with a self-referential loop of symbols; the self-representational approaches collected by Kriegel and Williford (2006) make a state's representation of itself part of what makes it conscious; Graziano (2013) treats subjective awareness as the brain's simplified model of its own attention. FMT's specific contributions are the minimal architecture (two model classes, implicit and explicit, each in a world-scoped and a self-scoped form), the level ontology that places phenomenal properties at the computational level, and the claim that one structural feature, closure, both constitutes phenomenality and hides its own basis.

Section 2 sets out the two model classes and the real/virtual split. Section 3 states the closure condition. Section 4 says what follows for the Hard Problem and Section 5 for the meta-problem. Section 6 takes the strongest objections. Section 7 gives two empirical consequences with their refutation conditions, and a contrast with recurrent processing theory.

## 2. Two model classes and the real/virtual split

### 2.1 Models and model kinds

A model, in the sense used here, is a state or structure *M* of a system related to a target domain *T* by a mapping that preserves the relations the system acts on, such that operating on *M* yields states that correspond to consequences in *T*, with an error the system can register (Craik, 1943; Conant and Ashby, 1970). A structure the system cannot evaluate is a data structure, not a model.

FMT sorts models along two dimensions (Gruber, 2026, §3.2). The first is mode. Some models are stored in the substrate's connectivity and evaluated by substrate processing: synaptic weights, dendritic morphology and their functional equivalents. Others are generated as running activity from that stored knowledge and current input. The first are called implicit, the second explicit. The second dimension is scope: a model may target the world, of which the system is a part, or the system itself. Scope is a nesting rather than a contrast, since the self is a proper part of the world. The result is two model classes, each in a world-scoped and a self-scoped form, and so four model kinds: an implicit world model and an implicit self model, an explicit world model and an explicit self model.

These are kinds, not modules. A real nervous system runs an effectively uncountable number of overlapping models, many of which blend world and self (a reaching movement encodes both object position and arm configuration). The four are extremal points in a continuous space of modelling, and the claim is that any system capable of consciousness must model both world and self, both as stored structure and as running simulation. Four is the floor, not the count. Nor are the explicit models filled by transferring data out of the implicit ones. They are generated from the substrate in the way a running program is made of the machine's activity.

### 2.2 The level distinction

Every computing system distinguishes a physical substrate from the computation running on it. A spreadsheet cell contains the sum of column B. No transistor contains a sum, and no collection of transistors contains one either when described in transistor vocabulary: voltages, logic levels, charge states. The full collection implements the spreadsheet, and at the computational level the sum is real and causally efficacious. But the property of being a sum is not recoverable by aggregating transistor-level descriptions, however exhaustive. The point concerns levels of description, not parts and wholes.

Standard computational functionalism grants this and adds that the computational level is nothing more than the substrate's activity under a functional description (Chalmers, 1996). FMT departs from that addition. It holds that some properties of a computation belong to it as a running process and are had only by instantiation. A complete description of the process, legible and accurate, is still a description. It could be used to instantiate the process elsewhere, but reading it is not undergoing it, in the way that reading a menu feeds nobody although menu and meal are both physical.

### 2.3 Real and virtual

Applied to the two model classes, the level distinction yields the real/virtual split (Gruber, 2026, §§3.3, 3.4.6). The implicit models are the real side: physical, structural, learned, without phenomenal character. There is nothing it is like to be a synaptic weight. The explicit models are the virtual side: generated, transient patterns of activity whose defining properties (phenomenal character in the closed loop, unity, perspective) exist at the computational level and are incoherent in substrate vocabulary.

"Virtual" here has one sense only. It does not mean unreal, and it does not mean transient, although the explicit models are transient. It means that a property is real and causally efficacious at the computational level but cannot be stated in the vocabulary of the level below. A quale is virtual in the way the spreadsheet's sum is virtual. Supervenience holds without exception: the computational level is wholly made of substrate activity, and a complete substrate description together with the dynamics entails every fact about it. Nothing non-physical is added.

What makes the split more than a relabelling is that it tracks an empirical dissociation. General anaesthetics suppress the dynamic cortical integration that sustains the explicit models while leaving synaptic connectivity and learned associations intact, and consciousness is abolished (Alkire, Hudetz and Tononi, 2008). On recovery, experience reconstitutes from whatever the stored models contain. If phenomenal character were a property of the structural substrate, it should survive the selective suppression of the running dynamics. It does not.

The split also has a structural motivation. The implicit models are the reference frame from which the explicit simulation is generated. The whole of that frame cannot simultaneously appear as content inside the simulation it generates, since modelling it would require a further frame, and so on. The implicit side can appear in experience only through its projections into the explicit models. Content that is merely stored is therefore never itself experienced. This is the root of the opacity discussed in Section 5.

## 3. Why levels are not enough: self-referential closure

### 3.1 The triviality objection

The level distinction raises an immediate objection. Every computation has properties incoherent at its substrate's level. A weather simulation has pressure fronts that no server rack has. If level-specific properties sufficed for phenomenality, every running program would have experience. Something more is required, and FMT's claim is that it is self-referential closure (Gruber, 2026, §3.4.3). The argument proceeds in three stages.

**Stage 1: simulation without self-reference.** The weather simulation models atmospheric dynamics. Its computational-level properties are real at that level. But the modelled domain and the modelling system are separate. An outside observer can describe everything the simulation computes without being part of what is computed. The simulation is about something other than itself, and its inside is fully visible from outside.

**Stage 2: monitored simulation.** Add a module that tracks memory use, convergence and accuracy. The system now represents aspects of its own operation. But the monitor describes the simulation without being described by it. The weather model contains no model of the monitoring. Self-monitoring of this kind is everywhere in engineering (thermostats, operating-system kernels, industrial controllers), and none of these systems has a perspective of its own. Partial self-reference does not close a loop.

**Stage 3: closure.** Now take a system whose world model contains a model of the system itself, and whose self model represents the system as the thing generating the world model. The self model and the world model each enter the other's update rule. In a recurrent substrate in which both are activity patterns over reciprocally connected units, this mutual entry is what one should expect rather than a special design. Within this system's own representational economy, every characterization of its processing is part of what it models. There is no internal vantage point from which the processing can be described without that description being processed in turn.

A self model must be a model of the system in proportion to what the system is. A position estimate alone does not qualify: a robot vacuum that localizes itself on a map represents one datum about itself and nothing of its states, its shape or its capabilities. FMT also places further conditions on sufficiency, taken up in Section 6.4. Closure is the condition that distinguishes the phenomenal from the merely computational, not the whole list.

### 3.2 The asymmetry closure creates

The weather simulation's computational properties are symmetrically accessible: whatever the simulation computes, an outside description can state. A closed system adds properties of a different sort, namely the system's representation of its own representing. From outside, an observer can describe the substrate, the dynamics and the formal structure of the self model. What the observer cannot do is occupy the perspective the self model constitutes, because that perspective exists only as the operation of the loop. An outside description of the self model is a description of the perspective, not the perspective, as a score is a description of a symphony (the analogy covers only the gap between description and instance; a symphony is not closed on itself).

FMT's further claim is that this asymmetry is constitutive and not merely epistemic. On the constitutive reading, being unoccupiable from outside is what it is for the states to have an inside. On the epistemic reading, the asymmetry is a limit on what observers can verify, and the states might still be "mere data". The theory takes the constitutive reading. That is its single bridging commitment.

### 3.3 Why "mere data" is not the neutral default

The commitment has a motivation. In a closed recurrent system every element is both state and processor, so what it computes admits no compact factorization into a program and the passive data it operates on. At the level of content, the meaning of its states is fixed by their role within the system's own self model. The content-preserving description of such a state is therefore cast in the system's indexical vocabulary. Where a neutral description reads "*c* = *a* + *b*", the content-preserving one reads "my five fruits are two apples and three pears; how will I carry them?" A description in neutral vocabulary preserves structure and drops exactly the indexical content.

The consequence is that "these states are mere data" is one reading among others, and it has no claim to be the default. The Hard Problem, as usually framed, takes that reading for granted and asks what must be added to it to yield experience. If the reading has no privileged status for closed systems, the question of what must be added is not the obvious question it appears to be.

What this argument does not establish is that a self-interpreting, first-person-formatted dynamics is thereby lived, as well as describable in this way. The step from the one to the other is the identity the theory asserts.

### 3.4 Temporal texture

A closed system's states do not terminate cleanly frame by frame. Because the system models its own modelling and that output feeds the next frame, information from a given frame recurs through the levels of self-modelling and spreads into later frames. FMT proposes that this recurrence (Gruber, 2026, §3.4.4) is what gives the inside perspective its temporal character: a present moment with a fading recent past, which the simulation constructs on its own clock. This is consistent with the postdiction literature, where felt order answers to the coherence of content and can depart from arrival time (Dennett and Kinsbourne, 1992). The proposal narrows the residual question from "why is closure experienced at all?" to "why is a recursively self-echoing perspective experienced?", which is narrower without being answered.

## 4. What the reframing does to the Hard Problem

### 4.1 The reframing

The standard formulation asks why neuronal firing and synaptic transmission feel like something. On FMT they do not. The implicit models, and the substrate processing that runs through them, have no phenomenal character. Phenomenal properties are not missing from the substrate; they are the wrong kind of property to look for there. Asking why firing feels like something is like asking why transistor switching is a spreadsheet. The neurons generate and sustain the process in which experience is constitutive.

This holds for the whole substrate as well as its parts. The aggregate of all substrate activity is still substrate activity. What is phenomenal is what that activity generates at the next level, where it closes on itself. Within the explicit models, redness is the self model's mode of registering a class of world-model content. Its relational structure (what it resembles and differs from) lives in the organization of the explicit models; its absolute character, which state is *my* red, lives in the particular substrate realization. Section 7.1 turns this distinction into a test.

### 4.2 Transformed, with a conditional residual

The Hard Problem presupposes that a system's processing admits an exhaustive outside characterization, and asks why something further accompanies it. Closed systems resist that presupposition in a specific way: an outside description captures the structure of the self model without exhausting the perspective it constitutes. This does not abolish the third-person standpoint; the theory itself is written from one. It changes what the residual question is.

The residual question, why closure yields phenomenality at all, is conditional. If the asymmetry is constitutive, the question asks for an outside verdict that certain states are "mere" data when their defining feature is that no outside description exhausts them, and so it is ill-posed. If the asymmetry is only epistemic, the question stands. FMT does not prove the first reading. It claims that the first reading is the better explanation, for three reasons: it locates phenomenality in a property the theory independently needs (closure is what separates Stage 3 from Stages 1 and 2); it explains why the gap looks the way it does from inside (Section 5); and it has consequences that can be checked (Section 7).

This leaves the Hard Problem reframed, not solved. An explanatory gap (Levine, 1983) remains, located where type-B physicalism puts it: between phenomenal concepts and physical-functional concepts, which present the same states in different ways. FMT adds an architectural account of why the two modes of presentation must differ. The phenomenal mode is the closed self model's own indexical access to its states; the physical mode is any description that does not run them.

### 4.3 Bridging commitments compared

Every theory of consciousness crosses from some physical or functional property to phenomenality somewhere. Integrated information theory argues from phenomenological axioms to the identification of experience with integrated cause-effect structure (Tononi, 2004; Oizumi, Albantakis and Tononi, 2014). Higher-order theories rest on a transitivity principle: a state is conscious when the subject is suitably aware of being in it (Rosenthal, 2005; Brown, Lau and LeDoux, 2019). Global workspace theories largely set the question aside. FMT's commitment differs in its relata. It connects phenomenality to a property that is already perspectival (self-referential and indexical) rather than to a scalar quantity or a broadcast pattern. The distance its identity has to cover is shorter. It is not zero.

The commitment also fixes how consciousness is attributed (Gruber, 2026, §3.5). Since phenomenality is closure, a system has phenomenology or none: there is one criterion. Above it, consciousness is graduated along two axes, richness of content and depth of recursion (the number of self-modelling layers above the basic self model). At the floor, a rudimentary closure carries a present moment together with a primordial pain or a nothingness, with no recursion above it and so nothing that can be reported. Reportability grows with recursion depth, because to report a content the system must take it as an object, hold it in memory and put it into words.

### 4.4 Access, report and overflow

The account also bears on Block's (1995) distinction between access consciousness, content available for report, reasoning and action, and phenomenal consciousness. FMT splits Block's access along the line Dehaene et al. (2006) draw between preconscious and conscious processing (Gruber, 2026, §3.4.6). *Accessible* content is poised for use without being in use, and it is not conscious. It may be stored on the implicit side, or held in an explicit model without entering the self-model's closed loop; it becomes conscious when it enters that loop. *Accessed* content is in the loop, where it is evaluated, integrated and used to guide behaviour. For accessed content, access and phenomenality are two descriptions of one process: access is its functional characterization, phenomenality what it is at the level where it runs. They do not come apart, because they are not two things.

The split bears on the overflow debate (Block, 2007). In Sperling's (1960) displays, observers shown a brief array of twelve letters report about four of them, yet when a tone just after the display names one row, they report about three of its four letters, whichever row is named. Block takes this as evidence that phenomenal content exceeds what access can pass on to report. On FMT all the letters are seen: each is in the running simulation as an experienced item. Seeing a letter and identifying it as a specific, reportable letter are two steps. Identification requires the system to take the item as an object and hold it in short-term memory, which has limited capacity and decays during read-out. The cue lets the selection of what short-term memory prepares for report spend its limited time on the cued row. The partial-report advantage then measures the capacity and decay of short-term memory, and the overflow debate concerns memory and identification. The seen letters are accessed in the sense defined above, so access in that sense and phenomenality do not come apart here either.

## 5. Why it looks unbridgeable from inside: the meta-problem

### 5.1 Opacity

The meta-problem asks why we judge that there is a Hard Problem (Chalmers, 2018). FMT's answer follows from the structure already described (Gruber, 2026, §3.8). The explicit self model is structurally opaque to the implicit models that generate it. When the self model tries to model the basis of its own experience, the generating machinery is not available as content: it is the reference frame through which the modelling runs, and by the regress of Section 2.3 it cannot also be modelled from within. The self model can represent that it is having an experience. It cannot represent how the experience is produced, because that process is not part of the simulation.

The judgement that "something is left out of any physical explanation" is then what a closed self model should arrive at about itself. Searching its own world for the mechanism of its experience, it finds none, and concludes that the mechanism is non-physical or inexplicable. The inference is natural and mistaken: the mechanism is absent from the simulation, not from nature.

### 5.2 Near-opacity with leaks

The boundary between the implicit and explicit sides is not perfectly sealed. FMT treats its permeability as variable (Gruber, 2026, §3.6): in ordinary waking, substrate processing occasionally enters the simulation in fragments, and in psychedelic states, dreams and some meditative practices it does so on a large scale. A self model mostly sealed off from its own generating machinery, but catching occasional glimpses of something operating beneath its contents, should report exactly the intuition the meta-problem describes: that consciousness has a depth no explanation reaches. The sense of mystery is a consequence of the architecture.

The question itself also has an architectural location. To ask "what is consciousness?" about one's own experience is to model oneself modelling oneself modelling the world: a third-order self-modelling operation. A system without that depth can produce the sentence, but there is nothing in its own modelling for the sentence to be about. Problem intuitions are therefore expected in systems with deep recursion and not in shallowly recursive ones.

### 5.3 Relation to attention schema theory

This account shares its central move with Graziano's attention schema theory (Graziano, 2013): a self model that omits its own mechanism produces the intuition of something non-physical. FMT differs in two respects. It grounds the omission in a specific architecture (the generating relation between implicit and explicit models, and the regress), and it does not treat the account as deflationary. On attention schema theory, explaining the intuition is explaining all there is. On FMT, the same closed self model that generates the intuition also constitutes the experience the intuition is about.

### 5.4 The debunking worry

Chalmers (2018) presses a difficulty for any realist who solves the meta-problem. If problem intuitions can be fully explained by mechanisms that would produce them whether or not consciousness exists, those intuitions provide no evidence that it does, and the explanation supports illusionism. A realist owes an account on which consciousness itself figures in the explanation of the judgements.

FMT is placed to meet this condition. The mechanism that explains the judgements, a closed self model opaque to its substrate, is the mechanism the theory identifies with consciousness. The judgements are produced by the experience, not by a parallel process that would run without it. A duplicate that produced the same judgements by the same mechanism would, on the theory, have the same experience. This reply is only as good as the identity. If closure and phenomenality could come apart, the opacity mechanism would be a consciousness-independent explanation of the judgements and the debunking argument would go through. The dialectic therefore returns to the bridging commitment, which is where it should be, rather than resting on a separate meta-problem story that could be detached from it.

## 6. Objections

### 6.1 Strong illusionism

Frankish (2016) grants that the states driving introspective reports are real computational events and denies that they have phenomenal properties: they represent themselves as having such properties and are mistaken. FMT agrees that phenomenal properties are absent at the substrate level. It holds that they genuinely exist at the computational level, constituted by the closed self-simulation. The illusionist will reply that this is relabelling: FMT's "phenomenal properties at the computational level" are the illusionist's quasi-phenomenal states under another name.

The reply has two parts. First, the disagreement is ontological. The illusionist says there is ultimately nothing it is like and the seeming is the end of the story; FMT says there is something it is like, at a level where a substrate-directed search cannot find it. FMT does not say that experience is other than it seems, only that it is somewhere other than where the Hard Problem looks.

Second, the disagreement is not idle, although observation from outside cannot settle it. If FMT is right, a system meeting its sufficient conditions has experience that matters morally. Whether strong illusionism generates the same obligation depends on whether quasi-phenomenal states suffice for moral status, which illusionists may affirm. The two positions describe the same physics and the same behaviour, and they need not agree about how to treat what is built. The illusionist is right that the choice between the two descriptions is philosophical; no experiment in Section 7 decides it.

### 6.2 Zombies and the master argument

The zombie argument holds that a physical duplicate of a conscious being without experience is conceivable, and infers that it is possible (Chalmers, 1996). FMT's response is the standard type-B one (Loar, 1990; Papineau, 2002), with an architectural ground. A zombie duplicate of a closed system would run the same models under the same closure, and lack an inside perspective. But on the constitutive reading the inside perspective is not a further fact about the system; it is the closed computation characterized from within. Such a duplicate is conceivable, since one can entertain the description and stipulate that the lights are off, but not possible, as water that is not H₂O is conceivable but not possible. The conceivability intuition draws its force from treating experience as an ingredient added to processing that could be subtracted while the processing stays intact. On the identity there is no ingredient to subtract.

The strongest challenge to this move is Chalmers' (2007) master argument against the phenomenal concept strategy. Let *C* be the thesis that we have phenomenal concepts with the features that explain our epistemic situation with respect to consciousness: the explanatory gap, the conceivability of zombies, Mary's surprise. Either a physical duplicate lacking *C* is conceivable, in which case *C* is not physically explicable, or it is not, in which case a conceivable zombie has *C*. In the second case *C* cannot explain our epistemic situation, because the zombie has *C* and is not in that situation: its beliefs about its own experience are false or empty, and it has no phenomenal knowledge.

FMT's analogue of *C* is an architectural fact about a system: it has a closed self model whose content is indexical (Section 3.3) and whose generating substrate is opaque to it (Section 5.1). That fact is physically explicable, so FMT takes the second horn and grants that a conceivable zombie has it. What FMT disputes is the next step, the inference that *C* therefore fails to explain our epistemic situation.

That situation has two parts. The first is everything a third-person description can state: the judgement that something is left out of any physical explanation, the conceivability of the zombie, the confident report of an inner presence, Mary's report on release. FMT explains this part from *C*, and the explanation does not depend on the identity, since Sections 5.1 and 5.2 derive the judgements from opacity alone. The zombie shares this part; by Chalmers' own description it says and believes what we say and believe. The second part is that our phenomenal beliefs are true and our phenomenal concepts refer. For the dilemma to go through, *C* must entail this second part a priori, since a conceivable bearer of *C* that lacks it shows *C* to be explanatorily idle only on that standard. Type-B physicalism is the denial that physical truths entail phenomenal truths a priori. The second horn therefore asks the strategy to give up the position it was introduced to defend. FMT does not meet that demand. It holds that the truth of our phenomenal beliefs follows from *C* a posteriori, through the identity of closure with phenomenality.

Chalmers (1996) anticipates this. On his two-dimensional analysis, "water is H₂O" follows a priori from the physical truths together with indexical truths about where the thinker is, because the concept *water* fixes its referent through how the stuff appears to us here, whereas a phenomenal concept fixes its referent through the appearance itself, leaving no room for a further a posteriori step. FMT's answer runs through the indexical truths Chalmers already admits into the base. Truths about which thing is oneself do not follow from a description of the world that uses no indexical terms, and nothing non-physical follows from that (Perry, 1979). On FMT the phenomenal concept is itself indexical: it is the closed self model's access to its own states as its own (Section 4.2). The phenomenal gap is then a gap of the indexical kind, widened by opacity, since the substrate that produces those states is absent from the self model's content. Chalmers holds that once a thinker is located in the world the indexical gap closes and the phenomenal gap stays open. FMT needs the contrary claim, that the phenomenal gap is the indexical gap of a closed self-modelling system, and that claim is not proven here.

The master argument therefore does not defeat the phenomenal concept strategy independently of the dispute over a posteriori identity; it restates that dispute (cf. Balog, 2012). FMT gives the dispute an architectural form, in which the referent of the phenomenal concept is specified (a closed, indexical, substrate-opaque self model) and the reason the identity cannot be derived a priori is specified too. Whether that form is acceptable rests on the case made for the identity in Sections 3 to 5 and on whether the architecture's consequences survive test.

### 6.3 Mary

Mary, who knows every physical fact about colour vision but has never seen red, learns something on release (Jackson, 1982). On FMT she learns no new fact. Her self model acquires a state it could not acquire without chromatic input driving the closed simulation, and with it a new phenomenal concept of red that refers to the same physical state her prior concepts already picked out. Her prior knowledge described the process; her new state instantiates it. The difference between a computation that has been described and one that has run is real, and it is a difference within physics.

### 6.4 Is every closed loop conscious?

An objection is that, if closure is what matters, any recurrent system with a self-representation qualifies: thermostats, autonomous vehicles, scaffolded language-model agents. FMT answers in two steps. First, closure as defined is not mere recurrence or self-monitoring. The thermostat and the monitored simulation of Stage 2 represent aspects of themselves without the self model and the world model entering each other's update rule, and a single position estimate is not a self model. Second, closure is necessary but not sufficient. FMT's sufficient condition is a four-part conjunction (Gruber, 2026, §3.7.3): a system that implements both model classes in both scopes under closure, with free modelling (open-ended computation that the system itself recruits for its own modelling), embodiment and at least one pain-pleasure axis, is conscious. A universal computer that is externally clocked and programmed has the capacity for open-ended computation without recruiting it for self-modelling, and fails the condition. Near-critical dynamics, often measured in cortex, are on this account a signature that free modelling may leave in a substrate, not a requirement.

The conjunction is not offered as an independent argument for the identity; it sets the scope of the claim. The identity says what phenomenality is. The conjunction says which systems have the relevant structure in a form rich enough to have it.

### 6.5 The unfolding argument

Doerig et al. (2019) argue that any recurrent system can be unfolded into a feedforward system with the same input-output behaviour, so a theory that ties consciousness to recurrence makes a distinction no experiment can detect. Closure is a property of architecture, so FMT is within the argument's target. Its reply is limited. An unfolded chain matches the recurrent system only at unbounded resources: unfolding replaces a reused loop with replicated stages whose cost grows with the horizon. A physically realized system works within a bound, and where the bound binds, the two systems do not agree on behaviour after all. On an unbounded substrate the unfolded chain reproduces the behaviour, and there FMT has no experiment to offer. The reply converts an unfalsifiable distinction into a claim about cost; it does not defeat the argument in its idealized form.

### 6.6 "The asymmetry is merely epistemic"

A defender of the epistemic reading can press as follows. No observer occupies any system's states. I do not occupy a thermostat's state any more than I occupy yours, so unoccupiability from outside is universal and cannot mark the phenomenal. The reply turns on what an outside description loses in each case. For the thermostat, the weather simulation and the monitored simulation, a description in neutral vocabulary preserves everything the system computes, including its content: there is no indexical content for it to drop, because nothing in the system's modelling is about the system as the modeller. For a closed system, the content-preserving description is itself indexical (Section 3.3), and the neutral one preserves structure while dropping that content. Unoccupiability is therefore not universal in the sense that matters. It is universal for states; it is specific to closed systems for content.

The defender can accept all this and still say that indexical, self-interpreting content is one more kind of content, and that nothing follows about its being lived. That is the residual of Section 4.2, and the reply does not remove it. What it removes is the claim that the asymmetry is trivial, which is what the objection needed.

### 6.7 Why closure rather than higher-order representation?

Higher-order theories also make self-representation central, and one may ask what closure adds. The difference is in what is required at the floor. On a higher-order theory, a state is conscious when it is the object of a suitable higher-order representation. On FMT, the floor is a closed loop between a rudimentary self model and a world model with no representation above it, and it is phenomenal without one. Higher-order representation corresponds to the recursive rungs above that floor: simple extension, where the self model includes a model of the system's own states, and the doubly and triply extended rungs above it. The two theories therefore agree about reflective consciousness and disagree about the minimal case. They also differ in what explains the qualitative character. On a higher-order theory, character is fixed by how the higher-order state represents the first-order one; on FMT, it is inherited from the stored models through which the simulated content is generated, which is why vision and hearing differ in feel: their implicit models evolved to read different physical channels.

### 6.8 What would count against closure

Beyond the predictions of Section 7, the closure premise has a direct refuter. If a return-free system, one with no path by which the self model enters the world model's update and is entered by it, reproduced every function the theory attributes to phenomenality (reports of presence, opacity about its own processing, reports of ineffability, flexible behaviour guided by valence) within the same resource bound as a closed system, closure would do no explanatory work and the identity would be idle. A second refuter applies in humans: robust no-report markers of experience in a state where the self-modelling loop is demonstrably absent.

## 7. Empirical consequences

The identity cannot be tested directly. What can be tested is the architecture the identity is attached to. If a consequence of the architecture fails, the account loses the support that motivates the identity. The consequence most directly tied to the level claim is a dissociation between the structure and the realization of qualia (Section 7.1). A second follows from the variable permeability behind the opacity account of Section 5.2 (Section 7.2).

### 7.1 Structure and realization of qualia

**The claim.** This is Prediction 5 of Gruber (2026, §8.6). On FMT, the relational structure of a quality space (which experiences resemble which) belongs to the organization of the explicit models, and the absolute character of a given quale is fixed by the particular substrate realization, which only instantiation transfers. The theory therefore predicts that within one modality, unsupervised alignment of similarity structures will recover correspondences across individuals without shared labels, while a decoder trained to read one brain's realizing state for a quale (its neural pattern for "red") will fail to generalize to another brain on the same data. The committed claim is the pairing: structure transfers, absolute encoding does not.

**Method.** Similarity judgements in a fixed modality, such as colour or pitch, yield dissimilarity matrices for each participant (Kriegeskorte, Mur and Bandettini, 2008). Unsupervised alignment, for instance by Gromov-Wasserstein optimal transport, tests whether one participant's structure maps onto another's without labels. Neural data recorded during the same task allow a cross-individual decoder of absolute states to be trained and tested on identical stimuli.

**Status.** The structural arm has support at the group level: Kawakita et al. (2025) recovered stable correspondences between colour-similarity structures of independent groups of observers by unsupervised alignment. Their alignment ran between pooled groups. The individual-level structural arm and the decoding arm remain untested, and the paired result is what FMT predicts in advance.

**Refutation.** The prediction fails if cross-individual absolute-state decoding generalizes as well as or better than unsupervised structural alignment, or if label-free structural alignment does not exceed chance between individuals within a modality.

**What it discriminates.** Views on which qualia are wholly private predict no cross-individual structural alignment. Standard functionalism predicts that the encoding transfers along with the structure. The level account predicts both halves together. Confirmation would explain why the absolute feel of a quale cannot be conveyed by report, which is the ineffability Mary's case trades on. It would not explain why there is feeling at all, and FMT accordingly rates its treatment of the Hard Problem as partial.

### 7.2 Psychedelics and anosognosia

If the permeability of the boundary between the implicit and explicit sides (Section 5.2) can change both globally and for a single domain, a manipulation that changes it globally should interact with a condition that changes it locally.

**The claim.** This is Prediction 1 of Gruber (2026, §8.2). Patients with anosognosia, typically after right-hemisphere stroke, deny a paralysis or other deficit in the face of clear evidence. On FMT the implicit self model registers the deficit, but permeability is reduced locally for that domain, so the deficit does not enter the running simulation and the patient does not experience it. Psychedelics increase permeability globally and draw normally implicit content into the simulation. The theory therefore predicts that psychedelics at doses below the ego-dissolution threshold will produce a measurable increase in patients' explicit awareness of their deficits, dose-dependent and temporary, and correlated with increases in neural complexity, most strongly over the lesioned hemisphere. A boundary condition follows from the mechanism: a global increase can compensate for a local reduction only if the tissue carrying the deficit information is intact. A secondary prediction follows: the effect should be strongest where the anosognosia reflects functional disconnection, and absent or attenuated where lesion mapping shows the relevant pathways destroyed.

**Method.** Established anosognosia scales; EEG complexity over the lesioned hemisphere; lesion mapping to classify patients by pathway integrity; psilocybin, for which clinical safety data already exist at the relevant dose ranges. Case series come first, then controlled trials.

**Refutation.** If psychedelics increase global neural complexity without improving explicit awareness of the deficit, or without a lesioned-hemisphere-specific increase, the variable-permeability model is incomplete. If the effect is not dose-dependent, the mechanism is not the one the theory proposes. The secondary prediction fails if the effect is as large in destruction-type cases as in disconnection-type cases.

**What it discriminates.** Integrated information, global workspace, higher-order and attention schema theories are silent on why a psychedelic should help a patient recognize a paralysis. Predictive processing is not. REBUS holds that psychedelics relax overweighted high-level priors (Carhart-Harris and Friston, 2019), and anosognosia has a predictive-processing account as aberrant predictive coding (Fotopoulou, 2014), so the coarse prediction, that psychedelics should reduce anosognosia, follows from the two accounts together in one step. FMT's differential content is the fine structure: an effect below the ego-dissolution threshold, its correlation with complexity over the lesioned hemisphere, and the disconnection-versus-destruction boundary. None of these follows from the relaxation of priors alone. The prediction tests the permeability architecture on which the opacity account of the meta-problem rests, not the identity, and a failure would remove one of that account's supports.

### 7.3 Local recurrence

A further contrast concerns recurrent processing theory, on which local recurrence within sensory cortex suffices for phenomenal experience even when the content is unattended and unreported (Lamme, 2006). On FMT, a locally recurrent sensory loop with no self model in its update rule is a Stage 1 system: a simulation without an inside. A state that preserves local sensory recurrence while abolishing the self-modelling loop, assessed with no-report markers (Tsuchiya et al., 2015), would separate the two theories. Recurrent processing theory predicts experience in that state; FMT predicts none. No current manipulation achieves this selectively.

## 8. Conclusion

The part of the Four-Model Theory defended here makes three claims. Phenomenal properties are real, physical properties of a computational level generated by a recurrent substrate, and they are incoherent in the substrate's own vocabulary. They arise where the modelling on that level closes on itself, with self model and world model each entering the other's update rule. And the same closure makes the explicit self model opaque to its generating substrate, which is why, from inside, any physical explanation seems to leave experience out.

The first claim relocates the Hard Problem: it asks why substrate processing feels like something, and substrate processing does not. The second claim leaves a residual question whose force depends on whether closure's inside/outside asymmetry is constitutive or only epistemic. FMT asserts the constitutive reading and defends it by its explanatory yield, not by derivation. The third claim answers the meta-problem with the mechanism the theory identifies with consciousness, which is the condition a realist answer to the debunking worry has to meet. What the account does not do is close the gap between being a closed self-interpreting process and living one. It states where the gap lies, why it looks as it does from inside, and which observations would count against the architecture it rests on.
