# Felt Authorship Without a Visible Cause: Separating Representation from Control in AI-Assisted Writing

*Submission to the NeurIPS 2026 Workshop "AI and the Self: Human Identity, Authenticity, and Agency in the Age of AI". Anonymous for review.*

## Abstract

Writers who use AI assistance report low ownership of the resulting text yet declare themselves authors — the AI Ghostwriter Effect. The natural explanation, imported from the theory of apparent mental causation, is that a visible AI contribution violates the exclusivity condition on the experience of will. We argue that the interface manipulations reported so far confound two variables this explanation treats as one: how much *control* the writer has over a contribution, and whether the contribution is *represented* as a contribution at the moment it enters the text. A self-simulation account of volition (Gruber, 2026) predicts that the two dissociate, with opposite signs: control raises felt authorship, representation lowers it. This puts transparency and felt authorship in partial opposition — a design trade-off currently invisible because the two variables travel together. We give a within-subjects design that varies representation at fixed control, and state what result would falsify the account.

## 1 The Ghostwriter puzzle

Draxler et al. (2024) studied personalized language generation with a language model and asked participants two questions that are usually assumed to have the same answer: do you own this text, and are you its author? The answers came apart. Participants did not consider themselves the owners and authors of AI-generated text, yet refrained from publicly declaring AI authorship. Draxler et al. named this dissociation the AI Ghostwriter Effect and found it across two studies (n = 30 and n = 96).

Two further results from the same paper matter here. First, higher levels of participants' influence on the texts increased their sense of ownership: the more the writer shaped the output, the more it felt like theirs. Personalization of the content, by contrast, did not affect the Ghostwriter Effect. Second, participants were more likely to attribute ownership to supposedly *human* ghostwriters than to AI ghostwriters, producing a larger ownership–authorship discrepancy in the human case. We return to this last finding in §6; the account developed below does not explain it.

The Ghostwriter Effect is a clean instance of what this workshop asks about: an AI system participating in an act — writing — through which people ordinarily constitute themselves, and measurably altering the writer's relation to the product. The question we take up is narrower than authenticity at large. It is: *what property of the interaction determines whether the text feels like the writer's own?* The literature's answer is the writer's control. We think the measured effect is confounded, and that the confound conceals a design trade-off.

## 2 Exclusivity, and what it leaves confounded

Draxler et al. explain the effect through control. Its natural theoretical home, and the account to beat, is Wegner and Wheatley (1999). On their account of apparent mental causation, the experience of willing an action is not a direct readout of causation but an inference: I experience my thought as causing my act when the thought precedes the act (priority), matches it (consistency), and no other candidate cause is in view (exclusivity). The experience of will can therefore be produced where there is no causation and withheld where there is.

Applied to assisted writing, the account is straightforward. A visible AI contribution is an obvious competing cause. Exclusivity is violated, and felt authorship drops. This predicts the direction of the Ghostwriter Effect, and it fits the influence result: more influence, fewer moments at which a competing cause is salient. Interface work has proceeded on the same logic. Joshi and Vogel (2025) report that writing with a language model lowers psychological ownership relative to unassisted writing, and that participants who composed longer prompts retained more of it. Hoque et al. (2024) built a tool that visualizes the provenance of each text segment and report, from a study with thirteen creative writers, that it helped them retain a sense of control and ownership.

Here is the difficulty. These manipulations move two variables at once. A prompt the writer composes at length is both more of the writer's own work *and* a stretch of text that never arrives marked as anyone else's. Provenance visualization adds information about origins *and* changes behaviour: under marking, the verbatim-AI share of writers' final stories fell from 14% to 3% (Hoque et al., 2024). Nothing in the reported literature, so far as we can establish, varies the writer's control over a contribution while holding fixed whether that contribution is represented as external — or the reverse. The measured sign therefore belongs to the bundle. If control and representation push felt authorship in the *same* direction, the bundling is harmless. If they push in *opposite* directions, the literature has been reading a net effect and attributing it to a mechanism.

One published result already strains the bundled reading. In a follow-up, Joshi and Vogel (2026) found that interaction techniques encouraging longer prompts raised both prompt length and psychological ownership — but that adding AI-generated *suggestions* for expanding the prompt raised prompt length without any improvement in ownership. The writer-contribution metric moved in both cases; ownership moved in only one. The difference between them is not how much text the writer committed to the prompt but where the increment visibly came from. That is a single study and it was not designed to test this, so we do not lean on it. It is the shape of the dissociation we predict, observed as a by-product.

The exclusivity account gives no reason to expect opposite signs; a competing cause that the writer controls is still a competing cause. The account we now introduce gives a specific reason.

## 3 The apparatus is the one thing not in the scene

Gruber (2026) proposes a self-simulation theory of consciousness on which experience is a simulation run by a neural substrate, populated by model kinds distinguished along two dimensions: implicit against explicit, world against self. One structural claim matters here. The simulation models the world, the body, other agents, and the self. It does not model the apparatus generating the simulation — representing it is a job the apparatus would have to perform on itself while performing everything else.

This omission has a specific consequence for volition. Causes that go unrepresented do not normally make their effects feel uncaused, because the simulation supplies an in-scene stand-in: nobody represents their gastric physiology, yet hunger is felt as coming from an empty stomach — a body-model object standing in for the unmodeled cause. A decision has no stand-in available. Its proximate cause is the apparatus itself, so the simulation assigns it to the self-model, and the self is where the regress stops, because what stands behind the self is the unrepresented machinery. The choice presents not as uncaused — that would feel arbitrary, something happening *to* me — but as *originating* in the self, with nothing behind the self. Felt freedom is what an origin feels like from inside. Reasons are no counterexample: they appear in the scene as contents weighed, never as the thing that produces the outcome of the weighing.

The account addresses phenomenology only. Whether choices are free in any metaphysical sense is a question about physics on which it is silent, and it settles nothing about who the author *is* in the normative or legal sense.

One independent line of evidence bears on the assumption that source attribution is not carried by an intrinsic tag on content. Dijkstra and Fleming (2023) found that imagined and perceived signals are intermixed within a sensory channel, with judgments of reality turning on whether the combined signal strength crosses a threshold: no single factor unambiguously marks a signal's source. Text entering a writer's draft is a within-channel case — words carry no intrinsic marker of who produced them — so an outward attribution must be built from something represented at the moment the text enters.

The application to assisted writing is direct. A suggestion the writer sees, weighs, and accepts is in-scene as an external contribution — a content weighed, like a colleague's proposed sentence. It enters the draft already attributed, and it does not become an origin. A contribution absorbed without separate representation — a silent completion, an ambient rewriting, a model that has shaped the writer's earlier drafts and through them the writer's habits — has no stand-in, and the regress terminates at the self exactly as for an unassisted thought. What determines felt authorship, on this account, is not the fraction of the text the writer produced and not the control the writer exercised, but whether each contribution received an in-scene representation *as a contribution* at the moment it entered.

## 4 A prediction that separates representation from control

The prediction follows from the structure just given. Hold control constant and vary only representation, and felt authorship should dissociate from control. Two interfaces delivering the identical final text, with identical accept/reject control and identical writer keystroke share, should produce different felt ownership depending on whether the system's contribution was separately represented when it entered. The sign is the substantive part: **representation should lower felt authorship, while control raises it.** An interface that maximizes both — the writer-drives-everything, full-provenance design the literature converges on — is pulling in two directions at once, and the reported net effect underestimates both components.

The prediction discriminates. The exclusivity account, as an inference theory, ties felt authorship to the writer's *beliefs* about causal origin: a competing cause the writer knows about should defeat exclusivity whether or not it was marked in the moment. The representational account ties felt authorship to what was *in the scene* when the text entered: a writer who knows in general that an assistant is active, but whose specific contributions arrive unmarked, has the belief without the representation — and the account predicts the felt ownership of an unassisted writer, belief notwithstanding. Where belief and moment-of-entry representation are separated, the two accounts come apart. The influence effect reported by Draxler et al. is consistent with both, which is exactly the point: it was measured under a bundle.

If the prediction is right, it names a trade-off that current design practice cannot see. Transparency about AI contribution — marking, attribution, provenance — is the accepted instrument for the goal this workshop's call states: supporting reflection over over-reliance. Felt authorship of one's own writing is served, on this account, by the *absence* of such marking. The two goals are in partial opposition, and interfaces optimize both at once without knowing it. The long-horizon case sharpens it. A model that has shaped a writer's drafts across months operates through the writer's own habits; its contributions are structurally unmarkable, arrive with no stand-in, and should feel entirely self-originated. The forms of dependence this workshop asks how to measure are, on this account, precisely the forms that are phenomenologically silent — the writer whose style has drifted furthest toward the model is the one to whom the writing feels most their own.

## 5 An experiment that decomposes the confound

**Design.** Within-subjects, two conditions, order counterbalanced. Participants write short argumentative texts (e.g., a letter to a local newspaper) with a writing interface backed by the same model in both conditions. Contributions are scripted per prompt from a fixed pool, so that the assistance offered — content, length, position — is matched across conditions and across participants; the interface is a controlled instrument, not a live product.

**The manipulation.** In both conditions, every system contribution requires the same motor act to enter the draft: it is staged at the cursor and the writer must actively confirm or dismiss it, with unlimited editing afterward. Control — accept/reject affordance, edit rights, keystroke share — is thereby held constant. The single difference is representation at entry. In the **marked** condition, the staged text appears visually attributed: highlighted, labeled as assistant output, distinct from the writer's own text until confirmed. In the **unmarked** condition, the staged text appears in the draft's own typography, as a continuation awaiting confirmation, with no attribution. Both conditions begin with identical disclosure that an assistant will contribute — global belief about AI involvement is constant; only the moment-of-entry representation varies.

**Measures.** Four, detailed in Appendix A: felt ownership of the final text (the ownership measure of Draxler et al., 2024, for comparability), declared authorship, a source-memory test over the assistant's passages, and a post-debriefing re-measure of felt ownership. Declared authorship is measured separately from felt ownership because Draxler et al. showed the two dissociate, and the account here addresses felt ownership only.

**Predictions, in order of evidential weight.** First, felt ownership is lower in the marked condition at identical control and identical final-text quality. Second, the debrief measure discriminates the accounts: on an inference account, revealing the contributions should collapse the conditions, since beliefs are now matched; on the representational account, the difference survives debriefing, because a stand-in cannot be installed retroactively into an experience already had — knowledge updates the declared-authorship measure, not the felt-ownership one. Third, source memory is worse for unmarked contributions.

**Falsification.** The account is wrong if felt ownership does not differ between conditions — control, on this account, is not the carrier, so equal-control conditions must still separate. It is wrong with the sign reversed if the marked condition yields *higher* felt ownership. And its representational core is wrong if the condition difference tracks belief — if debriefing collapses it — even should the first prediction hold. Each of these outcomes is informative: the first returns the field to control as the operative variable, the second supports an exclusivity reading on which salient help licenses ownership, and the third supports an inference account with representation as mere evidence.

## 6 Limitations, and what this account does not explain

Three limits.

The human-ghostwriter finding of §1 is not explained here. A human ghostwriter is fully represented in the scene as an external contributor, so the mechanism above predicts the writer's own felt ownership to be low — which is what happens — but it is silent on why *declared* authorship should detach further precisely when the ghostwriter is human. The natural explanation is social and normative — declaring human ghostwriting carries obligations and stigma that declaring tool use does not — and it lies outside a theory of the phenomenology of volition. The account covers one of Draxler et al.'s two measures.

The account is descriptive, and confirmation would be partial. A confirmed dissociation with the predicted sign would establish that representation, not control, carries felt authorship in assisted writing — a result of direct design consequence. It would not establish the self-simulation theory it was derived from; other mechanisms could produce the same dissociation, and identifying the derivation is not a claim of unique entailment. Conversely, the theory's broader claims do not stand or fall with this experiment alone.

Felt authorship is not authorship. Nothing above bears on whether a writer whose unmarked assistant shaped a text is entitled to sign it, owes disclosure, or holds copyright. The account predicts when a text will feel self-originated, including cases where that feeling is, by any external description, misattributed. If the prediction holds, the misattribution is systematic and designable — which is a reason for the norms conversation to know about it, and no substitute for having that conversation. A disclosure belongs here for the same reason: this paper was drafted with AI assistance under the marked condition of §5, every contribution staged and attributed before it entered the draft. No declaration is solicited for this venue. We record it because a paper on the phenomenology of assisted writing that concealed its own production would be a poor instrument.

## Appendix: Experimental design detail

**Measures.** (i) Felt ownership of the final text, via the ownership measure used by Draxler et al. (2024), so results are comparable to the effect being explained. (ii) Declared authorship, measured separately — willingness to sign sole-authored, and whether the participant would disclose AI contribution — since Draxler et al. showed the two dissociate and the account here addresses felt ownership only. (iii) A source-memory test: participants identify which passages the assistant contributed. This is the manipulation check and more: the account predicts degraded source memory for unmarked contributions, since no attribution was built at entry. (iv) After full debriefing that reveals every contribution, felt ownership is measured again.

**Power and preregistration.** With a smallest effect of interest of d = 0.3 on the within-subjects ownership contrast, α = 0.05 two-tailed and 0.90 power, approximately 120 participants are required. Effects, exclusions, and analyses (mixed-effects models with participant and prompt as random factors) should be preregistered. Perceived text quality must be measured and modeled, since marking could plausibly change quality perception and quality could carry ownership.
