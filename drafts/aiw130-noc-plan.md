## Abstract draft
Is there more to consciousness than computation? This paper answers yes — and argues that the additional ingredient is itself computation: a second-order regime in which computation operates on computation, the system's self-model modelling its own modelling. On this account consciousness is not a special substance and not a special substrate but a dynamical regime — self-referential closure sustained at criticality, the edge of chaos between order and disorder. The claim yields a sharp, testable lead: in biological brains, no criticality means no consciousness. Across anaesthetic, sleep, and pathological transitions the presence of experience tracks whether cortical dynamics sit near the critical point; pushing the cortex below it abolishes experience, and restoring near-critical dynamics returns it. Existing accounts treat cortical criticality as generically optimal for information processing, but optimality predicts criticality broadly and continuously, whereas the brain holds it selectively, gated to conscious states even when unconscious processing is heavy. The four-model theory explains this selectivity: criticality is the necessary substrate for the higher-order computation that constitutes experience, so it is deployed where and when that computation runs and released otherwise. An unexplained empirical regularity thereby becomes a predicted signature — one that has sat unrecognised in the neuroimaging record for two decades because it raises no measurable ability and carries a short-term cost. The paper adopts a standard operational definition of consciousness, operationalises the lead claim through convergent criticality measures and stated disconfirming results, positions the theory on the computationalism axis, and shows its consistency with integration, world-modelling, and recursive self-modelling frameworks.

## Anchor argument (lead)
THE LEAD ARGUMENT, in full.

Premise 1 — the necessary condition (biological scope). Across pharmacological, physiological, and pathological state transitions, the presence of consciousness tracks whether cortical dynamics sit near the critical point between order and disorder. Cortex exhibits neuronal avalanches consistent with self-organised criticality (Beggs & Plenz 2003); the waking set-point is slightly subcritical (Priesemann et al. 2014); the circuit is homeostatically regulated back toward criticality after perturbation (Ma et al. 2019); near-critical slow electrodynamics support consciousness and collapse when it is lost (Toker et al. 2022); large-scale synthesis reports criticality as a setpoint that tracks conscious state (Hengen & Shew 2025; Algom & Shriki 2026). Push the cortex subcritical (deep dreamless sleep, propofol) and experience vanishes; keep it near-critical or drive it past (ketamine, psychedelics) and experience persists though altered. For biological brains this licenses a sharp, disconfirmable lead: no criticality, no consciousness. Crucially, this correlation is SHARED with the entire criticality program — it is converging evidence, not yet anything FMT-specific.

Premise 2 — the puzzle the field leaves open. Why is the cortex critical at all? The standard answer is optimisation: criticality maximises dynamic range (Shew et al. 2009), information transmission and capacity (Beggs 2008; Shew & Plenz 2013), susceptibility and correlation length (Chialvo 2010), and the brain is homeostatically tuned to it (Ma et al. 2019). This literature must be engaged at full strength, not strawmanned: critical dynamics are demonstrably good for information processing, and the homeostatic-set-point work is a genuine, mechanistic account of how the brain gets there. The theory does NOT deny any of this.

The stress-test. An optimisation rationale is substrate-general and time-general: if criticality were maintained because it is generically optimal for information processing, the expectation is criticality broadly and continuously — wherever and whenever the brain computes hard. But criticality is deployed SELECTIVELY. It is gated to conscious states and released in states where massive unconscious processing continues unabated — deep NREM (memory consolidation, homeostatic and autonomic traffic) and anaesthesia are not low-computation states, yet they are subcritical and unconscious. Conversely, covert-awareness cases show near-critical dynamics with consciousness present despite no behavioural output, i.e. no expressed processing demand. The honest form of the wedge: the optimisation/homeostatic account predicts criticality should track computational load or benefit; the data show it tracking CONSCIOUSNESS specifically. Where load and consciousness dissociate (heavy-load unconscious sleep; low-output covert awareness), criticality follows consciousness, not load. This is an explanatory incompleteness in the optimisation account on the SELECTIVITY — not a refutation of its claim that criticality is useful. Stated this way it survives hostile review: I concede optimality is correct about the benefits and concede homeostatic tuning is real; I deny only that usefulness-in-general explains why the brain spends to hold criticality in conscious states and lets it go otherwise.

FMT's move — the explanatory payoff. Criticality is held selectively because it is the necessary substrate for a specific higher-order computation: self-referential closure, the self-model modelling its own modelling — computation upon computation. Modelling an open-ended, self-referential process requires open-ended (universal) computation, and in a dynamical substrate open-ended computation lives only at the edge of chaos (ordered enough to sustain long-range structured correlations, disordered enough to avoid collapse to fixed points or cycles). So the cortex is driven to criticality and held there by the evolutionary demand to run that second-order computation for autonomous self-modelling — not because criticality is generically optimal, but because it is the enabling condition for the computation that IS consciousness. The trichotomy that makes this non-trivial (stated in edge-of-chaos terms, with Wolfram/Class-4 vocabulary removed): a substrate must be CAPABLE of open-ended computation, must ACTUALLY DEPLOY it, and must do so for AUTONOMOUS SELF-MODELLING. A universal digital computer satisfies the first, fails the third (heteronomous — externally clocked and programmed), and so nothing drives it to criticality; universality alone leaves it non-conscious. This is why the argument is not "criticality is optimal therefore present" but "criticality is the substrate the required computation must occupy, therefore selectively present exactly when that computation runs."

The reframe of the evidence. This turns an unexplained empirical curiosity into a PREDICTED SIGNATURE. FMT predicts criticality present where/when second-order self-modelling runs and absent otherwise — which is precisely the observed consciousness-locked pattern. The optimisation account is CONSISTENT WITH criticality being present; only FMT PREDICTS its selective, consciousness-locked deployment. "Hidden in plain sight": the signature has been in the neuroimaging record for two decades, read as an efficiency/optimality property rather than as the fingerprint of the computation that constitutes experience — a forest-for-trees detection failure. Why it hides: it raises no measurable ability (no IQ gain), gives no short-term advantage (short-term it costs — near-criticality is metabolically expensive and courts supercritical runaway), and optimises only a few narrow long-horizon capacities (consequence-simulation, planning). That non-optimising profile is itself why the brain is not critical everywhere-always, and why nobody flagged the signature as the marker of consciousness.

Operationalisation and falsification (carried into §5). Three convergent signatures — branching ratio σ→1, DFA exponent α in 0.6–0.9 (primary measure), power-law avalanche exponent ≈ −3/2 — committed as a convergence, not singly, precisely because power laws alone are not criticality (Touboul & Destexhe 2017; and the "how critical is brain criticality" reviews are cited, not dodged). EXTENT (spatial edge-of-chaos recruitment, a percolation-style order parameter) distinguished from raw co-activation. Disconfirmers: (a) all three signatures intact but no consciousness by independent criteria; (b) robust consciousness with cortex demonstrably subcritical on all three; (c) high edge-of-chaos extent with unconsciousness. The generalised tonic-clonic seizure is the built-in negative control: near-maximal co-activation but hypersynchronous, LOW edge-of-chaos extent → unconscious, as predicted.

Scope guardrail (load-bearing). This "no criticality → no consciousness" holds for BIOLOGICAL neural tissue. Criticality is the FORM the required open-ended computation takes in a neural substrate — a non-universal signature, not a substrate-neutral necessity. The substrate-neutral requirement is open-ended computation deployed for autonomous self-modelling; a different (e.g. digital) substrate could realize the same second-order computation without exhibiting neuronal-avalanche statistics. This is deliberately NOT asserted as substrate-neutral, to keep Lane A consistent with the JAIC/open-ended-computation slice — one condition seen from two sides.

How it engages the strongest counter-literature: it does not deny that criticality is optimal (Shew, Plenz, Chialvo, Beggs) or that the set-point is homeostatically maintained (Ma et al., Hengen & Shew); it grants both and locates the residual they cannot cover — the consciousness-locked SELECTIVITY of deployment — then supplies the teleological reason (second-order computation) that the optimisation frame lacks. The claim is "correct but incomplete," the honest-convergence register throughout.
