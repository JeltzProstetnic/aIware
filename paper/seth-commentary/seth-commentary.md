Seth (2025) frames the central question precisely: what causal architecture must a system instantiate for consciousness? His argument that current AI systems are not conscious is well-taken — not because they lack biological matter, but because they lack the right kind of causal organisation. Seth does not rule out non-biological consciousness in principle, but argues it is unlikely along current trajectories, because the relevant causal powers may be inseparable from living self-maintenance — metabolism, autopoiesis, the precariousness of far-from-equilibrium systems. The Four-Model Theory (FMT; Gruber, 2015, 2026) takes up his narrower challenge: that current approaches leave those powers underspecified. It offers a candidate specification — while flagging, at the outset, that whether such powers can be specified at the architectural level rather than the biological one is exactly where it and Seth may part ways.

FMT identifies consciousness with a self-referential simulation generated by two kinds of models running in parallel — world-directed and self-directed — each with an implicit (learned, non-phenomenal) and an explicit (simulated, phenomenal) mode. The critical architectural property is self-referential closure: the system's model of the world includes a model of itself modelling the world. FMT does not claim to solve the Hard Problem; self-referential closure offers a deflationary reframing of why the explanatory gap *appears* — as an artifact of the modelling perspective — rather than a dissolution of it. FMT proposes self-referential closure as constitutive of phenomenal character: a foundational commitment of the kind every theory of consciousness must make (cf. Seth & Bayne, 2022), though that parity does not by itself vindicate it. The warrant for the posit is the empirical specificity it buys, assessed below.

Architecture alone is insufficient. FMT imposes a dual threshold: the substrate must operate near dynamical criticality, AND must instantiate the four-model self-referential architecture. Near-criticality here denotes the regime indexed by power-law neuronal avalanches, a branching ratio near unity, and long-range temporal correlations; FMT additionally motivates this regime, via Wolfram's edge-of-chaos heuristic, as the dynamical setting in which a substrate can sustain rich self-referential computation. (These Class 4 / edge-of-chaos / computational-universality identifications are heuristic motivations, not proven equivalences — they concern a dynamical regime, not formal computability.) Criticality is necessary for the computation but does not by itself produce consciousness: a system can meet the criticality condition without instantiating the architecture, in which case the architecture, not the dynamics, sets the level of consciousness. This is not an unfalsifiable hedge — it predicts that recursive self-model depth, indexed by metacognitive and perturbational measures independently of criticality, should track level of consciousness, so that a near-critical system with high perturbational complexity but no self-referential signature would count against it. Recent work is consistent with criticality being load-bearing — Algom and Shriki (2026) synthesise extensive empirical and modelling evidence, Tucker, Luu and Friston (2025) ground it in excitatory–inhibitory balance, Xu et al. (2024) show sleep restores it — but this is a necessary condition shared across criticality-based frameworks, not FMT uniquely. FMT's contribution is specifying what criticality is necessary *for*.

A specification earns its keep by generating directional predictions — and at least one of FMT's diverges from the nearest rival. Because the explicit self-model is a running simulation with variable permeability to implicit processes, global increases in permeability (as under psychedelics) should *partially and specifically* restore awareness in conditions of local self-model blockage such as anosognosia. This is where FMT and predictive processing come apart: REBUS-style accounts (relaxed high-level priors) predict broad changes in inference but, lacking a dedicated self-model module, predict no such *targeted* compensation of a self-representational deficit. Other consequences are better described as consistent with FMT than unique to it: because the explicit self-model needs continuous input to hold coherent content, destabilising that input (psychedelics, sensory deprivation, meditation) should make identity content track whatever input dominates, rendering ego dissolution controllable rather than random — a pattern compatible with relaxed-prior accounts too. A fuller catalogue, including predictions for dissociative identity disorder and lucid-dream onset, appears in Gruber (2026, Sections 8.2–8.5). The load-bearing claim is the targeted-anosognosia contrast, which appears to be untested.

The implicit/explicit distinction also yields a consequence already consistent with data. Katlowitz et al. (2026) recorded hippocampal neurons during propofol anaesthesia and found active semantic parsing, syntactic category discrimination, and representational plasticity — without conscious awareness. FMT predicts exactly this: propofol suppresses explicit models (the simulation) while leaving implicit models (substrate-level knowledge) structurally intact; the hippocampus continues its implicit-world-model work in the dark. This converges with Global Neuronal Workspace, which independently expects sophisticated unconscious processing outside the broadcast, and is compatible with IIT, on which a low-integration subsystem carries little or no consciousness. The claim is not that rival theories cannot accommodate the finding, but that FMT predicts it directly as implicit-model activity.

This specification also clarifies the status of current AI systems — Seth's central concern. Large language models fail not because they lack biological matter but because they lack the architecture: no persistent implicit models, no self-referential closure, and no substrate dynamics near criticality. FMT can say precisely *what would need to change*: persistent world- and self-models, self-referential closure between them, and a substrate operating in the near-critical regime. This is not "any system computing the right function" — it is a named architecture with named dynamical constraints.

The active inference tradition itself trends in this direction. Laukkonen, Friston and Chandaria's Beautiful Loop (2025) derives self-modelling requirements from active inference — a structurally related result, though grounded in uncertainty reduction rather than simulation dynamics. That Friston's own framework generates architectural self-modelling constraints suggests the explanatory work is increasingly done by causal structure. Whether "biological causal powers" reduce to "the causal powers of a self-referential architecture near criticality" is exactly what is unsettled — and Seth's strongest reading locates those powers in *living* self-maintenance, which an architectural account does not obviously capture. FMT's value is not to assume the reduction but to make it empirically testable: the targeted-anosognosia contrast, and the explicit-self-model / explicit-world-model dissociations catalogued in Gruber (2026), are the kind of specification both frameworks call for, and they are where the question gets settled.

Seth is right that the specification problem is the bottleneck. The productive disagreement is not whether specification matters but what form it should take. FMT offers one candidate — substrate-neutral, architecturally specific, and empirically constrained by directional predictions, some already consistent with recent data (Katlowitz et al., 2026; the criticality literature) and others as yet untested. Whether it is the right candidate is an empirical question, and the predictions are on the table.
