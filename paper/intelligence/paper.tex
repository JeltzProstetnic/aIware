\begin{abstract}
Intelligence research has produced a paradox: the field widely acknowledges that motivation influences cognitive development, yet virtually every major model of intelligence formally excludes it. The Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) taxonomy, the dominant psychometric framework, contains no motivational component. Cattell's own investment theory treats motivation as an external condition rather than a constitutive element. This paper argues that this exclusion is not merely an oversight but a systematic blind spot that distorts our understanding of intelligence in three specific ways. First, it mischaracterizes intelligence as a static trait rather than a recursive, self-reinforcing system in which knowledge, cognitive performance, and motivation form a closed amplification loop. Second, it renders invisible the role of operational knowledge---\hspace{0pt}learning strategies, logical tools, and strategic thinking---\hspace{0pt}which functions as the primary multiplier within this loop. Third, it leaves the field unable to explain why current artificial intelligence systems, which possess vast knowledge and computational performance but no intrinsic motivation, fail to exhibit the self-directed development that characterizes human intelligence. The paper further argues that motivation is not the family of independent constructs (need for cognition, typical intellectual engagement, openness, need for achievement) that the personality-intelligence literature treats it as, but a single evaluative function of the brain's explicit processing that appears fragmented because different measurement instruments observe it in different contexts---\hspace{0pt}an interpretation supported by Brunswik symmetry analysis, which predicts that the modest motivation-intelligence correlations reported in the literature are measurement artifacts rather than evidence of weak association. This paper proposes a three-component recursive model (Knowledge × Performance × Motivation) and argues that intelligence is best understood not as a capacity but as a \emph{learning ability}---\hspace{0pt}one whose trajectory is determined by the dynamics of this recursive loop rather than by any single component measured in isolation.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} intelligence, motivation, recursive systems, operational knowledge, artificial intelligence, CHC theory, Cattell investment theory
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction: A Curious Omission}

Consider two children with identical IQ scores at age six. By age thirty, one has become a research scientist, the other has not opened a book since leaving school. Standard psychometric models of intelligence struggle to account for this divergence. The six-year-old's IQ score, which captured cognitive performance under controlled test conditions, told us almost nothing about the trajectory of intellectual development that would follow. Something was missing from the measurement---\hspace{0pt}and, I argue, from the models themselves.

The missing variable is not mysterious. Every teacher knows it. Every parent has observed it. Every employer screens for it. Motivation---\hspace{0pt}the sustained drive to learn, to understand, to act on one's environment---\hspace{0pt}is universally acknowledged as critical to cognitive achievement. The empirical literature on motivation and academic performance is vast \citep{DeciRyan2000,Dweck2006,WigfieldEccles2000}. Meta-analyses consistently find reciprocal relationships between motivation and achievement that strengthen over developmental time (\citealp[for a recent multilevel meta-analysis, see][]{Huang2024}).

And yet, motivation does not appear in the formal structure of any major intelligence model.

The Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) taxonomy---\hspace{0pt}the most widely used framework in intelligence research---\hspace{0pt}is a hierarchical arrangement of cognitive abilities beneath a general factor \emph{g}, descending through broad stratum abilities (fluid reasoning, comprehension-knowledge, short-term memory, processing speed, and others) to narrow abilities \citep{McGrew2009,SchneiderMcGrew2018}. It is an impressive edifice. It contains no motivational component whatsoever.

\citeauthor{Cattell1971}'s \citeyearpar{Cattell1971} own investment theory, which proposed that fluid intelligence (Gf) is ``invested'' into crystallized intelligence (Gc) through learning, comes closer to acknowledging the role of motivation---\hspace{0pt}after all, investment requires an investor, and investors require reasons to invest. But Cattell treated motivation as an \emph{external condition} that modulates the investment process, not as a constitutive part of intelligence itself. In his framework, motivation is to intelligence what temperature is to a chemical reaction: it affects the rate, but it is not part of the reaction.

This paper argues that this analogy is wrong. Motivation is not external to intelligence; it is a constitutive component of a recursive system. Excluding it is not a harmless simplification---\hspace{0pt}it produces a systematically distorted picture that fails to capture the self-reinforcing dynamics that define intellectual development across the lifespan.

The argument proceeds in six steps. Section~2 surveys major intelligence models and documents the systematic exclusion of motivation. Section~3 presents a recursive three-component model in which knowledge, cognitive performance, and motivation form a closed amplification loop. Section~4 examines the special role of \emph{operational knowledge}---\hspace{0pt}the knowledge about how to learn---\hspace{0pt}which functions as the primary multiplier within this loop. Section~5 explores what the recursive model implies about artificial intelligence systems that lack motivation. Section~6 draws out what I consider the most consequential implication of the framework: that intelligence is, to a large extent, learnable---\hspace{0pt}and that educational practices which fail to recognize this are actively undermining cognitive development. Section~7 discusses limitations and avenues for empirical testing.


\section{The Status Quo: How Intelligence Models Treat Motivation}

\subsection{The CHC Taxonomy}

The Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) model is the ``standard reference point'' for intelligence research \citep[p.~1]{McGrew2009}. It arranges cognitive abilities into a three-stratum hierarchy: a general factor \emph{g} at the apex, broad abilities at stratum II (including fluid reasoning Gf, comprehension-knowledge Gc, short-term memory Gsm, processing speed Gs, long-term storage and retrieval Glr, visual processing Gv, auditory processing Ga, and others), and narrow abilities at stratum I.

This model is explicitly cognitive. It measures what the brain can do on demand, under standardized conditions, with maximum effort assumed. Motivational variation is treated as measurement noise---\hspace{0pt}something to be minimized through standardized test administration, not modeled as a structural feature of intelligence \citep{SchneiderMcGrew2018}. The implicit assumption is that motivation is a confound, not a component. The CHC framework's internal coherence has also been questioned on its own terms. \citet{CanivezYoungstrom2019} documented that Carroll and Horn held irreconcilable positions on the status of \emph{g}, that confirmatory factor analyses of CHC-based test batteries show poor fit, and that the broad stratum abilities provide weak incremental validity beyond \emph{g}. These internal difficulties suggest that the CHC taxonomy may be less settled than its widespread adoption implies---\hspace{0pt}and that the systematic exclusion of motivational variables may be contributing to its interpretive limitations.

\subsection{Cattell's Investment Theory}

\citet{Cattell1971} proposed that Gc develops from the ``investment'' of Gf in learning experiences. This was a significant conceptual advance: it introduced a dynamic, developmental dimension to intelligence that the purely psychometric models lacked. Gf represents the biological substrate---\hspace{0pt}the raw computational power of the brain, heavily influenced by genetics, peaking in early adulthood and declining thereafter. Gc represents the accumulated product of applying that power to the world---\hspace{0pt}knowledge, skills, cultural repertoire---\hspace{0pt}which continues to grow throughout life.

But who decides what to invest in? Cattell's theory requires an agent who allocates Gf to particular learning domains. That agent must be motivated---\hspace{0pt}must find some domains more interesting, more rewarding, or more useful than others. Without motivation, the ``investment'' metaphor collapses: one cannot invest without the intention to invest.

Cattell was aware of this. He discussed personality traits---\hspace{0pt}particularly what he called ``investment traits''---\hspace{0pt}as modulators of the Gf-to-Gc pathway (\citealp[see also][on the relationship between investment traits and intelligence]{Ziegler2012}). But he kept these firmly outside the intelligence construct. The result is an investment theory without an investor.

\subsection{Sternberg's Triarchic Theory}

\citeauthor{Sternberg1985}'s \citeyearpar{Sternberg1985} triarchic theory expanded the scope of intelligence beyond the psychometric tradition by proposing three aspects: analytical (similar to traditional IQ), creative (the ability to deal with novel situations), and practical (the ability to adapt to, shape, and select environments). This was a deliberate attempt to broaden the construct beyond what IQ tests measure.

Practical intelligence---\hspace{0pt}knowing how to get things done in real-world contexts---\hspace{0pt}comes close to the territory of motivation, since effectively shaping one's environment requires the drive to do so. But Sternberg framed practical intelligence as a \emph{competence}, not a \emph{drive}. You can know how to navigate a social environment without being motivated to do so. The triarchic theory, like CHC, treats motivation as logically separate from ability.

\subsection{Wechsler's Unfulfilled Call}

David Wechsler, whose intelligence scales remain the most widely administered in the world, was remarkably explicit about this problem. He defined intelligence as ``the global capacity of a person to act purposefully, to think rationally, and to deal effectively with his environment'' \citep[p.~3]{Wechsler1944}. As early as 1943, he had made the argument explicit: ``We cannot expect to measure total intelligence until our tests also include some measures of the non-intellective factors''---\hspace{0pt}identifying persistence, curiosity, and goal orientation as essential components of intelligent behavior \citep{Wechsler1943}.

The field ignored him. The modern Wechsler scales (WAIS, WISC) remain purely cognitive instruments. Wechsler's own tests do not measure the non-intellective factors he argued were essential. This is perhaps the clearest illustration of the blind spot described here: the founder of the world's most widely used intelligence test explicitly called for the inclusion of motivational factors, and the field proceeded as if he had not spoken.

\subsection{Gardner's Multiple Intelligences}

\citet{Gardner1983} proposed eight (later nine) relatively independent intelligences: linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalist, and (tentatively) existential. While intrapersonal intelligence---\hspace{0pt}the capacity for self-understanding---\hspace{0pt}includes awareness of one's own motivational states, Gardner did not identify motivation itself as an intelligence or as a structural component of his framework. The model was criticized extensively for failing to demonstrate that its proposed intelligences are truly independent rather than facets of \emph{g} \citep{Waterhouse2006}, but the absence of motivation was rarely noted as a problem.

\subsection{The General Pattern}

Across all major models, the pattern is the same: motivation is acknowledged as important, treated as correlated with intelligence, and then excluded from the formal model. The reasons are methodological: intelligence tests measure \emph{maximum performance} (what a person \emph{can} do, not what they \emph{will} do; \citealp{Cronbach1949}), factor-analytic models are built from cognitive test scores in which motivation does not appear, and disciplinary boundaries keep the motivation and intelligence literatures separate (\citealp[for exceptions, see][]{Murayama2013,CredeKuncel2008}). \citet{CarrDweck2019}, in their Cambridge Handbook chapter on ``Intelligence and Motivation,'' exemplify this pattern: they review extensive evidence of interaction between the two constructs but frame them as ontologically separate, never questioning whether the separation itself is the problem.

The problem runs deeper than disciplinary boundaries. Intelligence testing paradigms are specifically designed to suppress motivational variance: constrained time limits, rigid testing environments, no feedback, no opportunity for social interaction, and no access to aids create conditions under which personality and motivation have minimal room to express themselves \citep{Ackerman2018}. The measurement context is engineered to isolate ``pure'' cognitive capacity---\hspace{0pt}and then the resulting scores are used to argue that intelligence is a purely cognitive construct. The circularity is rarely noted.

These are legitimate practical considerations. But methodological convenience should not be confused with theoretical truth. The question is not whether motivation is hard to measure alongside cognitive abilities (it is), but whether a model of intelligence that excludes motivation provides an adequate account of how intelligence actually develops and operates.

I argue that it does not.


\section{Intelligence as a Recursive System}

\subsection{The Three Components}

This paper proposes that intelligence---\hspace{0pt}understood as \emph{learning ability} (Lernf\"ahigkeit)---\hspace{0pt}is constituted by three interacting components. This recursive model was first proposed in Gruber (2015, in German) and is developed here in its first systematic English-language presentation, with significant theoretical extensions and engagement with the contemporary intelligence literature. The three components are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Knowledge} (\emph{Wissen}): The accumulated content of learning. This includes both factual knowledge (what is known) and operational knowledge (how to learn, how to reason, how to strategize). It corresponds roughly, though not perfectly, to Cattell's Gc. It is acquired through upbringing, formal education, and independent learning.

\item \textbf{Performance} (\emph{Leistung}): The processing capacity of the cognitive system, including working memory capacity, processing speed, and the computational power of the neural substrate. It corresponds roughly to Cattell's Gf and to \citeauthor{Frank1959}'s \citeyearpar{Frank1959} short-term memory capacity C = S × D (where S is processing speed in bits/second and D is memory span in seconds). It is influenced by genetics and by training.

\item \textbf{Motivation}: The sustained drive to engage with the world in ways that produce learning. At the intuitive level, two aspects are readily distinguished: \emph{Wissensdrang} (thirst for knowledge)---\hspace{0pt}the drive to understand, to learn, to make sense of the world---\hspace{0pt}and \emph{Handlungsdrang} (urge to act)---\hspace{0pt}the drive to apply knowledge, to experiment, to explore. These correspond to familiar constructs in the personality-intelligence literature: Wissensdrang maps closely to \citeauthor{CacioppioPetty1982}'s \citeyearpar{CacioppioPetty1982} need for cognition (NFC; \citealp[see also][for the dispositional characterization]{Cacioppo1996}) and \citeauthor{GoffAckerman1992}'s \citeyearpar{GoffAckerman1992} typical intellectual engagement (TIE); Handlungsdrang maps to the exploration and risk-taking dispositions that \citet{WittmannHattrup2004} showed mediate intelligence-performance relationships by generating new learning opportunities.

However, the relationship between these intuitive categories and their empirical proxies is more complex than a simple mapping suggests---\hspace{0pt}and understanding why reveals something important about the motivation construct itself. NFC and TIE, despite being highly correlated ($r \approx .78$--$.87$; \citealp{vonStummAckerman2013}), predict different intelligence facets: NFC correlates primarily with fluid reasoning (Gf), while TIE correlates primarily with crystallized knowledge (Gc). Need for achievement, openness to experience, and sensation seeking show yet other patterns \citep{Ackerman2018}. The standard interpretation is that these represent genuinely distinct motivational constructs. This paper proposes an alternative: they are \emph{measurement-context projections} of a single underlying function.

The argument, which draws on the architectural framework developed in \citet{Gruber2026}, is as follows. Motivation---\hspace{0pt}the evaluative process that assigns salience, directs attention, and sustains engagement---\hspace{0pt}is a function of the brain's explicit processing: the self-model that consciously evaluates situations, weighs options, and decides where to invest effort. Ordinary usage of ``motivation'' reaches into subconscious drive territory as well---\hspace{0pt}affective valuations, automatic approach/avoidance, drives the subject cannot articulate. The construct used here is deliberately narrower: the conscious, symbolizable evaluative layer. The substrate-level optimization that underlies it---\hspace{0pt}what the companion theory of consciousness terms \emph{will} \citep[\S4.2.2]{Gruber2026}---\hspace{0pt}is real and constitutive of behavior, but it is not what the recursive loop's multiplier measures. This is a unified computational function, not a family of independent traits. It \emph{appears} fragmented in the empirical literature because different measurement instruments observe this function in different contexts. NFC measures motivation-as-observed-during-novel-reasoning, which naturally co-varies with the processing substrate that novel reasoning depends on (Gf). TIE measures motivation-as-observed-during-habitual-knowledge-seeking, which naturally co-varies with the accumulated knowledge store (Gc). Risk-taking measures motivation-as-observed-during-exploration, which naturally generates new learning opportunities. The constructs diverge not because the underlying motivation differs, but because the measurement context determines which intelligence facet the observation co-activates.

This interpretation gains support from an unexpected direction: artificial intelligence. Current large language models possess vast knowledge and high processing performance, yet exhibit no motivation whatsoever---\hspace{0pt}no curiosity, no self-directed learning, no drive to explore gaps in their understanding. The recursive model predicts that this absence should prevent the self-sustaining developmental loop that characterizes human intelligence, and this is precisely what is observed (Section~5). Critically, the \emph{kind} of motivation that AI lacks is not NFC, or TIE, or need for achievement specifically---\hspace{0pt}it is the entire evaluative function that these constructs partially measure. No amount of engineering NFC-like behavior (preference for complex problems) or TIE-like behavior (habitual engagement with information) would produce genuine motivation without the underlying explicit self-model that generates it. The AI case thus supports the unity claim: what is missing in artificial systems is not one of psychology's motivation constructs but the single computational function from which all of them derive.

This reinterpretation has a direct precedent in Wittmann's own Brunswik symmetry framework \citep{Wittmann1988,WittmannKlumb2006}: observed correlations are bounded by the symmetry between predictor and criterion bandwidth. Narrow motivation measures (a single questionnaire scale) correlated with broad intelligence criteria (a full test battery) will produce attenuated and criterion-specific correlations---\hspace{0pt}not because motivation is weakly related to intelligence, but because the measurement design is asymmetric. Properly aggregated motivation measures, assessed across multiple contexts and occasions, should produce substantially higher and more uniform correlations with intelligence---\hspace{0pt}a testable prediction of the present framework. A further attenuation arises from temporal reliability: motivation, unlike a fixed cognitive capacity, varies across situations and time. A single-occasion questionnaire captures a snapshot of a fluctuating state, and the resulting low reliability mechanically suppresses the observed correlation---\hspace{0pt}an artifact of temporal sampling, distinct from and compounding the bandwidth asymmetry.

The recursive model therefore treats Motivation as a single component with two functional expressions---\hspace{0pt}the epistemic (Wissensdrang: directing engagement toward understanding) and the agentic (Handlungsdrang: directing engagement toward action and exploration)---\hspace{0pt}while maintaining that these expressions reflect a unified evaluative process rather than structurally independent traits. What the personality-intelligence literature has catalogued as distinct constructs (NFC, TIE, openness, need for achievement, risk-taking) are, in this view, context-specific behavioral signatures of the same underlying function, measured at different points of its expression.

The three components are functionally separable but not mechanistically independent, and the model should say so rather than let the notation imply otherwise. Knowledge, Performance and Motivation are distinguished by what each contributes to the loop---\hspace{0pt}stored content, processing capacity, and the evaluative direction of engagement---\hspace{0pt}not by running on disjoint machinery. The capacity to construct a model and run it detached from present input is drawn on by both Performance and Motivation: it is what reasoning with unfamiliar material requires, and it is also what allows a person to represent a state of affairs that does not obtain and be moved by it, which is what sustained engagement toward a distant goal consists in. The architectural account of that capacity is given in \citet{Gruber2026}. For a measurement model this shared dependence is a constraint rather than a defect. It predicts that the three components will not be found orthogonal, that attempts to purify them will strip out variance the loop actually uses, and that a decomposition succeeding in making them independent would be measuring something other than the system described here.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Recursive Loop}

The recursive loop described here requires a specific cognitive capacity: cognitive learning---\hspace{0pt}the induction of general theories from particular observations---\hspace{0pt}as distinct from reinforcement learning \citep{Gruber2015}. The distinction between cognitive learning and reinforcement learning, and the specific neural architecture that enables the former, is developed in \citet{Gruber2015} and formalized in \citet{Gruber2026}. What matters here is the implication: the recursive intelligence loop can only self-sustain in systems capable of cognitive learning, which in turn requires the explicit self-modeling that consciousness provides.

The critical claim of this framework is that these three components are not merely additive; they form a \emph{closed recursive loop} in which each component amplifies the others:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Knowledge enhances Performance}: Learning strategies, logical tools, and strategic thinking (operational knowledge) directly improve the efficiency of cognitive processing. A chess player who has learned heuristics can process positions faster than one who relies on brute-force search. A reader who has learned phonemic decoding processes text more fluently, freeing working memory for comprehension.

\item \textbf{Performance enhances Knowledge}: Greater cognitive processing capacity enables faster and deeper learning. Higher working memory capacity allows the learner to hold more information in mind simultaneously, facilitating the formation of connections and the extraction of patterns.

\item \textbf{Motivation enhances both Knowledge and Performance}: The motivated learner seeks out learning opportunities (expanding Knowledge) and practices cognitive skills (training Performance). Crucially, motivation sustains engagement over time, which is essential for the recursive loop to iterate.

\item \textbf{Knowledge and Performance enhance Motivation}: Success in learning and problem-solving generates positive affect and self-efficacy \citep{Bandura1997}, which in turn sustains motivation. This is the mechanism behind the Matthew effect: early success breeds the motivation that produces further success \citep{Stanovich1986}.
\end{itemize}

This recursive structure produces the self-reinforcing dynamics that are empirically well-documented but theoretically under-explained. The Matthew effect in reading \citep{Stanovich1986}---\hspace{0pt}whereby children who read well read more, thereby becoming even better readers---\hspace{0pt}is a specific instance of this general recursive loop. The divergence of Gf and Gc across the lifespan---\hspace{0pt}Gf declining from early adulthood while Gc continues to grow---\hspace{0pt}is explained by the recursive model as the growing dominance of the Knowledge and Motivation legs as the Performance leg (biological processing capacity) begins to decline.

\subsection{Why This Is Not Simply "Motivation Matters"}

The claim here is stronger than the trivial observation that motivation influences learning outcomes. This paper advances a specific structural claim: that intelligence is a \emph{system} whose behavior is determined by the recursive interaction of three components, and that removing any one component from the model produces qualitatively wrong predictions about the system's dynamics.

Consider the prediction that intelligence should be a self-reinforcing process. Standard psychometric models, which treat intelligence as a relatively stable trait (albeit one that changes somewhat over the lifespan), have difficulty explaining why some individuals show dramatically increasing intellectual capability over decades while others plateau early. The recursive model predicts exactly this: small initial differences in any of the three components---\hspace{0pt}even in motivation alone---\hspace{0pt}should compound over time, producing the wide variance in adult intellectual achievement observed empirically.

This is not merely a statistical amplification of initial differences (as in a simple fan-spread model). It is a qualitatively different dynamic: a positive feedback loop that can be entered at any point. A child with modest initial cognitive ability but high motivation and good operational knowledge (learning strategies) can, through the recursive loop, develop intellectual capabilities far beyond what their initial ``IQ'' would predict. Conversely, a child with high initial cognitive ability but low motivation or poor learning strategies may stagnate. Both patterns are widely observed empirically but poorly explained by models that treat intelligence as a primarily cognitive, primarily stable construct.

Population-level evidence supports this process view. \citet{BratsbergRogeberg2018}, analyzing Norwegian military conscript data, demonstrated that IQ scores rose and then declined across birth cohorts \emph{within families}---\hspace{0pt}ruling out genetic explanations and confirming environmental causation. The Flynn effect reversal, now documented across multiple countries, is incompatible with models that treat intelligence as a primarily biological trait. It is, however, precisely what a recursive model predicts: when environmental conditions that support the loop (educational quality, intellectual engagement) degrade, the loop weakens at the population level.

The structural claim also carries a measurement commitment that distinguishes it from the weaker one. If motivation is constitutive of the loop rather than a correlate of its output, it must be visible \emph{within} a person across occasions, not only as a between-person trait correlation. \citet{Brose2010} provide that observation directly: in the COGITO study, daily fluctuations in effort and enjoyment co-varied with same-day working memory performance within individuals. The effect is not universal---\hspace{0pt}it was heterogeneous across participants and weak or absent among older adults---\hspace{0pt}and the recursive model does not require it to be, since a component embedded in an interacting system will not behave as a uniform main effect.

Even more striking is the dissociation reported by \citet{GignacZajenkowski2024}: IQ scores rose while \emph{g}---\hspace{0pt}the general factor extracted from the correlation matrix---\hspace{0pt}simultaneously declined. This dissociation is inexplicable under static-trait models but directly predicted by the recursive framework. Teaching to the test inflates Performance scores (narrow task-specific knowledge) without engaging the recursive loop. The result is higher scores on standardized measures with lower capacity for the self-directed, generalizable learning that the recursive loop produces. IQ goes up; intelligence, properly understood, goes down.

\subsection{Relation to Existing Work}

Several researchers have proposed models that move in this direction, though none, to my knowledge, has formalized the full recursive structure.

\citeauthor{Sternberg2019}'s \citeyearpar{Sternberg2019} concept of ``adaptive intelligence'' emphasizes the role of goals and purpose in intelligent behavior. More recently, \citet{Sternberg2021} proposed the concept of ``meta-intelligence''---\hspace{0pt}intelligence that operates on itself, recursively improving its own functioning. This is structurally identical to the recursive loop proposed here, though Sternberg and colleagues do not formally integrate motivation into the recursive structure or specify the Knowledge-Performance-Motivation triad as its components. \citeauthor{Dweck2006}'s \citeyearpar{Dweck2006} work on mindset shows that beliefs about the malleability of intelligence affect effort investment and, through effort, actual cognitive development---\hspace{0pt}a finding that only makes sense if motivation is constitutive of the intelligence development process, not merely correlated with it.

The ``investment traits'' literature \citep{Ziegler2012,vonStummAckerman2013} has documented that personality traits related to intellectual engagement---\hspace{0pt}curiosity, openness to experience, need for cognition---\hspace{0pt}predict intellectual development beyond what cognitive ability alone predicts. \Citet{vonStummChamorroPremuzic2011} showed in a meta-analysis that ``hungry minds''---\hspace{0pt}individuals high in intellectual curiosity---\hspace{0pt}develop greater knowledge and skill over time. These findings are consistent with the recursive model: investment traits are proxies for the motivation component, and their predictive power for long-term intellectual development is exactly what the recursive model predicts.

\citet{Murayama2013} demonstrated that intrinsic motivation predicted mathematics achievement growth over several years, even controlling for prior achievement and intelligence---\hspace{0pt}direct evidence that motivation contributes to intellectual development beyond cognitive ability. The transmission of motivational states across educational contexts \citep{Schiefele2017} and the meta-analytic findings of \citet{Huang2024} document the bidirectional relationship between motivation and achievement that the recursive model formalizes.

Several other frameworks approach the territory of the recursive model without completing the integration. \citeauthor{Ackerman1996}'s (\citeyear{Ackerman1996}, \citeyear{Ackerman2018}) PPIK theory (Process, Personality, Interests, Knowledge) comes closest to the present proposal: it explicitly models how personality traits and interests direct the Gf-to-Gc investment process, and its empirical program has demonstrated that these non-cognitive factors predict intellectual development beyond cognitive ability alone. The recursive model can be understood as extending PPIK by treating motivation not as a moderator of the investment process but as a constitutive component of the intelligence construct itself---\hspace{0pt}a step that changes the formal structure of the model rather than merely adding predictors to it. \citet{WittmannSuss1999}, working within the PPIK framework, demonstrated empirically that motivation's effect on complex performance is largely indirect---\hspace{0pt}mediated through knowledge rather than acting directly on performance. Their path-analytic model, which explained approximately 50\% of variance in dynamic task performance, showed intelligence-as-knowledge as the strongest direct predictor, with motivational variables contributing primarily via knowledge acquisition. This is precisely the M → K → Performance pathway that the recursive model formalizes. \citet{WittmannHattrup2004} further showed that risk-taking---\hspace{0pt}a motivational disposition---\hspace{0pt}mediates the relationship between intelligence and performance in dynamic systems by generating new learning opportunities, providing empirical evidence for the exploration-exploitation dynamic that the recursive loop predicts. Similarly, \citeauthor{Duckworth2007}'s \citeyearpar{Duckworth2007} ``grit'' and \citeauthor{Stanovich2016}'s \citeyearpar{Stanovich2016} Rationality Quotient each capture what IQ misses---\hspace{0pt}including the drive to engage effortful processing---\hspace{0pt}but frame these as separate constructs rather than constitutive components of intelligence. \citeauthor{Snow1996}'s \citeyearpar{Snow1996} cognitive-conative-affective framework acknowledged the interdependence of cognition and motivation in learning, but remained in educational psychology and was never integrated into mainstream intelligence theory. Notably, \citeauthor{Ackerman2018}'s own empirical work on trait complexes points toward the recursive model's conclusion without reaching it: personality traits, he finds, ``are less influential as independent predictors of learning and knowledge'' but ``in conjunction with other trait families, jointly influence the outcomes'' \citep[p.~9]{Ackerman2018}. This is an empirical observation of exactly the system-level dynamics the recursive model formalizes---\hspace{0pt}the components are not additive predictors but interacting elements of a recursive system.

The dynamic systems approach provides deeper theoretical support. \Citet{vanGeert2020} argues that constructs like intelligence must be understood as ``temporary process stabilities'' rather than fixed traits; \citet{BalboniNaglieri2021} argue that intelligence is an emergent property of person-task-situation interaction. What the present framework adds to these process accounts is specificity: it identifies the three components (Knowledge, Performance, Motivation), their recursive interaction, and the temporal dynamics by which these interactions compound over the lifespan.

What is missing from these individual contributions is the integration: a single framework that specifies (a) the three necessary components, (b) their recursive interaction, and (c) the system-level consequences of the recursion, including the Matthew effect, the Gf-Gc divergence, and the educational and AI implications discussed below. The mechanism that enables cognitive learning---\hspace{0pt}third-person perspective simulation via explicit self-modeling---\hspace{0pt}is detailed in a companion paper on consciousness \citep{Gruber2026}, which argues that consciousness is constituted by four interacting models arranged along two axes (scope and mode) and that the resulting architecture enables a qualitatively different mode of learning than any non-conscious system can achieve.


\section{Operational Knowledge: The Hidden Multiplier}

\subsection{What Operational Knowledge Is}

Within the Knowledge component, two categories are distinguished:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Factual knowledge}: Knowledge of content---\hspace{0pt}facts, concepts, procedures, cultural repertoire. This is what IQ tests (and educational systems) primarily measure under the rubric of ``crystallized intelligence.''

\item \textbf{Operational knowledge} (\emph{Metawissen}): Knowledge about \emph{how to learn and think}---\hspace{0pt}learning strategies, reasoning heuristics, metacognitive skills, strategic planning, logical tools, and the ability to evaluate one's own understanding. This is sometimes subsumed under the label ``metacognition'' \citep{Flavell1979} or ``self-regulated learning'' \citep{Zimmerman2002}, but these labels do not fully capture the concept, which extends to include general-purpose reasoning strategies and logical tools that are not domain-specific.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why Operational Knowledge Is the Multiplier}

Operational knowledge occupies a special position in the recursive loop because it amplifies the \emph{rate} of knowledge acquisition. Factual knowledge is additive: learning a new fact adds one fact to the store. Operational knowledge is multiplicative: learning a new learning strategy improves the efficiency of all subsequent learning.

Consider a concrete example. A student who learns the technique of \emph{spaced repetition} (distributing practice over time rather than massing it) does not merely acquire one new fact. She acquires a tool that increases the retention rate of all subsequent learning. This is a fundamentally different kind of knowledge---\hspace{0pt}it is knowledge that accelerates the recursive loop itself.

The intelligence literature has noted the existence of metacognitive skills and learning strategies. But it has not applied this insight. The distinction between factual and operational knowledge is noted and then set aside---\hspace{0pt}treated as a curiosity rather than as the structurally decisive feature it is. In the present framework, operational knowledge is the transmission gear of the recursive loop: it determines how efficiently the turning of one component (Motivation → effort) translates into the turning of another (Knowledge → capability).

\subsection{Implications for the AI Age}

This distinction becomes acutely consequential in the age of artificial intelligence. When factual knowledge is instantly available to anyone with an internet connection, and when computational performance is available for the cost of an API call, the relative importance of the three components shifts dramatically.

Factual knowledge, which educational systems have traditionally spent most of their time transmitting, is no longer scarce. Performance, in the narrow sense of computational processing, is no longer exclusively biological. What remains uniquely human---\hspace{0pt}and uniquely valuable---\hspace{0pt}is the combination of intrinsic motivation and operational knowledge: the drive to learn \emph{and} the meta-skill of knowing how to learn effectively.

If this analysis is correct, it implies that the most valuable thing an educational system can transmit is not factual knowledge but operational knowledge---\hspace{0pt}the strategies, heuristics, and metacognitive skills that allow a learner to learn independently. In the AI age, \emph{learning how to learn} is close to the only thing still worth teaching.


\section{The AI Implication: What Machines Are Missing}

The recursive model makes a specific prediction about artificial intelligence systems: they should exhibit a characteristic failure mode in which high Knowledge and high Performance do not produce the self-directed development that characterizes human intelligence.

Current artificial intelligence systems provide an unintended but remarkably clean test case for the recursive model---\hspace{0pt}one that allows us to examine, facet by facet, what happens when Knowledge and Performance are scaled to superhuman levels while Motivation remains entirely absent.

\subsection{A Facet-by-Facet Comparison}

Consider the major facets of the CHC taxonomy and how current AI systems perform on each:

\textbf{Memory and retrieval.} Large language models (LLMs) trained on trillions of tokens have access to a store of factual knowledge that dwarfs any individual human's. In terms of sheer breadth of stored information, AI is unambiguously superior. This corresponds to the Knowledge component of the recursive model---\hspace{0pt}and it is precisely the component that can be supplied externally, without recursion.

\textbf{Processing speed.} AI systems process information orders of magnitude faster than biological neural networks. Where a human working memory system operates at roughly 7 ± 2 chunks \citep{Miller1956} with processing cycles measured in hundreds of milliseconds, LLMs process millions of tokens per second. This corresponds to the Performance component---\hspace{0pt}and again, it can be engineered without recursion.

\textbf{Reasoning.} This is where the comparison becomes interesting. The most recent generation of ``reasoning models'' (e.g., OpenAI's o1 and o3 series, released 2024--2025) achieve performance on mathematical competition problems, graduate-level science exams, and complex logical tasks that would have been considered impossible for AI just two years ago. Yet these systems exhibit systematic failures that are structurally revealing. They ``hallucinate''---\hspace{0pt}generating confident but false outputs that superficially resemble correct reasoning. These hallucinations bear a striking resemblance to what \citet{Kahneman2011} described as System~1 errors: fast, associative processing that produces plausible-sounding but incorrect results. Human intelligence addresses such errors through System~2---\hspace{0pt}slow, deliberate, effortful reasoning that monitors and corrects System~1 outputs. But System~2 is itself driven by motivation: the willingness to invest cognitive effort in checking one's own outputs, to tolerate the discomfort of uncertainty, to persist in reasoning when the easy answer is available. \citet{Gigerenzer2007} has argued that the boundary between ``error'' and ``adaptive heuristic'' depends on the ecology in which the reasoning occurs---\hspace{0pt}a point that underscores the role of the agent's goals and motivations in determining what counts as intelligent behavior in context. It is worth noting that the ``reasoning'' in reasoning models is not a structural innovation but a quantitative one: these systems use the same transformer architecture as their predecessors, trained via reinforcement learning to produce longer chain-of-thought sequences before generating a final answer. More computation per response---\hspace{0pt}more tokens of intermediate ``thinking''---\hspace{0pt}but no qualitative change in the underlying neural architecture, though the inference procedure is significantly modified. The improvement is real but reveals its nature under pressure: the system allocates more processing to a problem, not different processing. AI systems can also be engineered to ``check their work'' through chain-of-thought prompting and verification loops, but these are externally imposed scaffolds---\hspace{0pt}surrogate motivation supplied by the human designer, not endogenous drive arising from the system itself.

\textbf{Motivation.} Here the comparison is not one of degree but of kind. AI systems have no motivation whatsoever. They have no intrinsic drive to learn, no curiosity, no goals of their own. They process the input they are given and produce the output they are asked for. Between queries, they do nothing. They do not seek out new information. They do not practice skills. They do not wonder about problems. They do not experience the satisfaction of understanding or the discomfort of ignorance. The absence is total.

\subsection{The Predicted Failure Mode}

According to the recursive model, this absence of motivation should prevent the recursive loop from self-sustaining---\hspace{0pt}and this is precisely what is observed. LLMs do not improve themselves between training runs. They do not independently seek out areas of ignorance and address them. They do not show the progressive intellectual development over time that characterizes human intelligence. Their ``intelligence'' (to the extent the term applies) is static---\hspace{0pt}entirely determined by their training, with no endogenous drive to extend it.

The reasoning models sharpen this point. These systems solve competition-level problems when prompted but do not independently seek out problems, do not self-direct their learning, and require external scaffolding---\hspace{0pt}prompts, reinforcement learning from human feedback, reward signals---\hspace{0pt}that functions as a surrogate for the absent motivation component. Scaling Performance and Knowledge to extraordinary levels produces extraordinary outputs on demand, but not the self-sustaining developmental trajectory that defines intelligence.

The absence of operational knowledge integration provides a further illustration. A human learner who discovers a more effective reasoning strategy can immediately apply it to subsequent problems within the same cognitive session---\hspace{0pt}the operational knowledge becomes part of the active processing. Current LLMs cannot do this. External scaffolding systems can modify the instructions given to an LLM (analogous to updating a set of rules the system follows), but such modifications do not integrate into the system's processing in the way that learned strategies integrate into human cognition. The system must be restarted with new instructions, at which point it is, in a meaningful sense, a new system---\hspace{0pt}one that has no continuity of experience with its predecessor. This is the opposite of the recursive loop, in which operational knowledge acquired through motivated engagement continuously reshapes the cognitive process that produced it.

\subsection{Why Engineering Motivation Requires Consciousness}

As argued in Section~3.1, the motivation absent from AI systems is not any single construct from the personality-intelligence literature---\hspace{0pt}not need for cognition, not typical intellectual engagement, not need for achievement---\hspace{0pt}but the unified evaluative function from which all such constructs derive. This function, in the architectural framework of \citet{Gruber2026}, is a property of the explicit self-model: the conscious system that evaluates its own states, assigns value to outcomes, and directs behavior accordingly.

This has a specific engineering implication: motivation cannot be added to AI systems as a module or a reward function. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), which trains AI systems to produce outputs that humans rate as helpful, is not motivation---\hspace{0pt}it is an external selection pressure that shapes behavior without generating the endogenous evaluative process that motivation requires. The system does not \emph{want} to be helpful; it has been shaped to produce outputs that are rated as helpful. The distinction matters because endogenous motivation generates the self-directed exploration, error-correction, and sustained engagement that drive the recursive loop, while externally imposed reward shaping does not.

One might object that this is merely because LLMs are not designed to self-improve. But this objection concedes the point: designing a system that genuinely self-improves---\hspace{0pt}not merely self-optimizes within predefined parameters, but identifies its own knowledge gaps and autonomously seeks to fill them---\hspace{0pt}requires engineering a functional analogue of the explicit self-model. Until AI systems have this, they will remain tools that are used rather than agents that develop. For a detailed analysis of what architectural features consciousness requires, and why current AI systems lack them, see \citet{Gruber2026}.


\section{The Learnability of Intelligence}

\subsection{A Structural Prediction}

The recursive model yields an implication that is, in my view, more consequential than the AI case: it predicts that intelligence is, to a large extent, \emph{learnable}.

This is not a feel-good platitude. It is a structural prediction that follows directly from the composition of the model's three components. Consider each in turn:

\textbf{Knowledge is entirely learnable.} This is true by definition. Knowledge---\hspace{0pt}both factual and operational---\hspace{0pt}is the accumulated product of learning. No one is born knowing calculus or knowing how to use spaced repetition. The entire Knowledge component, including the operational knowledge that functions as the system's multiplier (Section~4), is acquired through experience, instruction, and practice.

\textbf{Motivation is substantially learnable.} This claim requires more argument, but the evidence is strong. Self-Determination Theory \citep{DeciRyan2000} demonstrates that intrinsic motivation is not a fixed trait but a response to environmental conditions---\hspace{0pt}specifically, to the satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs. Environments that support these needs cultivate intrinsic motivation; environments that thwart them extinguish it. \citeauthor{Dweck2006}'s \citeyearpar{Dweck2006} work on growth mindset shows that beliefs about the malleability of intelligence---\hspace{0pt}themselves a form of knowledge---\hspace{0pt}directly affect motivational persistence: students who believe intelligence is developable invest more effort and recover more effectively from failure. These beliefs are teachable \citep{YeagerDweck2012}. However, the empirical record is more complex than the initial enthusiasm suggested. \citet{MacnamaraBurgoyne2023}, in a comprehensive meta-analysis, found that growth mindset interventions produced negligible effects on academic achievement (\emph{d} = 0.05 after correction for publication bias), with independent researchers finding significantly smaller effects than researchers with financial ties to mindset theory. This is consistent with the recursive model: changing beliefs about intelligence (a narrow sub-component of Motivation) without simultaneously addressing Knowledge and operational Knowledge cannot restart the recursive loop. The failure of mindset interventions, far from undermining the importance of motivation, illustrates what happens when motivation is treated as an add-on rather than as a constitutive component of a recursive system. Motivation is not purely innate temperament; it is shaped, for better or worse, by the environments and feedback systems that surround the learner---\hspace{0pt}but effective intervention must engage the full loop, not merely one belief within one component.

\textbf{Performance has a biological ceiling---\hspace{0pt}but the ceiling on the measure sits above the ceiling on the substrate, and neither is usually the binding constraint.} Cognitive processing capacity is the component most strongly influenced by genetics and neurobiology. Working memory capacity, processing speed, and the raw computational power of the neural substrate are not infinitely malleable, and the heritability of fluid ability in adulthood is among the better-replicated findings in the field. Three points qualify what follows from this, and the first two dispute none of it. First, heritability describes the sources of variance in a population across the range of environments that population happened to occupy. It does not fix the standing of any individual, and it does not establish that the observed range of environments is the possible range. Second, a fluid-reasoning score is not a direct read-out of substrate capacity. Reasoning with unfamiliar material requires constructing a model of the problem that the examinee does not have stored and then running it, and model construction is something a person becomes better at. To the extent that a Gf test scores that construction alongside the substrate carrying it, measured Gf is part capacity and part skill. This reading accommodates two findings that a pure-capacity account handles awkwardly: working memory training produces gains that are reliable on trained and near tasks while far transfer remains contested (\citealp{Jaeggi2008}; though see \citealp[][for debate about transfer]{MelbyLervag2013}), which is what one expects if what improves is a constructed procedure rather than a capacity, and expertise routinely allows individuals to circumvent apparent working memory limits through chunking and automatization \citep{ChaseSimon1973}. Neither finding shows that the substrate is elastic. What they show is that the measured quantity is not only the substrate. Third---\hspace{0pt}and this is the decisive point---\hspace{0pt}for the vast majority of people, Performance is \emph{not the bottleneck}. Average cognitive processing capacity is more than sufficient to support what most people would recognize as highly intelligent behavior in real-world contexts. The bottleneck, for most people most of the time, is Knowledge and Motivation.

\subsection{Why Performance Is Not the Bottleneck}

This claim deserves elaboration, because it runs counter to the implicit assumption of the psychometric tradition---\hspace{0pt}that intelligence differences are primarily differences in cognitive processing capacity.

Consider the distribution of Gf in the general population. The difference between the 25th and 75th percentile in working memory capacity is real but modest. It means that one person can hold, roughly, one more chunk of information in mind than another. This difference matters at the extremes---\hspace{0pt}in theoretical physics, in certain forms of mathematical proof, in competitive chess at the grandmaster level. But it matters far less in the contexts where most people live their lives: in learning a profession, in solving practical problems, in understanding complex arguments, in acquiring expertise in a domain of interest.

What separates the person who becomes expert in their field from the person who remains a novice is overwhelmingly not a difference in working memory capacity. It is a difference in accumulated knowledge (including operational knowledge---\hspace{0pt}knowing how to learn effectively), sustained over time by a difference in motivation. The expert has iterated the recursive loop thousands of times; the novice stopped iterating early. The recursive model predicts that this difference in iteration count---\hspace{0pt}driven primarily by K and M, not P---\hspace{0pt}should dominate real-world outcomes, and it does.

To put it bluntly: a person of average cognitive processing capacity who is deeply motivated and who possesses strong operational knowledge will, over a lifetime, develop intellectual capabilities that far exceed those of a person with superior processing capacity but low motivation and poor learning strategies. The recursive loop is a compound interest machine, and compound interest cares more about the rate of deposit (Motivation) and the investment strategy (operational Knowledge) than about the initial principal (Performance).

A caveat is necessary here: this argument applies to the broad middle of the cognitive distribution. At the extremes---\hspace{0pt}individuals with significant cognitive impairments, or tasks requiring exceptional processing capacity (theoretical physics, grandmaster-level chess)---\hspace{0pt}Performance does become the binding constraint. The recursive model does not deny the reality of individual differences in Gf, and it does not treat them as wholly acquired. It argues that the loop amplifies K and M differences far more than P differences across the lifespan, and that some part of the P difference measured in adulthood is itself an output of that amplification rather than an input to it. For the broad middle of the distribution---\hspace{0pt}which is where most people are---\hspace{0pt}Performance is sufficient. It is K and M that determine the trajectory.

\subsection{The School Grade Disaster}

If intelligence is largely learnable, and if the recursive loop is driven primarily by Motivation and Knowledge, then a disturbing implication follows: any system that systematically destroys motivation in learners is not merely failing to develop intelligence---\hspace{0pt}it is \emph{actively suppressing} it.

I submit that conventional grading systems do exactly this.

Consider what happens when a child receives a poor grade. The grade is presented as a measurement---\hspace{0pt}an objective assessment of the child's ability or performance. But through the lens of the recursive model, the grade functions as an intervention on the Motivation component. A poor grade communicates: \emph{you are not good enough at this.} For a child who has not yet developed a robust growth mindset---\hspace{0pt}which is most children---\hspace{0pt}this message translates directly into: \emph{you are not intelligent enough.} The child's self-efficacy is damaged. Their intrinsic motivation to engage with the subject is diminished. Their willingness to invest effort---\hspace{0pt}the fuel of the recursive loop---\hspace{0pt}is reduced.

This is not speculation. Although \citeauthor{RosenthalJacobson1968}'s \citeyearpar{RosenthalJacobson1968} original Pygmalion study has been criticized on methodological grounds, the broader finding that teacher expectations influence student performance has been supported by subsequent meta-analyses \citep{Rosenthal2002,JussimHarber2005}. When teachers believed certain students were ``bloomers,'' those students showed greater intellectual gains---\hspace{0pt}not because they were inherently brighter, but because teacher expectations shaped the motivational environment. The stereotype threat literature \citep{SteeleAronson1995} has documented the same mechanism from the other direction: when individuals are made aware that they belong to a group stereotyped as intellectually inferior, their cognitive performance measurably declines---\hspace{0pt}not because of any change in ability, but because the threat consumes working memory resources and undermines task engagement.

The recursive model makes this dynamic structurally precise. A poor grade attacks M. Reduced M means fewer iterations of the loop. Fewer iterations mean slower growth in K. Slower growth in K means worse performance on subsequent assessments. Worse performance means more poor grades. The loop has been reversed: instead of a virtuous cycle of compounding growth, the child is now trapped in a vicious cycle of compounding stagnation. The grading system is not merely measuring an outcome---\hspace{0pt}it is \emph{producing} the outcome it claims to measure.

This is a self-fulfilling prophecy in the precise technical sense \citep{Merton1948}, and the recursive model explains \emph{why} it is self-fulfilling: because intelligence is a recursive system, any intervention that suppresses one component has compounding effects on all components over time.

\subsection{Compounding Effects: A Structural Prediction}

The recursive model makes a specific, testable prediction about the time course of these effects: motivation-destroying interventions should produce damage that \emph{compounds} over time, not damage that remains static. A single bad grade in first grade does not merely reduce motivation in first grade; it slightly reduces the rate at which the loop iterates, which produces a slightly smaller knowledge base by second grade, which produces slightly worse performance in second grade, which produces another discouraging signal, and so on. The recursive structure predicts that early motivational damage should be visible as an accelerating divergence from peers---\hspace{0pt}a fanning out of trajectories that grows wider with each passing year.

Conversely, the model predicts that motivation-enhancing interventions---\hspace{0pt}environments that support autonomy, competence, and relatedness \citep{DeciRyan2000}; feedback systems that emphasize growth rather than fixed ability \citep{Dweck2006}; educational practices that teach operational knowledge explicitly \citep{DignathButtner2008}---\hspace{0pt}should produce benefits that \emph{compound} over time. An intervention that boosts M in first grade should show larger effects at five-year follow-up than at one-year follow-up, because the additional loop iterations accumulate.

Some evidence supports this compounding pattern. \citeauthor{Heckman2006}'s \citeyearpar{Heckman2006} analysis of early childhood interventions, including the Perry Preschool Project, shows returns that grow over time---\hspace{0pt}larger effects at age 27 than at age 7---\hspace{0pt}not because initial cognitive gains persist (they often fade), but because motivational and self-regulatory gains compound through subsequent learning. This is precisely what a recursive model predicts and precisely what a static-trait model does not.

\subsection{Implications for Educational Practice}

If the recursive model is correct, the most effective educational interventions are not those that maximize the transmission of factual knowledge (the current default), nor those that attempt to boost raw cognitive processing capacity (which has limited malleability). The most effective interventions are those that target the two learnable components---\hspace{0pt}Knowledge (especially operational knowledge) and Motivation---\hspace{0pt}in ways designed to initiate and sustain the recursive loop.

Specifically, the model implies:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Teach operational knowledge explicitly.} Learning strategies, metacognitive skills, and reasoning heuristics should not be incidental byproducts of content instruction---\hspace{0pt}they should be a primary focus, because they function as the multiplier within the recursive loop.

\item \textbf{Protect motivation above all.} Any educational practice that systematically reduces intrinsic motivation is, from the perspective of the recursive model, directly suppressing intelligence development. This includes not only punitive grading but also ability tracking, competitive ranking, and any system that communicates to children that their intellectual capacity is fixed.

\item \textbf{Replace grades with growth feedback.} If assessment is necessary---\hspace{0pt}and it may be, for diagnostic purposes---\hspace{0pt}it should take a form that feeds the recursive loop rather than breaking it. This means feedback that is specific, actionable, and oriented toward improvement rather than toward ranking. It means communicating to learners not ``you scored 60\%'' but ``here is what you have mastered, here is what to work on next, and here is how.''

\item \textbf{Recognize that average Performance is sufficient.} Educational systems should stop treating cognitive processing capacity as the primary constraint on intellectual development. For most students, it is not. The binding constraints are motivation and operational knowledge, and both are responsive to intervention.
\end{enumerate}

None of this is entirely new. Several established educational approaches already align with the recursive model's predictions: Montessori education prioritizes autonomy and intrinsic motivation; mastery learning \citep{Bloom1968} replaces competitive grading with criterion-referenced progression that protects self-efficacy; portfolio assessment shifts the focus from summative ranking to growth documentation. What the recursive model adds is a \emph{structural explanation} for why these practices work: they target the components of the recursive loop that are both most learnable and most consequential for long-term intellectual development. The model transforms what might otherwise appear to be soft pedagogical preferences into hard predictions about the dynamics of a formal system.


\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Relation to Established Intelligence Models}

The proposal advanced here does not require abandoning existing intelligence models. The CHC taxonomy remains a useful descriptive framework for the cognitive components of intelligence. Cattell's investment theory remains a useful account of the Gf-to-Gc developmental trajectory. What this paper proposes is that these models are \emph{incomplete}---\hspace{0pt}they describe the Performance and Knowledge components of intelligence with considerable precision while systematically excluding the Motivation component that drives the developmental dynamics.

The recursive model can be understood as an extension of Cattell's investment theory that (a) makes the investor explicit, (b) formalizes the recursive feedback structure, and (c) identifies operational knowledge as the rate-limiting factor in the loop.

The recursive model also explains a structural property of intelligence that factor-analytic approaches have difficulty accommodating: intelligence behaves as a \emph{polythetic} construct \citep{Beckner1959}. In a polythetic class, members share most but not all defining properties, and no single property is necessary or sufficient for membership. Multiple K $\times$ P $\times$ M configurations can produce equifinal outcomes---high knowledge compensates for moderate performance, sustained motivation compensates for late starts, strong operational knowledge accelerates the loop regardless of starting conditions. This is why early IQ (which primarily captures Performance) is a poor predictor of adult intellectual achievement, and why factor-analytic models that impose a single underlying dimension systematically underfit the observed data.

Independent within-person evidence indicates that this is not merely a modelling preference. \citet{Schmiedek2020}, analyzing the COGITO study---\hspace{0pt}nine cognitive tasks measured on approximately 100 occasions per participant---\hspace{0pt}found that the structure of within-person daily performance differs systematically from the between-person structure of cognitive abilities, and that the general factor recovered between persons does not describe how cognition is organized within a person. That work does not test motivation and was not conducted with the present framework in view; what it establishes is that between-person factor structure cannot be assumed to serve as a surrogate for the within-person process. This matters here because the recursive loop is a within-person process. The present model adds an account of what organizes that process: the K $\times$ P $\times$ M interaction iterating across developmental time.

The capacity the model assigns to the Performance and Motivation components---\hspace{0pt}constructing a representation of something not present and running it---\hspace{0pt}is not an import into intelligence research from outside psychology. Memory research arrived at it independently. \citet{Bartlett1932} established that remembering is reconstruction rather than replay; \citeauthor{SchacterAddis2007}'s \citeyearpar{SchacterAddis2007} constructive episodic simulation hypothesis proposes that memory is reconstructive because its function is to supply recombinable elements for the simulation of events that have not occurred; and \citet{Hassabis2007} found that patients with hippocampal amnesia were markedly impaired at imagining novel experiences, their attempts lacking spatial coherence. This is consistent with the present framework rather than a test of it. What it establishes is that remembering and prospection draw on a common constructive capacity---\hspace{0pt}the capacity the recursive loop requires, and one that no current intelligence model represents.

At the neural level, \citet{Hilger2020} have shown that intelligence is associated not with a fixed brain network topology but with the brain's dynamic ability to reconfigure its network architecture in response to task demands. This neural-level finding parallels the recursive model's claim at the psychological level: intelligence is a dynamic process, not a static capacity.

\subsection{Testable Predictions}

The recursive model generates several testable predictions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Motivation predicts long-term intellectual development beyond IQ}: Measures of intellectual curiosity and learning drive, assessed in childhood, should predict adult intellectual achievement (education, creative output, problem-solving ability) beyond what childhood IQ predicts. Existing evidence supports this \citep{vonStummChamorroPremuzic2011,Murayama2013}, but more long-term longitudinal studies are needed.

\item \textbf{Operational knowledge moderates the motivation-achievement relationship}: The effect of motivation on intellectual development should be stronger in individuals who also possess effective learning strategies. Motivation without operational knowledge produces effort without efficiency; operational knowledge without motivation produces capability without deployment.

\item \textbf{The Matthew effect should be strongest where all three components co-vary}: The rich-get-richer dynamic should be most pronounced in individuals who are simultaneously high in cognitive performance, knowledge, and motivation. The model predicts that the Matthew effect is weakest when motivation is low, even if performance and knowledge are high---\hspace{0pt}because the loop lacks the driving force to iterate.

\item \textbf{Interventions targeting operational knowledge should have outsized long-term effects}: If operational knowledge is the multiplier in the recursive loop, then teaching learning strategies should produce effects that compound over time---\hspace{0pt}larger at long follow-up than at short follow-up. Some evidence supports this \citep{DignathButtner2008}, but it has not been interpreted in the context of a recursive intelligence model.

\item \textbf{AI systems will not exhibit self-directed intellectual development until they have functional motivation analogues}: This is a prediction about the future trajectory of AI development. It implies that the path to artificial general intelligence runs through motivation engineering, not merely through scaling Knowledge and Performance.

\item \textbf{Motivation-destroying educational interventions should produce compounding negative effects}: The recursive model predicts that practices such as punitive grading, ability tracking, and fixed-ability labeling should show damage that accelerates over time---\hspace{0pt}with effect sizes at 5-year follow-up significantly larger than at 1-year follow-up when the same cohort is tracked longitudinally. Conversely, motivation-enhancing interventions should show compounding benefits on the same timescale, distinguishing recursive compounding from simple persistence of initial gains.

\item \textbf{Properly aggregated motivation measures should show substantially stronger correlations with intelligence than currently reported}: The existing literature reports motivation-intelligence correlations of approximately $r = .20$--$.35$ \citep{vonStummAckerman2013}. A reviewer might reasonably ask: if the relationship is this modest, why call it a major omission? The answer lies in Brunswik symmetry \citep{Wittmann1988,WittmannKlumb2006}. The modest correlations are, in large part, measurement artifacts. Motivation is typically assessed with single questionnaire scales capturing a narrow behavioral window---\hspace{0pt}a state-level snapshot of a broad dispositional function. These narrow measures are then correlated with broad intelligence criteria (full test batteries yielding composite scores). The resulting asymmetry between predictor bandwidth and criterion bandwidth systematically attenuates the observed correlation, exactly as Wittmann's framework predicts. \citet{WittmannKlumb2006}, analyzing the parameters of the augmented Tucker lens model equation, demonstrated that for any observed effect size in psychological research, there are six sources of underestimation (range restriction, unreliability of both treatment and criterion, lack of construct symmetry, and negative sampling error) against only two sources of overestimation (range enhancement and positive sampling error). The odds are structurally stacked toward underestimation. Applied to motivation-intelligence correlations: the reported values of $r \approx .20$--.35 are almost certainly attenuated by multiple simultaneous artifacts. The present model therefore predicts that when motivation is measured with the same care for bandwidth matching that intelligence researchers apply to cognitive assessment---\hspace{0pt}aggregated across multiple contexts, occasions, and expression modalities---\hspace{0pt}the motivation-intelligence correlation will prove substantially larger than the values currently reported in meta-analyses.

\item \textbf{Motivation should predict behavioral consistency across intellectual engagement situations, not merely effort level on single occasions}: The recursive loop iterates through repeated engagement episodes across diverse situations. The Motivation component's primary contribution to the loop is therefore not how hard one tries on any given occasion, but how \emph{consistently} one engages across situations and over time. Wittmann's demonstration with Eysenck's personality dimensions provides the methodological model: Extraversion predicted the \emph{mean} of aggregated behavioral indicators across situations, while Neuroticism predicted their \emph{intraindividual standard deviation} \citep{Wittmann1988}. Applied to the recursive model: motivation should primarily predict the consistency (low intraindividual variability) of intellectual engagement across diverse situations. Highly motivated individuals engage consistently; low-motivation individuals engage sporadically. The loop compounds through iteration count, not iteration intensity. This predicts that the strongest longitudinal predictor of intellectual development is not peak motivation (highest observed engagement) but motivational consistency (lowest variance in engagement across occasions)---\hspace{0pt}a prediction that is directly testable with experience-sampling methods. Within-person data of the required form already exist: \citet{Brose2010}, using daily measures of effort and enjoyment alongside working memory performance in the COGITO study, found that among participants whose motivation varied, higher-motivation days were also better-performance days. The coupling was heterogeneous across individuals and weak or absent in the older sample, which is what a model treating motivation as one interacting component predicts rather than a uniform main effect. The COGITO design---\hspace{0pt}repeated measurement across many occasions, with structure estimated within rather than between persons---\hspace{0pt}is the methodological precedent for testing predictions 7 and 8 as stated.

\item \textbf{Aggregating imagination measures across modalities, not merely across occasions, should raise their convergence with fluid reasoning}: The argument of Section~3.1---\hspace{0pt}that a single function appears in the literature as a family of separate constructs because different instruments observe it in different contexts---\hspace{0pt}is not specific to motivation. The capacity to construct a model of something not present and run it is an operation rather than a location: it is carried out in whatever representational system holds the content being modelled, and is therefore instantiated separately for visual scenes, for narrative episodes, for spatial layouts, and for relational structure. Any single instrument samples one instantiation. Imagery-vividness scales sample the visual one, daydreaming inventories and absorption scales \citep{TellegenAtkinson1974} the narrative one, matrix and mental-rotation tasks the relational and spatial ones. The literature's current answer is that these do not converge with reasoning ability: \citeauthor{AckermanHeggestad1997}'s \citeyearpar{AckermanHeggestad1997} meta-analysis puts the Openness-Gf correlation at $r = .08$, against .30 with Gc, and \citet{NusbaumSilvia2011}, working with latent variables, found that the Openness aspect predicted creative behavior but not fluid intelligence while the Intellect aspect predicted fluid intelligence but not creative behavior. The present model reads that dissociation as a modality projection rather than a structural boundary, and predicts that aggregation across modalities will recover convergence that aggregation within a single modality does not. This yields a contrast that separates the two claims. Bandwidth asymmetry alone (prediction 7) predicts that observed convergence rises with the number of aggregated observations, whatever their source. The present model predicts more: that $k$ observations spanning four modalities will converge with aggregated fluid ability more strongly than $k$ observations of one modality gathered across $k$ occasions. If the two aggregation schemes perform equivalently at matched $k$, the mechanistic claim is wrong and only the psychometric one survives.
\end{enumerate}

A further consequence, which can be tested on data already collected and which specifies in advance what would disconfirm it, is developed separately in Section~7.3.

\subsection{A Direct Test: Subtest Composition and Secular Change}

The recursive model implies a partition of standard test content that cuts across the usual verbal/performance and Gf/Gc divisions. Some subtests require the examinee to construct a model of a situation that is not present and then run it. Matrix reasoning and Raven's progressive matrices require building and testing a rule that no item states; block design and mental rotation require transforming a represented figure; Similarities requires constructing a superordinate relation that neither term supplies; Comprehension items about hypothetical circumstances require simulating an agent's situation. Other subtests require retrieval from a store: Vocabulary, Information, and arithmetic-fact items. Call these simulation-loaded and retrieval-loaded. The partition is not the Gf/Gc distinction---\hspace{0pt}Similarities and Comprehension are scored as verbal, knowledge-dependent subtests---\hspace{0pt}and it is not the verbal/nonverbal distinction. Arithmetic is the contested case: it loads on working memory, yet its items call for the application of a taught procedure rather than the construction of a novel one, and the classification assigns it to the retrieval-loaded class.

The partition sorts the largest secular changes on record. Across the American Wechsler standardizations from 1947 to 2002, \citet{Flynn2007} reports a gain of roughly 24 IQ points on Similarities and gains of 12 to 21 points across the five Performance subtests, against 4 points on Vocabulary and 2 points each on Information and Arithmetic, with a Full Scale gain of about 18 points. At the level of domains rather than subtests, \citeauthor{PietschnigVoracek2015}'s \citeyearpar{PietschnigVoracek2015} meta-analysis of 271 samples from 31 countries estimates annual gains of 0.41 IQ points for fluid and 0.30 for spatial performance, against 0.21 for crystallized. In the Norwegian conscript series, \citet{Sundet2004} found that from the early 1970s the gains in general ability were driven almost exclusively by the Raven-like subtest, with the language and mathematics subtests contributing little, until the gains on the Raven-like test themselves stopped in the late 1990s.

Flynn's own account attributes this pattern to a secular shift toward abstract and scientific habits of classification rather than to any change in motivation, and the present framework does not displace that reading. What it adds is a reason why these two classes of subtest should be the ones that separate: a simulation-loaded item asks for model construction, which is the operation the recursive loop trains, while a retrieval-loaded item asks for a lookup against a store the loop fills only incidentally.

Three predictions follow, none of them yet tested against a classification fixed in advance.

The differential pattern should extend to the reversal. If the environmental conditions that support the loop have degraded, as \citeauthor{BratsbergRogeberg2018}'s \citeyearpar{BratsbergRogeberg2018} within-family analysis indicates, the decline should fall disproportionately on simulation-loaded content, mirroring the ascent. The Norwegian conscript archive carries its three subtests separately and can settle this directly.

The two classes should differ in trainability. Interventions that teach model construction should move simulation-loaded scores more than retrieval-loaded scores; content instruction should do the reverse. This predicts the shape of the working-memory-training record: transfer should appear where the trained operation and the criterion share the construction demand and fail where they do not.

The two classes should differ in their coupling to within-person motivational state. Repeated-measures and experience-sampling designs should find simulation-loaded performance covarying with momentary engagement more strongly than retrieval-loaded performance does, because the former requires sustained construction and the latter does not.

Two constraints govern the test. Subtest score changes cannot be read as changes in a latent ability without invariance testing: \citet{Wicherts2004} showed that the Wechsler scales are not measurement invariant over time, and that the observed pattern of subtest gains is not what a change in common-factor means would produce. The prediction is therefore stated over observed subtest scores, and any latent reading requires the invariance model to be fitted first. That constraint is also what makes the pattern informative here. A secular change not carried by the common factor is what the \citet{GignacZajenkowski2024} dissociation reports, and it is what the recursive model expects when scores rise without the loop being engaged.

The prediction is disconfirmed if simulation-loaded and retrieval-loaded subtests show statistically indistinguishable secular trajectories once the classification is fixed in advance, or if the reversal falls on retrieval-loaded content while simulation-loaded scores hold. Competing accounts do not generate the contrast: a test-familiarity account predicts gains concentrated on whatever content is most practiced, and a nutrition or general-health account predicts a broadly uniform lift.

\subsection{Limitations}

This paper presents a theoretical framework, not empirical data. The recursive model is offered as a conceptual tool for integrating existing findings and generating new predictions, not as a fully specified formal model. Formalizing the model mathematically---\hspace{0pt}specifying the functional forms of the recursive interactions, the role of time and developmental stage, and the boundary conditions under which the loop amplifies, stagnates, or collapses---\hspace{0pt}is the task for a subsequent paper.

The distinction between factual and operational knowledge, while intuitively clear, is not always sharp in practice. Many items of knowledge have both factual and operational aspects (e.g., knowing that spaced repetition works is factual; knowing how to implement it is operational). A more precise taxonomy of knowledge types, grounded in empirical data, would strengthen the framework.

The model also does not address the neuroscience of motivation in detail. The dopaminergic reward system, the role of the anterior cingulate cortex in effort allocation, and the neuromodulatory mechanisms that sustain engagement over time are all relevant but beyond the scope of a theoretical critique paper. Integrating the recursive model with the neuroscience of motivation is an important direction for future work.

Finally, there is a deeper limitation that the model shares with every intelligence framework: it says nothing about what intelligence is \emph{for}. If intelligence does not result in a good life---\hspace{0pt}in well-being, in flourishing---\hspace{0pt}is it really intelligence? The recursive loop can amplify itself indefinitely, but amplification without direction is not wisdom. A complete account of intelligence may ultimately require not just a theory of cognitive dynamics but a companion theory of living well. This paper does not attempt that integration, but it would be dishonest not to flag it.

The present framework and the four-model theory of consciousness \citep{Gruber2026} describe two halves of a causal chain: consciousness enables cognitive learning, and cognitive learning enables the recursive intelligence loop described here. The consciousness paper specifies the architectural mechanism (four nested models, criticality, virtual qualia) that makes cognitive learning possible; the present paper traces the downstream consequences of that capacity for intelligence, education, and artificial systems. Together, the two frameworks argue that consciousness and intelligence are not merely correlated but causally linked through a specific cognitive capacity---\hspace{0pt}one that no current artificial system possesses.

\subsection{A Historical Note}

The exclusion of motivation is not an inevitable consequence of studying cognition---\hspace{0pt}it is a historical choice. Binet stressed that motivation affects test results; \citet{Wechsler1940,Wechsler1943} explicitly called for the inclusion of non-intellective factors (Section~2.4). The psychometric tradition's reliance on factor analysis of cognitive test scores created a self-reinforcing methodological loop---\hspace{0pt}intelligence is what intelligence tests measure, intelligence tests measure cognitive abilities, therefore intelligence is a cognitive construct \citep{Sternberg1985}---\hspace{0pt}whose specific consequence for the treatment of motivation has not been systematically examined until now.


\section{Conclusion}

The systematic exclusion of motivation from intelligence models is not a harmless simplification. It produces a fundamentally incomplete picture: one that cannot explain the self-reinforcing dynamics of intellectual development, that renders invisible the crucial role of operational knowledge, and that leaves the field unable to account for the characteristic limitations of artificial intelligence systems.

Intelligence, I argue, is not a capacity. It is a \emph{learning ability}---\hspace{0pt}a recursive system in which Knowledge, Performance, and Motivation interact to produce a self-reinforcing developmental trajectory whose course is determined by the dynamics of the loop as it iterates across the lifespan, not by any single component measured at a single point in time.

The recursive model implies that intelligence is largely learnable---\hspace{0pt}that two of its three components are highly responsive to environmental influence and intervention, and that the recursive structure amplifies gains over time. Including motivation is not merely a matter of completeness but of structural adequacy: a model of intelligence that excludes motivation is like a model of combustion that includes fuel and oxygen but excludes heat. And the cost of this exclusion is not theoretical. When educational systems, hiring practices, and social institutions build on a model that reduces intelligence to cognitive performance, they create environments that systematically destroy the very component that drives intellectual development---\hspace{0pt}measured in children who stop trying, in potential never realized, and in a collective future diminished by every mind that was told it was not enough.
