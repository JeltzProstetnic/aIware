\begin{abstract}

Every major model of intelligence leaves motivation out of its formal structure, and the field has treated this as an omission ---\hspace{0pt} something to be repaired by adding a component. This paper argues that the omission is a symptom rather than the problem. What went wrong is a matter of \emph{type}: the tradition assumed that the constituents of intelligence are all the same kind of thing, and typed each of them as a capacity, because that is the kind of thing psychometric instruments were built to measure. On the account developed here they are not. Performance ---\hspace{0pt} processing capacity ---\hspace{0pt} is a capacity in the psychometric sense, and the only one of the three that is. Knowledge is not one constituent but two, divided by the physical boundary between what is stored in structure and what runs as process. And motivation is not a constituent of the loop at all: it is the policy that allocates the loop's time, a schedule rather than a substance. Re-typing motivation this way is not a relabelling, because it changes what should be expected of measurement. Four results the field currently reports without an account follow from it directly: motivation's century-long resistance to trait measurement, which is what an instrument finds when it looks for the magnitude of something that has none; the attenuation of motivation-intelligence correlations under narrow single-occasion sampling, which becomes a consequence of what is being sampled rather than an excuse for a disappointing number; the contrast between the durable effect of schooling on fluid ability and the absence of far transfer from working-memory training against active controls, which is what follows if education re-points the schedule while training drills the capacity measure; and the prediction that motivation should predict the \emph{consistency} of intellectual engagement rather than its peak intensity, because a loop compounds through iteration count. Two results would defeat it. A symmetric aggregation of motivation measures that still returned the currently reported average correlation would defeat the measurement claim, and a demonstration that peak engagement predicts long-term intellectual development better than consistency would defeat the policy reading. The recursive structure itself is not claimed as novel ---\hspace{0pt} mutualism and multiplier accounts formalized reciprocal causation two decades ago. What is claimed is that one of the three things being reciprocally caused has been the wrong kind of thing all along, and that correcting its type explains why the instruments behaved as they did.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} intelligence, motivation, allocation policy, construct validity, recursive systems, operational knowledge, psychometrics, CHC theory
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction: A Curious Omission}

Consider two children with identical IQ scores at age six. By age thirty, one has become a research scientist, the other has not opened a book since leaving school. Standard psychometric models of intelligence struggle to account for this divergence. The six-year-old's IQ score, which captured cognitive performance under controlled test conditions, told us almost nothing about the trajectory of intellectual development that would follow. The usual reading of this is that something was left out of the measurement. This paper argues something more specific: what differed between the two children was not a missing quantity but a \textbf{schedule} ---\hspace{0pt} how much of their cognitive machinery was pointed at what, and for how long, over twenty-four years. A schedule is not a smaller or larger amount of anything, which is why an instrument built to measure amounts registered so little of it.

The missing variable is not mysterious. Every teacher knows it. Every parent has observed it. Every employer screens for it. Motivation---\hspace{0pt}the sustained drive to learn, to understand, to act on one's environment---\hspace{0pt}is universally acknowledged as critical to cognitive achievement. The empirical literature on motivation and academic performance is vast \citep{DeciRyan2000,Dweck2006,WigfieldEccles2000}. Meta-analyses find reciprocal relationships between motivation and achievement: in a multilevel meta-analysis of longitudinal studies both directions were positive, with the pooled effect of achievement on later motivation about twice that of motivation on later achievement \citep{Vu2024}.

And yet, motivation appears nowhere in the formal structure of any major model of intelligence ---\hspace{0pt} at most, as in Ackerman's PPIK framework (Section 3.4), it enters as a moderator of intellectual development, outside the intelligence construct itself.

The Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) taxonomy---\hspace{0pt}the most widely used framework in intelligence research---\hspace{0pt}is a hierarchical arrangement of cognitive abilities beneath a general factor \emph{g}, descending through broad stratum abilities (fluid reasoning, comprehension-knowledge, short-term memory, processing speed, and others) to narrow abilities \citep{McGrew2009,SchneiderMcGrew2018}. It is an impressive edifice. It contains no motivational component whatsoever.

\citeauthor{Cattell1971}'s \citeyearpar{Cattell1971} own investment theory, which proposed that fluid intelligence (Gf) is ``invested'' into crystallized intelligence (Gc) through learning, comes closer to acknowledging the role of motivation---\hspace{0pt}after all, investment requires an investor, and investors require reasons to invest. But Cattell treated motivation as an \emph{external condition} that modulates the investment process, not as a constitutive part of intelligence itself. In his framework, motivation is to intelligence what temperature is to a chemical reaction: it affects the rate, but it is not part of the reaction.

This paper argues that this analogy is wrong, and that the reason it is wrong is a matter of type. Motivation is not external to intelligence, and it is not a further ingredient inside it either; it is the policy that allocates the system's time. Excluding it is not a harmless simplification ---\hspace{0pt} it produces a systematically distorted picture that fails to capture the self-reinforcing dynamics that define intellectual development across the lifespan.

The argument proceeds in seven steps. Section 2 surveys major intelligence models, documents the systematic exclusion of motivation, and closes (Section 2.7) with the critique the field already makes of its own construct-typing. Section 3 presents a recursive three-component model in which knowledge, cognitive performance, and motivation form a closed amplification loop, argues that the three are of different kinds, and derives four standing results from re-typing motivation as the loop's allocation policy. Section 4 examines the special role of \emph{operational knowledge} ---\hspace{0pt} the knowledge about how to learn ---\hspace{0pt} which functions as the primary multiplier within this loop. Section 5 explores what the recursive model implies about artificial intelligence systems that lack motivation. Section 6 draws out what I consider the most consequential implication of the framework: that intelligence is, to a large extent, learnable, and that educational practices built on the contrary assumption carry a cost the model predicts will compound. Section 7 relates the framework to established models, states its testable predictions and their disconfirmation criteria, and sets out its limitations.


\section{The Status Quo: How Intelligence Models Treat Motivation}

\subsection{The CHC Taxonomy}

The Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) model is the dominant framework in contemporary intelligence research \citep{McGrew2009, SchneiderMcGrew2018}. It arranges cognitive abilities into a three-stratum hierarchy: a general factor \emph{g} at the apex, broad abilities at stratum II (including fluid reasoning Gf, comprehension-knowledge Gc, short-term memory Gsm, processing speed Gs, long-term storage and retrieval Glr, visual processing Gv, auditory processing Ga, and others), and narrow abilities at stratum I.

This model is explicitly cognitive. It measures what the brain can do on demand, under standardized conditions, with maximum effort assumed. Motivational variation is treated as measurement noise---\hspace{0pt}something to be minimized through standardized test administration, not modeled as a structural feature of intelligence \citep{SchneiderMcGrew2018}. The implicit assumption is that motivation is a confound, not a component. The CHC framework's internal coherence has also been questioned on its own terms. \citet{CanivezYoungstrom2019} documented that Carroll and Horn held irreconcilable positions on the status of \emph{g}, that confirmatory factor analyses of CHC-based test batteries show poor fit, and that the broad stratum abilities provide weak incremental validity beyond \emph{g}. These internal difficulties suggest that the CHC taxonomy may be less settled than its widespread adoption implies---\hspace{0pt}and that the systematic exclusion of motivational variables may be contributing to its interpretive limitations.

\subsection{Cattell's Investment Theory}

\citet{Cattell1971} proposed that Gc develops from the ``investment'' of Gf in learning experiences. This was a significant conceptual advance: it introduced a dynamic, developmental dimension to intelligence that the purely psychometric models lacked. Gf represents the biological substrate---\hspace{0pt}the raw computational power of the brain, heavily influenced by genetics, peaking in early adulthood and declining thereafter. Gc represents the accumulated product of applying that power to the world---\hspace{0pt}knowledge, skills, cultural repertoire---\hspace{0pt}which continues to grow throughout life.

But who decides what to invest in? Cattell's theory requires an agent who allocates Gf to particular learning domains. That agent must be motivated---\hspace{0pt}must find some domains more interesting, more rewarding, or more useful than others. Without motivation, the ``investment'' metaphor collapses: one cannot invest without the intention to invest.

Cattell was aware of this. He discussed personality traits---\hspace{0pt}particularly those the later literature labels ``investment traits'' \citep{Ackerman1996}---\hspace{0pt}as modulators of the Gf-to-Gc pathway (\citealp[see also][on the relationship between investment traits and intelligence]{Ziegler2012}). But he kept these firmly outside the intelligence construct. The result is an investment theory without an investor.

\subsection{Sternberg's Triarchic Theory}

\citeauthor{Sternberg1985}'s \citeyearpar{Sternberg1985} triarchic theory expanded the scope of intelligence beyond the psychometric tradition by proposing three aspects: analytical (similar to traditional IQ), creative (the ability to deal with novel situations), and practical (the ability to adapt to, shape, and select environments). This was a deliberate attempt to broaden the construct beyond what IQ tests measure.

Practical intelligence---\hspace{0pt}knowing how to get things done in real-world contexts---\hspace{0pt}comes close to the territory of motivation, since effectively shaping one's environment requires the drive to do so. But Sternberg framed practical intelligence as a \emph{competence}, not a \emph{drive}. You can know how to navigate a social environment without being motivated to do so. The triarchic theory, like CHC, treats motivation as logically separate from ability.

\subsection{Wechsler's Unfulfilled Call}

David Wechsler, whose intelligence scales remain the most widely administered in the world, was remarkably explicit about this problem. He defined intelligence as ``the aggregate or global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, to think rationally, and to deal effectively with his environment'' \citep[p.~3]{Wechsler1944}. As early as 1943, he had made the argument explicit: ``We cannot expect to measure total intelligence until our tests also include some measures of the non-intellective factors''---\hspace{0pt}identifying persistence, curiosity, and goal orientation as essential components of intelligent behavior \citep{Wechsler1943}.

The field ignored him. The modern Wechsler scales (WAIS, WISC) remain purely cognitive instruments. Wechsler's own tests do not measure the non-intellective factors he argued were essential. This is perhaps the clearest illustration of the blind spot described here: the founder of the world's most widely used intelligence test explicitly called for the inclusion of motivational factors, and the field proceeded as if he had not spoken.

\subsection{Gardner's Multiple Intelligences}

\citet{Gardner1983} proposed seven relatively independent intelligences: linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal and intrapersonal. A naturalist intelligence was added in 1999, bringing the count to eight, with an existential intelligence floated tentatively as a ninth. While intrapersonal intelligence---\hspace{0pt}the capacity for self-understanding---\hspace{0pt}includes awareness of one's own motivational states, Gardner did not identify motivation itself as an intelligence or as a structural component of his framework. The model was criticized extensively for failing to demonstrate that its proposed intelligences are truly independent rather than facets of \emph{g} \citep{Waterhouse2006}, but the absence of motivation was rarely noted as a problem.

\subsection{The General Pattern}

Across all major models, the pattern is the same: motivation is acknowledged as important, treated as correlated with intelligence, and then excluded from the formal model. The reasons are methodological: intelligence tests measure \emph{maximum performance} (what a person \emph{can} do, not what they \emph{will} do; \citealp{Cronbach1949}), factor-analytic models are built from cognitive test scores in which motivation does not appear, and disciplinary boundaries keep the motivation and intelligence literatures separate (\citealp[for exceptions, see][]{Murayama2013,CredeKuncel2008}). \citet{CarrDweck2019}, in their Cambridge Handbook chapter on ``Intelligence and Motivation,'' exemplify this pattern: they review extensive evidence of interaction between the two constructs but frame them as ontologically separate, never questioning whether the separation itself is the problem.

The problem runs deeper than disciplinary boundaries. Intelligence testing paradigms are specifically designed to suppress motivational variance: constrained time limits, rigid testing environments, no feedback, no opportunity for social interaction, and no access to aids create conditions under which personality and motivation have minimal room to express themselves \citep{Ackerman2018}. The measurement context is engineered to isolate ``pure'' cognitive capacity---\hspace{0pt}and then the resulting scores are used to argue that intelligence is a purely cognitive construct. The circularity is rarely noted.

These are legitimate practical considerations. But methodological convenience should not be confused with theoretical truth. The question is not whether motivation is hard to measure alongside cognitive abilities (it is), but whether a model of intelligence that excludes motivation provides an adequate account of how intelligence actually develops and operates.

I argue that it does not.


\subsection{The Field's Own Critique}

Whether psychology's constructs are of the right kind, and whether anyone has checked, is a question the field has put to itself for decades, in its own central venues.

\citet{Meehl1978} supplied the classic statement of the symptom. Surveying the "soft" areas of psychology, he observed that their theories "rise and decline, come and go, more as a function of baffled boredom than anything else," and that "the enterprise shows a disturbing absence of that cumulative character" found in the established sciences (p. 807). His diagnosis was not that psychological concepts are illegitimate ---\hspace{0pt} the same paper defends open concepts as scientifically respectable and probably unavoidable ---\hspace{0pt} but that theories built on them are abandoned rather than refuted, so that nothing accretes. Meehl documented the non-cumulativeness and left the disease underdetermined.

One candidate for the disease is that the field rarely asks what its measures refer to. \citet{Borsboom2004} proposed that "a test is valid for measuring an attribute if and only if (a) the attribute exists and (b) variations in the attribute causally produce variations in the outcomes of the measurement procedure" (p. 1061), and in the same paper severed validity from prediction: a score can forecast outcomes indefinitely without that fact bearing on whether the attribute it names exists. The definition is conditional and asserts nothing about whether any particular psychological attribute is real. What it does is separate two questions the validity literature had run together ---\hspace{0pt} does the score predict, and is there a thing being measured ---\hspace{0pt} and observe that working practice answers only the first. \citet{EronenBringmann2021} drew the consequence for theory-building: "psychological science is permeated by numerous psychological constructs of unknown validity," which is among the reasons good psychological theories are so difficult to construct. They add that they "by no means intend to suggest that theorizing in psychology is hopeless." The licensed conclusion is unknown validity, never known invalidity: the field predicts with instruments whose referents it has not established.

Intelligence research owns the sharpest demonstration that this situation is stable. Van der Maas et al. (2006) showed that initially uncorrelated processes reinforcing one another's growth reproduce the positive manifold ---\hspace{0pt} the empirical signature from which \emph{g} is extracted ---\hspace{0pt} without any general cause behind it. Within that model "there is nothing wrong with using the g factor as a summary or psychometric index variable (e.g., in prediction), as long as we do not assume that this variable relates to a single underlying quantitative process or capacity" (pp. 855--856). Mutualism does not show that \emph{g} fails to refer; its authors offer it as an alternative, not a refutation. What it shows is how the strongest predictor in psychology \emph{could} predict everything it predicts while referring to nothing of the kind its name suggests. The joint upshot of these four sources reaches exactly that far: constructs of unknown validity that predict anyway. The critique concerns typing ---\hspace{0pt} whether a construct is an index, a capacity, or a cause ---\hspace{0pt} and predictive success settles none of it.

The clearest exhibit sits inside the metric itself. The Wechsler batteries score by age-banded deviation: scaled scores are computed within age bands, against age-matched norms. The raw age-related decline is documented ---\hspace{0pt} it is in the norm tables from which the bands are built ---\hspace{0pt} but the construction relocates it out of the score: a seventy-year-old at the median of seventy-year-olds and a twenty-five-year-old at the median of twenty-five-year-olds both receive 100. Whether that relocation is the right construction depends on what the instrument measures across age, and on precisely this question the field's foremost measurement scholars disagree. In a single exchange in \emph{Neurobiology of Aging}, \citet{Salthouse2009a} argues that some aspects of cognitive decline begin in the twenties and thirties once retest effects are corrected; \citet{Schaie2009} replies that this reifies the cross-sectional fallacy; \citet{Nilsson2009} of the Betula study enter a separate rebuttal on longitudinal grounds; \citeauthor{Salthouse2009b}'s rejoinder \citeyearpar{Salthouse2009b} closes the exchange. The exhibit is the disagreement itself: a field whose central instrument admits no agreed answer to what it measures across age has a typing problem, acknowledged from within.

\section{Intelligence as a Recursive System}

\subsection{Three Components, and What Kind of Thing Each One Is}

Intelligence ---\hspace{0pt} understood as \emph{learning ability} (Lernf\"ahigkeit) ---\hspace{0pt} is treated here as a system of three interacting components. That much the recursive model shares with the investment tradition, and the model was first proposed in Gruber (2015, in German), developed here in its first systematic English-language presentation.

What it does not share is the assumption that the three are the same \emph{kind} of thing. They are not, and the field's long difficulty with motivation follows from proceeding as though they were. Performance is a capacity in the psychometric sense ---\hspace{0pt} the only one of the three that is. Knowledge is not one thing but two, divided by a physical boundary. And Motivation is not a component of the loop at all: it is the \textbf{policy that allocates the loop's time}. Four results the field currently reports without an account follow from this single re-typing (Section 3.3).

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Performance} (\emph{Leistung}) ---\hspace{0pt} a capacity, and the clear case. The processing capacity of the cognitive system: working memory capacity, processing speed, and the computational power of the neural substrate. It corresponds roughly to Cattell's Gf and to \citeauthor{Frank1962}'s \citeyearpar{Frank1962} short-term memory capacity C = S $\times$ D, where S is processing speed in bits/second and D is memory span in seconds. It is influenced by genetics and by training. Performance is what the psychometric instrument was built for: it is bounded, it is present at the moment of measurement, and a single occasion samples it well because there is no sense in which a person's processing capacity is \emph{elsewhere} while the test is running.

\item \textbf{Knowledge} (\emph{Wissen}) ---\hspace{0pt} two things, not one, and the boundary between them is physical rather than taxonomic. The accumulated content of learning divides into what is \emph{stored in structure} and what is \emph{run as process}. The distinction has a blunt operational form: \textbf{what would a dead brain still yield to a sufficiently good structural readout, and what stops when the dynamics stop?} What survives is the implicit, structural side ---\hspace{0pt} synaptic weights, dendritic morphology, receptor configurations; the sediment of everything the person has learned. What does not survive is the explicit side: the running, generative activity that constructs a model of a situation and operates on it. Factual knowledge sits on the first side. Operational knowledge ---\hspace{0pt} how to learn, how to reason, how to strategize ---\hspace{0pt} is a disposition stored on the first side that exists only as the second when it does any work. Both correspond loosely to Cattell's Gc, which is why the tradition has had no reason to separate them; the reason to separate them appears in Section 4, where the two sides turn out to behave differently under intervention. The criterion is idealized and its edges are messy: persistent activity carries content across seconds without any structural change to read, neuromodulatory state alters what the same structure computes, and no present technique performs the readout the criterion invokes. It is a conceptual boundary with a physical basis, not a laboratory procedure.

\item \textbf{Motivation} ---\hspace{0pt} not a component of the loop but the \textbf{allocation policy over it}. Motivation does not contribute a quantity that combines with the other two. It decides \emph{how much of the loop runs, on what, and for how long}. A policy is not a capacity: it has no ceiling to measure, it is not present-at-a-moment in the way a capacity is, and asking how much of it someone has is close to a category error ---\hspace{0pt} the question a policy answers is not \emph{how much} but \emph{when, and on what}.

An observatory makes the three types visible at once. The optics are a capacity: aperture and resolution are fixed, measurable on any clear night, and they bound what the instrument can ever see. The plate archive is accumulated content ---\hspace{0pt} it survives the observatory going dark, and it is what the institution actually knows. And the telescope-time schedule is neither. It is the allocation policy: it decides which instrument points where, on which nights, for how long. \textbf{You cannot find the schedule by disassembling the instrument}, because it is not in the instrument. Three consequences follow directly: a schedule is weather- and state-bound, so any single night is a poor estimate of it; a good schedule shows up as \emph{consistency} of observation across a season rather than as intensity on one night; and the archive grows only where the schedule has been pointing.

At the intuitive level two expressions of the policy are readily distinguished: \emph{Wissensdrang} (thirst for knowledge) ---\hspace{0pt} allocation toward understanding ---\hspace{0pt} and \emph{Handlungsdrang} (urge to act) ---\hspace{0pt} allocation toward action and exploration. These correspond to familiar constructs in the personality-intelligence literature: Wissensdrang maps closely to \citeauthor{CacioppioPetty1982}'s \citeyearpar{CacioppioPetty1982} need for cognition (NFC; see also \citealp{Cacioppo1996}, for the dispositional characterization) and \citeauthor{GoffAckerman1992}'s \citeyearpar{GoffAckerman1992} typical intellectual engagement (TIE); Handlungsdrang maps to the exploration and risk-taking dispositions that Wittmann and Hattrup (2004) showed mediate intelligence-performance relationships by generating new learning opportunities.

However, the relationship between these intuitive categories and their empirical proxies is not a simple mapping. NFC and TIE correlate at $r \approx .78$--$.87$ \citep{WooHarmsKuncel2007, Schweitzer2025}, yet they distribute differently across intelligence facets. In a multi-level meta-analysis, NFC was more strongly related to fluid reasoning (Gf) than TIE was ($r = .19$ vs $.12$), while TIE was more strongly related to crystallized knowledge (Gc) than NFC was ($r = .35$ vs $.24$); both contrasts are significant \citep{Schweitzer2025}. Neither trait is Gf-specific---\hspace{0pt}each correlates more strongly with Gc than with Gf---\hspace{0pt}but the two traits are ordered oppositely on the two facets despite measuring something close to a common construct. Need for achievement, openness to experience, and sensation seeking show yet other patterns \citep{Ackerman2018}. The standard interpretation is that these represent genuinely distinct motivational constructs.

The re-typing supplies an alternative that does not have to be stipulated, because it follows from what kind of thing a policy is. \textbf{A policy has no trait essence to be measured.} A capacity has a magnitude that is there whether or not anyone samples it; a policy exists only in its allocations, and an instrument can therefore catch it only in the act ---\hspace{0pt} in some particular context, allocating to some particular thing. Two instruments that sample different contexts are not measuring two constructs. They are measuring the same policy at two of its expressions, and they will disagree exactly to the extent that the contexts differ. \textbf{Fragmentation across instruments is what a policy looks like from outside}, and a literature that finds a family of moderately-correlated, differently-loading motivation constructs has found precisely the signature the re-typing predicts. Under the trait reading that pattern is an embarrassment to be explained; under the policy reading it is the expected observation.

The argument, which draws on the architectural framework developed in \citet{Gruber2026}, is as follows. Motivation---\hspace{0pt}the evaluative process that assigns salience, directs attention, and sustains engagement---\hspace{0pt}is a function of the brain's explicit processing: the self-model that consciously evaluates situations, weighs options, and decides where to invest effort. Ordinary usage of ``motivation'' reaches into subconscious drive territory as well---\hspace{0pt}affective valuations, automatic approach/avoidance, drives the subject cannot articulate. The construct used here is deliberately narrower: the conscious, symbolizable evaluative layer. The substrate-level optimization that underlies it---\hspace{0pt}what the companion theory of consciousness terms \emph{will} \citep[\S4.2.2]{Gruber2026}---\hspace{0pt}is real and constitutive of behavior, but it is not what the recursive loop's multiplier measures. This is a unified computational function, not a family of independent traits. It \emph{appears} fragmented in the empirical literature because different measurement instruments observe this function in different contexts. NFC observes motivation during novel reasoning, and so carries more of the processing substrate that novel reasoning depends on (Gf) than an instrument sampling a different context does. TIE observes it during habitual knowledge-seeking, and so carries more of the accumulated knowledge store (Gc). Risk-taking observes it during exploration, which generates new learning opportunities. What this predicts is a difference of degree, not an exclusive loading: each instrument should relate to both facets and differ in how much of each it carries, which is the pattern the estimates above show. The constructs diverge not because the underlying motivation differs, but because the measurement context determines which intelligence facet the observation co-activates most strongly.

Artificial intelligence is often invoked at this point. It settles less than it appears to. Deployed large language models possess vast knowledge and high processing performance while displaying none of the behaviour that any motivation construct was built to measure: no curiosity, no self-directed learning, no drive to close gaps in their own understanding (Section~5). It is tempting to read this as support for the unity claim---\hspace{0pt}what is missing is not need for cognition, or typical intellectual engagement, or need for achievement, but the single function of which these are partial measures. The inference does not go through. These systems lack \emph{all} of the constructs at once, and the simultaneous absence of many things is equally consistent with there being many things absent. A case in which everything is missing cannot discriminate one missing function from a set of missing traits.

What the case does supply is the observation that the missing element is not supplied by scaling either of the other two components, which is a claim about the loop rather than about the structure of motivation. The unity claim has to be carried by evidence that can come apart: the aggregation contrast of prediction 9, which pits a single-function reading against a distinct-constructs reading at matched sample size, is where it is put at risk.

This reinterpretation has a direct precedent in Wittmann's own Brunswik symmetry framework \citep{Wittmann1988,WittmannKlumb2006}: observed correlations are bounded by the symmetry between predictor and criterion bandwidth. Narrow motivation measures (a single questionnaire scale) correlated with broad intelligence criteria (a full test battery) will produce attenuated and criterion-specific correlations---\hspace{0pt}not because motivation is weakly related to intelligence, but because the measurement design is asymmetric. Properly aggregated motivation measures, assessed across multiple contexts and occasions, should produce substantially higher and more uniform correlations with intelligence---\hspace{0pt}a testable prediction of the present framework. A further attenuation arises from temporal reliability: motivation, unlike a fixed cognitive capacity, varies across situations and time. A single-occasion questionnaire captures a snapshot of a fluctuating state, and the resulting low reliability mechanically suppresses the observed correlation---\hspace{0pt}an artifact of temporal sampling, distinct from and compounding the bandwidth asymmetry.

The recursive model therefore treats Motivation as a single component with two functional expressions---\hspace{0pt}the epistemic (Wissensdrang: directing engagement toward understanding) and the agentic (Handlungsdrang: directing engagement toward action and exploration)---\hspace{0pt}while maintaining that these expressions reflect a unified evaluative process rather than structurally independent traits. What the personality-intelligence literature has catalogued as distinct constructs (NFC, TIE, openness, need for achievement, risk-taking) are, in this view, context-specific behavioral signatures of the same underlying function, measured at different points of its expression.

The three components are functionally separable but not mechanistically independent. Knowledge, Performance and Motivation are distinguished by what each contributes to the loop---\hspace{0pt}stored content, processing capacity, and the evaluative direction of engagement---\hspace{0pt}not by running on disjoint machinery. The capacity to construct a model and run it detached from present input is drawn on by both Performance and Motivation: it is what reasoning with unfamiliar material requires, and it is also what allows a person to represent a state of affairs that does not obtain and be moved by it, which is what sustained engagement toward a distant goal consists in. The architectural account of that capacity is given in \citet{Gruber2026}. For a measurement model this shared dependence is a constraint rather than a defect. It predicts that the three components will not be found orthogonal, that attempts to purify them will strip out variance the loop actually uses, and that a decomposition succeeding in making them independent would be measuring something other than the system described here.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Recursive Loop}

The recursive loop described here requires a specific cognitive capacity: cognitive learning---\hspace{0pt}the induction of general theories from particular observations---\hspace{0pt}as distinct from reinforcement learning \citep{Gruber2015}. The distinction between cognitive learning and reinforcement learning, and the specific neural architecture that enables the former, is developed in \citet{Gruber2015} and formalized in \citet{Gruber2026}. What matters here is the implication: the recursive intelligence loop can only self-sustain in systems capable of cognitive learning. What a system must be like in order to be capable of it is a further question, and the present argument does not settle it (Sections~3.4, 5.3 and 7.4).

The critical claim of this framework is that these three components are not merely additive; they form a \emph{closed recursive loop} in which each component amplifies the others:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Knowledge enhances Performance}: Learning strategies, logical tools, and strategic thinking (operational knowledge) directly improve the efficiency of cognitive processing. A chess player who has learned heuristics can process positions faster than one who relies on brute-force search. A reader who has learned phonemic decoding processes text more fluently, freeing working memory for comprehension.

\item \textbf{Performance enhances Knowledge}: Greater cognitive processing capacity enables faster and deeper learning. Higher working memory capacity allows the learner to hold more information in mind simultaneously, facilitating the formation of connections and the extraction of patterns.

\item \textbf{Motivation enhances both Knowledge and Performance}: The motivated learner seeks out learning opportunities (expanding Knowledge) and practices cognitive skills (training Performance). Motivation sustains engagement over time, which is essential for the recursive loop to iterate.

\item \textbf{Knowledge and Performance enhance Motivation}: Success in learning and problem-solving generates positive affect and self-efficacy \citep{Bandura1997}, which in turn sustains motivation. This is the mechanism behind the Matthew effect: early success breeds the motivation that produces further success \citep{Stanovich1986}.
\end{itemize}

This recursive structure produces the self-reinforcing dynamics that are empirically well-documented but theoretically under-explained. The Matthew effect in reading \citep{Stanovich1986}---\hspace{0pt}whereby children who read well read more, thereby becoming even better readers---\hspace{0pt}is a specific instance of this general recursive loop. The divergence of Gf and Gc across the lifespan---\hspace{0pt}Gf declining from early adulthood while Gc continues to grow---\hspace{0pt}is explained by the recursive model as the growing dominance of the Knowledge and Motivation legs as the Performance leg (biological processing capacity) begins to decline.

\subsection{What the Re-Typing Derives}

The claim here is stronger than the trivial observation that motivation influences learning outcomes. This paper advances a specific structural claim: that intelligence is a \emph{system} whose behavior is determined by the recursive interaction of three components, that those components are of different kinds, and that removing any one of them ---\hspace{0pt} or mis-typing it ---\hspace{0pt} produces qualitatively wrong predictions about the system's dynamics.

Four results follow from the re-typing that the field currently reports without an account. None of the four is new as an observation; what is new is that they stop being separate embarrassments and become one consequence seen four times.

\textbf{First, motivation's resistance to trait measurement.} A century of instrument-building has produced a family of moderately intercorrelated motivation scales that load differently on different ability facets and none of which behaves like a clean trait. Under the trait reading this is a failure of instrumentation, to be fixed by a better scale. Under the policy reading no better scale is coming, because the thing being measured has no trait essence to find (Section 3.1). The observed pattern is the predicted one.

\textbf{Second, Brunswik-symmetry attenuation stops being an excuse and becomes a theorem.} A state- and situation-bound policy \emph{must} attenuate under narrow, single-occasion measurement ---\hspace{0pt} not as an artifact that careful work might avoid, but as a consequence of what is being sampled. A capacity is present while you measure it; an allocation policy is visible only in the allocations it happens to have made in the window you sampled. So the aggregation prediction of Section 3.1 is not a hope that better measurement will raise a disappointing correlation. It is what the re-typing entails, and its failure would count against the re-typing.

\textbf{Third, the contrast between what education does to fluid ability and what working-memory training does not.} \citet{RitchieTuckerDrob2018}, meta-analysing designs that control prior intelligence, find education raises intelligence by roughly one to two points per year, with the estimate for adult outcomes at 1.197 points per year of education; on fluid tests in adults the effect is at the lower end of that range. \citet{MelbyLervag2016} find that working-memory training produces no far transfer to nonverbal ability against \emph{treated} control groups ---\hspace{0pt} \emph{g} = 0.05 immediately and --0.05 at delayed follow-up.

The naive form of the comparison does not work. Against \emph{untreated} controls, working-memory training gives \emph{g} = 0.20, which on a raw reading of effect sizes would beat a year of schooling; education has no placebo arm, so the two literatures do not share a ruler and their headline numbers cannot be set beside each other. What can be compared is two things that do survive the design difference. One is \textbf{what the treated-control comparison isolates}: when the control group also trains something, expectancy and engagement are held constant, and what remains is the specific effect of drilling the capacity measure ---\hspace{0pt} which is nil. The other is \textbf{durability}: working-memory far transfer is not merely small but \emph{negative} at follow-up, while education's effect is estimated on adult outcomes years downstream.

Read through the re-typing, this is one prediction rather than two findings. \textbf{Education builds the loop; working-memory training drills the proxy.} Schooling is a sustained institutional re-pointing of the allocation policy ---\hspace{0pt} years of it, across content, with the schedule set by someone other than the learner. Working-memory training raises a score on the instrument that estimates Performance without touching either the policy or the stored content, and a capacity trained in isolation from what allocates it has nowhere to propagate.

\textbf{Fourth, consistency rather than level.} A trait predicts how much; a policy predicts how regularly. If Motivation is a schedule, the quantity that should carry the loop is not peak engagement on a good day but the consistency of engagement across occasions, because the loop compounds through iteration count rather than iteration intensity. This is Prediction 8. The two readings disagree about which statistic of a within-person series should predict long-term intellectual development, and experience-sampling designs can already settle it.

Consider the prediction that intelligence should be a self-reinforcing process. Standard psychometric models, which treat intelligence as a relatively stable trait (albeit one that changes somewhat over the lifespan), have difficulty explaining why some individuals show dramatically increasing intellectual capability over decades while others plateau early. The recursive model predicts exactly this: small initial differences in any of the three components ---\hspace{0pt} even in motivation alone ---\hspace{0pt} should compound over time, producing the wide variance in adult intellectual achievement observed empirically.

This is not merely a statistical amplification of initial differences (as in a simple fan-spread model). It is a qualitatively different dynamic: a positive feedback loop that can be entered at any point. A child with modest initial cognitive ability but high motivation and good operational knowledge (learning strategies) can, through the recursive loop, develop intellectual capabilities far beyond what their initial "IQ" would predict. Conversely, a child with high initial cognitive ability but low motivation or poor learning strategies may stagnate. Both patterns are widely observed empirically but poorly explained by models that treat intelligence as a primarily cognitive, primarily stable construct.

Population-level evidence supports this process view. \citet{BratsbergRogeberg2018}, analyzing Norwegian military conscript data, demonstrated that IQ scores rose and then declined across birth cohorts \emph{within families} ---\hspace{0pt} a design that rules out explanations resting on changing between-family composition, including the prominent genetic ones, and is consistent with environmental causation. The Flynn effect reversal, now documented across multiple countries, is incompatible with models that treat intelligence as a primarily biological trait. It is, however, precisely what a recursive model predicts: when environmental conditions that support the loop (educational quality, intellectual engagement) degrade, the loop weakens at the population level.

The structural claim also carries a measurement commitment that distinguishes it from the weaker one. If motivation is constitutive of the loop rather than a correlate of its output, it must be visible \emph{within} a person across occasions, not only as a between-person trait correlation. \citet{Brose2010} provide that observation directly: in the COGITO study, daily fluctuations in effort and enjoyment co-varied with same-day working memory performance within individuals. The effect is not universal ---\hspace{0pt} it was heterogeneous across participants and weak or absent among older adults ---\hspace{0pt} and the recursive model does not require it to be, since a component embedded in an interacting system will not behave as a uniform main effect.

A related pattern appears in the recent Austrian record, though it is a weaker result than it first looks. \citet{Oberleiter2024}, comparing two population-representative Germanophone samples (N = 1267) across six measurement-invariant subscales from 2005 to 2024, report substantial declines in single-factor \emph{g} assessments ($\Delta$R$^2$ between --.037 and --.066) alongside meaningful score increases in every domain (\emph{d} between 0.18 and 1.24); \citet{Andrzejewski2024} report the same direction over a shorter window (2005--2018), where the change (R² from .908 to .892) was not statistically significant. What declines in these records is the strength of the positive manifold ---\hspace{0pt} the inter-correlation among subtests ---\hspace{0pt} and not the level of any measured ability: every domain gained. Both sets of authors read the pattern as increasing ability differentiation in the general population. The recursive model does not predict that decoupling in advance and does not claim it as support. What it adds is a candidate mechanism for the differentiation: a loop engaged unevenly across content would produce increasingly asymmetric individual profiles while every subtest mean rises. Section 7.3 states the subtest-level test that would tell such an account from its competitors.

\subsection{Relation to Existing Work}

\subsubsection{Motivation as allocation is occupied ground}

The claim that motivation is best understood as the allocation of a limited resource is not new, and it is occupied three times over. The nearest occupant is inside the ability literature itself.

\textbf{\citet{KanferAckerman1989}} treat motivation as the allocation of limited attentional resources during skill acquisition, distinguishing a distal component ---\hspace{0pt} an expectancy-based allocation of effort across tasks ---\hspace{0pt} from a proximal one that divides attention on-task between the task itself and self-regulatory activity. \textbf{\citet{Shenhav2013}} derive the allocation of cognitive control from an expected-value optimization, with the anterior cingulate specifying the identity and intensity of the control signal to deploy. \textbf{\citet{Kurzban2013}} account for the phenomenology: the sensation of mental effort is the output of mechanisms that measure the opportunity cost of continuing with the present task rather than the next-best one.

Ackerman is this paper's chief interlocutor throughout (Sections 2.6, 3.4 and 7.1), which makes the first of the three the closest. The re-typing proposed here does not correct that tradition. It completes a line the field's own measurement programme began and then did not carry into the definition of the construct.

\textbf{What remains separable.} All three accounts allocate \emph{attention or control during task engagement} ---\hspace{0pt} Kanfer and Ackerman's distal component allocates prospectively between tasks, so "momentary" would be the wrong word for it, but the currency in every case is engagement with a task at hand. The present proposal allocates something else: \textbf{offline simulation time in an explicit self-model, across developmental time.} The resource is not attention within a task but the running of a model of situations that are not present, over years, and the thing it builds is the stored content of Section 3.1's second component. That is a different resource on a different timescale, and it is what makes the allocation a claim about intellectual \emph{development} rather than about task performance.

Two further separations hold, and one commonly-offered separation does not. None of the three re-types the intelligence construct: motivation remains, in each, an input to performance rather than one of the things intelligence is made of. And none derives consequences for the \textbf{measurement of the allocated disposition itself} ---\hspace{0pt} attenuation under narrow single-occasion sampling, consistency rather than level as the predictive statistic ---\hspace{0pt} which is what Section 3.3 takes from the re-typing and what Sections 7.2 and 7.3 put at risk. What is \emph{not} available is the claim that these accounts derive no psychometric consequences at all: Kanfer and Ackerman's paper is an aptitude-treatment-interaction study whose central deliverable is a set of predictions about ability-performance correlations across practice, and it sits on Ackerman's own programme concerning the attenuation of those correlations with automatization. The vacancy this paper fills is narrower than "psychometrics".

\subsubsection{Precedents in the intelligence literature}

Several researchers have proposed models that move in this direction, and the recursive structure itself has been formalized before.

\citeauthor{Sternberg2019}'s \citeyearpar{Sternberg2019} concept of ``adaptive intelligence'' emphasizes the role of goals and purpose in intelligent behavior. More recently, \citet{Sternberg2021} proposed the concept of ``meta-intelligence''---\hspace{0pt}intelligence that operates on itself. Meta-intelligence is a control layer that selects among creative, analytical, practical and wisdom-based approaches to a problem, not a loop through which capacity raises itself. The recursion proposed here operates on the components rather than on the choice between approaches, and it carries motivation inside the loop. \citeauthor{Dweck2006}'s \citeyearpar{Dweck2006} work on mindset indicates that beliefs about the malleability of intelligence affect effort investment---\hspace{0pt}a finding consistent with motivation being constitutive of the intelligence development process, though equally accommodated by treating it as an external moderator; the intervention record is examined in Section 6.1.

The ``investment traits'' literature \citep{Ziegler2012,vonStummAckerman2013} has documented that personality traits related to intellectual engagement---\hspace{0pt}curiosity, openness to experience, need for cognition---\hspace{0pt}predict intellectual development beyond what cognitive ability alone predicts. \Citet{vonStummChamorroPremuzic2011} showed in a meta-analysis that ``hungry minds''---\hspace{0pt}individuals high in intellectual curiosity---\hspace{0pt}attain greater academic performance than cognitive ability alone predicts. These findings are consistent with the recursive model: investment traits are proxies for the motivation component, and their predictive power for long-term intellectual development is exactly what the recursive model predicts.

\citet{Murayama2013} demonstrated that intrinsic motivation predicted mathematics achievement growth over several years, even controlling for prior achievement and intelligence---\hspace{0pt}direct evidence that motivation contributes to intellectual development beyond cognitive ability. The transmission of motivational states across educational contexts \citep{Schiefele2017} and the meta-analytic findings of \citet{Vu2024} document the bidirectional relationship between motivation and achievement that the recursive model formalizes.

Several other frameworks approach the territory of the recursive model without completing the integration. Ackerman's (1996, 2018) PPIK theory (Process, Personality, Interests, Knowledge) comes closest to the present proposal: it explicitly models how personality traits and interests direct the Gf-to-Gc investment process, and its empirical program has demonstrated that these non-cognitive factors predict intellectual development beyond cognitive ability alone. The recursive model can be understood as extending PPIK by treating motivation not as a moderator of the investment process but as the allocation policy that governs it ---\hspace{0pt} a step that changes the formal structure of the model rather than merely adding predictors to it. \citet{WittmannSuss1999} established the Brunswik symmetry framework for these path relationships, and \citet{Wittmann2002} applied it to motivation directly: his path-analytic model, which explained approximately 50\% of variance in dynamic task performance, showed intelligence-as-knowledge as the strongest direct predictor, with motivational variables contributing primarily via knowledge acquisition ---\hspace{0pt} motivation's effect on complex performance being largely indirect, mediated through knowledge rather than acting directly on performance. This is precisely the M $\rightarrow$ K $\rightarrow$ Performance pathway that the recursive model formalizes. \citet{WittmannHattrup2004} further showed that risk-taking ---\hspace{0pt} a motivational disposition ---\hspace{0pt} mediates the relationship between intelligence and performance in dynamic systems by generating new learning opportunities, providing empirical evidence for the exploration-exploitation dynamic that the recursive loop predicts. Similarly, \citeauthor{Duckworth2007}'s \citeyearpar{Duckworth2007} ``grit'' and \citeauthor{Stanovich2016}'s \citeyearpar{Stanovich2016} Rationality Quotient each capture what IQ misses ---\hspace{0pt} including the drive to engage effortful processing ---\hspace{0pt} but frame these as separate constructs standing outside intelligence rather than as anything internal to it. \citeauthor{Snow1996}'s \citeyearpar{Snow1996} cognitive-conative-affective framework acknowledged the interdependence of cognition and motivation in learning, but remained in educational psychology and was never integrated into mainstream intelligence theory. Notably, Ackerman's own empirical work on trait complexes points toward the recursive model's conclusion without reaching it: personality traits, he finds, "are less influential as independent predictors of learning and knowledge" but "in conjunction with other trait families, jointly influence the outcomes" (Ackerman, 2018, p. 9). This is an empirical observation of exactly the system-level dynamics the recursive model formalizes ---\hspace{0pt} the components are not additive predictors but interacting elements of a recursive system.

The dynamic systems approach provides deeper theoretical support. \Citet{vanGeert2020} argues that constructs like intelligence must be understood as ``temporary process stabilities'' rather than fixed traits; \citet{SternbergAdaptive2021} argues that intelligence is not a personal trait but emerges from person $\times$ task $\times$ situation interaction. What the present framework adds to these process accounts is specificity: it identifies the three components (Knowledge, Performance, Motivation), their recursive interaction, and the temporal dynamics by which these interactions compound over the lifespan.

A parallel lineage in computational cognitive science reached the constitutive claim from the direction of agent architecture, not psychometrics. \citeauthor{Dorner1999}'s Psi-theory \citep{Dorner1999,DornerGuss2013} models the psyche as an integrated system in which needs and drives are not a bolt-on module but an intrinsic aspect of cognition: a system stripped of need-pressure would lack direction and purpose. \citeauthor{Bach2009}'s MicroPsi, whose defining description is an architecture of motivated cognition \citep{Bach2009,Bach2015}, builds motivation into the substrate as the mechanism that selects goals and steers perception and reasoning. \citeauthor{Sun2009}'s CLARION \citep{Sun2009} makes the motivational subsystem one of four core pillars, holding that motivational representations are necessary for a comprehensive computational account of cognition; and the tradition descends from an early necessity argument, \citeauthor{SlomanCroucher1981}'s \citeyearpar{SlomanCroucher1981} claim that motive-driven processing is unavoidable in any resource-bounded intelligent agent. The recursive model converges with this tradition on the core point that motivation is constitutive of, not merely correlated with, intelligent cognition, and differs from it in two respects. The first is scope: these are architectures for situated \emph{agents}, whereas the present proposal brings the constitutive-motivation claim into the theory and measurement of \emph{intelligence} itself, the domain where, as Section~2 documents, motivation has been treated as an external predictor at best. The second is in what is asked of the claim: these architectures implement motivated cognition, whereas the present model uses the constitutive premise to generate measurement predictions about human data---\hspace{0pt}the Brunswik-symmetry aggregation prediction of Section~3.1 and the subtest-composition test of Section~7.3. The lineage supplies the architectural argument; what is added here is a set of consequences that can be checked against existing psychometric records. This same tradition also bears on the paper's claim about artificial intelligence (Section~5): a research programme that spent decades building \emph{motivated} cognitive architectures implies, by its own emphasis, that systems lacking a motivational layer are missing something architecturally consequential, not merely undertrained.

The closest formal precedents are not in the personality-intelligence literature at all. \Citet{vanderMaas2006} derived the positive manifold from \emph{mutualism}: a set of initially uncorrelated cognitive processes that reinforce one another's growth during development will produce a general factor without any general cause, and the resulting dynamics reproduce the hierarchical structure that factor analysis recovers. \Citet{DickensFlynn2001} formalized reciprocal causation between IQ and environment, showing that a multiplier---\hspace{0pt}in which a small initial advantage attracts a better-matched environment, which raises ability further, which attracts a better environment again---\hspace{0pt}reconciles high heritability estimates with the large environmental effects the Flynn effect demonstrates. \Citet{Savi2019} extended the network account into an explicitly developmental wiring model. These are the direct precedents for the present proposal, and the resemblance is close: each is a positive-feedback account of intellectual development in which compounding rather than fixed capacity does the explanatory work.

The recursive model should therefore not be read as discovering recursion in intelligence. That work is done, it is formalized, and it is two decades old. What is claimed here is narrower and comes to three points. First, the quantity that the mutualism and multiplier models leave implicit---\hspace{0pt}what determines how much of the reciprocal process actually runs, and for how long---\hspace{0pt}is motivation, and it belongs inside the system rather than in the environment term. Dickens and Flynn's multiplier operates through environment matching, which presupposes an agent that seeks out and holds onto matched environments; that seeking is the M component, and in their model it is a parameter rather than a construct. Second, motivation's apparent fragmentation across the personality-intelligence literature is a measurement artifact rather than a structural fact (Section~3.1)---\hspace{0pt}a claim about instruments that neither mutualism nor the multiplier model makes, and one that is testable independently of either. Third, the resulting programme is a measurement programme with stated falsifiers (Sections~7.2 and 7.3) rather than a further simulation study. Where van der Maas et~al. supply functional forms that the present paper does not (Section~7.4), the natural next step is to add M as a node to a mutualism network and ask whether the empirical signatures set out below follow---\hspace{0pt}a study this paper motivates rather than performs.

What none of these contributions supplies is the combination this paper assembles: a recursive account in which motivation is one of the interacting components, together with the measurement consequences that placing it there entails---\hspace{0pt}the Brunswik-symmetry aggregation prediction, the consistency-over-intensity prediction, and the subtest partition of Section~7.3. The system-level regularities the model is asked to accommodate, the Matthew effect and the Gf-Gc divergence among them, are accommodated by the mutualism and multiplier accounts as well; they are not what distinguishes it.

The loop turns on cognitive learning---\hspace{0pt}the induction of general theories from particular observations, as against reinforcement over particular outcomes---\hspace{0pt}so something must supply the capacity to construct a model of a situation that is not present and run it. That is a question about architecture, not psychometrics, and nothing below turns on the answer. The author's own answer, developed in a companion paper on consciousness \citep{Gruber2026}, is that the capacity arises from a specific architecture of explicit self-modeling. It is one candidate. A reader who rejects it, or prefers another, can accept everything the recursive model claims and predicts.


\section{Operational Knowledge: The Hidden Multiplier}

\subsection{What Operational Knowledge Is}

Within the Knowledge component, two categories are distinguished:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Factual knowledge}: Knowledge of content---\hspace{0pt}facts, concepts, procedures, cultural repertoire. This is what IQ tests (and educational systems) primarily measure under the rubric of ``crystallized intelligence.''

\item \textbf{Operational knowledge} (\emph{Metawissen}): Knowledge about \emph{how to learn and think}---\hspace{0pt}learning strategies, reasoning heuristics, metacognitive skills, strategic planning, logical tools, and the ability to evaluate one's own understanding. This is sometimes subsumed under the label ``metacognition'' \citep{Flavell1979} or ``self-regulated learning'' \citep{Zimmerman2002}, but these labels do not fully capture the concept, which extends to include general-purpose reasoning strategies and logical tools that are not domain-specific.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why Operational Knowledge Is the Multiplier}

Operational knowledge occupies a special position in the recursive loop because it amplifies the \emph{rate} of knowledge acquisition. Factual knowledge scaffolds further learning mainly within its own domain: a store of chess positions accelerates chess, not chemistry. Operational knowledge is multiplicative: learning a new learning strategy improves the efficiency of all subsequent learning.

Consider a concrete example. A student who learns the technique of \emph{spaced repetition} (distributing practice over time rather than massing it) does not merely acquire one new fact. She acquires a tool that increases the retention rate of all subsequent learning. This is a fundamentally different kind of knowledge---\hspace{0pt}it is knowledge that accelerates the recursive loop itself.

The intelligence literature has noted the existence of metacognitive skills and learning strategies. But it has rarely applied this insight. The distinction between factual and operational knowledge is noted and then set aside---\hspace{0pt}treated as a curiosity rather than as the structurally decisive feature it is. In the present framework, operational knowledge is the transmission gear of the recursive loop: it determines how efficiently the turning of one component (Motivation $\rightarrow$ effort) translates into the turning of another (Knowledge $\rightarrow$ capability).

\subsection{Implications for the AI Age}

This distinction becomes acutely consequential in the age of artificial intelligence. When factual knowledge is instantly available to anyone with an internet connection, and when computational performance is available for the cost of an API call, the relative importance of the three components shifts dramatically.

Factual knowledge, which educational systems have traditionally spent most of their time transmitting, is no longer scarce. Performance, in the narrow sense of computational processing, is no longer exclusively biological. What remains scarce---\hspace{0pt}absent from the systems in which knowledge and performance have been scaled (Section~5)---\hspace{0pt}is the combination of intrinsic motivation and operational knowledge: the drive to learn \emph{and} the meta-skill of knowing how to learn effectively.

If this analysis is correct, it implies that the most valuable thing an educational system can transmit is not factual knowledge but operational knowledge---\hspace{0pt}the strategies, heuristics, and metacognitive skills that allow a learner to learn independently. In the AI age, \emph{learning how to learn} is close to the only thing still worth teaching.


\section{The AI Implication: What Machines Are Missing}

The recursive model makes a specific prediction about artificial intelligence systems: they should exhibit a characteristic failure mode in which high Knowledge and high Performance do not produce the self-directed development that characterizes human intelligence.

Current artificial intelligence systems provide an unintended test case for the recursive model ---\hspace{0pt} one that allows us to examine, facet by facet, what happens when Knowledge and Performance are scaled to superhuman levels while Motivation remains entirely absent.

\subsection{A Facet-by-Facet Comparison}

Consider the major facets of the CHC taxonomy and how current AI systems perform on each:

\textbf{Memory and retrieval.} Large language models (LLMs) trained on trillions of tokens have access to a store of factual knowledge that dwarfs any individual human's. In terms of sheer breadth of stored information, AI is unambiguously superior. This corresponds to the Knowledge component of the recursive model---\hspace{0pt}and it is precisely the component that can be supplied externally, without recursion.

\textbf{Processing speed.} AI systems process information orders of magnitude faster than biological neural networks. Where a human immediate memory span is roughly 7 ± 2 items \citep{Miller1956} and processing cycles are measured in hundreds of milliseconds, LLMs ingest and generate text orders of magnitude faster. This corresponds to the Performance component---\hspace{0pt}and again, it can be engineered without recursion.

\textbf{Reasoning.} This is where the comparison becomes interesting. A generation of ``reasoning models'' (e.g., OpenAI's o1 and o3 series, released 2024--2025) achieve performance on mathematical competition problems, graduate-level science exams, and complex logical tasks that would have been considered out of reach shortly before. Yet these systems exhibit systematic failures that are structurally revealing. They ``hallucinate''---\hspace{0pt}generating confident but false outputs that superficially resemble correct reasoning. These hallucinations bear a striking resemblance to what \citet{Kahneman2011} described as System~1 errors: fast, associative processing that produces plausible-sounding but incorrect results. Human intelligence addresses such errors through System~2---\hspace{0pt}slow, deliberate, effortful reasoning that monitors and corrects System~1 outputs. But System~2 is itself driven by motivation: the willingness to invest cognitive effort in checking one's own outputs, to tolerate the discomfort of uncertainty, to persist in reasoning when the easy answer is available. \citet{Gigerenzer2007} has argued that the boundary between ``error'' and ``adaptive heuristic'' depends on the ecology in which the reasoning occurs---\hspace{0pt}a point that underscores the role of the agent's goals and motivations in determining what counts as intelligent behavior in context. The ``reasoning'' in reasoning models is not a structural innovation but a quantitative one: these systems use the same transformer architecture as their predecessors, trained via reinforcement learning to produce longer chain-of-thought sequences before generating a final answer. More computation per response---\hspace{0pt}more tokens of intermediate ``thinking''---\hspace{0pt}but no qualitative change in the underlying neural architecture, though the inference procedure is significantly modified. The improvement is real but reveals its nature under pressure: the system allocates more processing to a problem, not different processing. AI systems can also be engineered to ``check their work'' through chain-of-thought prompting and verification loops, but these are externally imposed scaffolds---\hspace{0pt}surrogate motivation supplied by the human designer, not endogenous drive arising from the system itself.

\textbf{Motivation.} Here the comparison is one of kind, not degree---\hspace{0pt}but the usual form of the claim, that artificial systems have no motivation at all, is false. A substantial research programme has engineered exactly this. \Citet{Schmidhuber1991} proposed implementing curiosity and boredom directly in a model-building controller, rewarding the agent for improvement in its own world model; \citet{Oudeyer2007} provided a typology of the resulting computational approaches; \citet{Pathak2017} showed that an intrinsic reward defined as prediction error in a learned feature space is sufficient to drive exploration in the complete absence of external reward. In the active-inference framework the point is stronger still: epistemic value---\hspace{0pt}the expected reduction in uncertainty---\hspace{0pt}falls out of the objective itself rather than being added to it, so that exploration is constitutive of inference and not an extra term \citep{Friston2015}. A theorist working in either tradition reads the claim that motivation must sit inside the model of cognition as something their framework already asserts.

Two things follow. The paper's thesis is not novel \emph{as a claim about artificial agents}; it is novel as a claim about the theory and measurement of human intelligence, where, as Section~2 documents, the exclusion is real. And what these systems supply is an exploration drive over a fixed task distribution, while the deployed systems whose behaviour Section~5.2 describes---\hspace{0pt}the large language models now doing the work that prompts comparisons with human intelligence---\hspace{0pt}carry none. Between queries they do nothing: they do not seek out information, practise skills, or return to a problem left unresolved. The gap is therefore not that intrinsic motivation is unengineerable, but that the systems in which Knowledge and Performance have been scaled to superhuman levels are not the systems in which motivation has been engineered, and the two research programmes have not been joined at scale.

\subsection{The Predicted Failure Mode}

According to the recursive model, a system in which the M component is not present should not sustain the loop, whatever the levels of K and P. In deployed language models this is what is observed: they do not improve themselves between training runs, do not independently seek out areas of ignorance and address them, and do not show progressive intellectual development over time. Their capability is static across sessions---\hspace{0pt}whatever adaptation occurs within a context does not carry beyond it---\hspace{0pt}with no endogenous drive to extend it.

On its own the observation is weak: a purely architectural account predicts it equally well, since these systems have no persistent state between sessions, so nothing could accumulate even if something were driving it. The content is in the conditional. The recursive model predicts that supplying an intrinsic drive of the kind Section~5.1 describes to a system that \emph{does} have persistent state and the capacity to modify itself will not, by itself, produce a self-sustaining developmental trajectory---\hspace{0pt}that the resulting system will explore, and will improve on the distribution it explores, without the open-ended compounding across domains that the loop is meant to describe. If such a system does compound in that way, the model's claim about what the M component contributes is wrong, and the loop is doing less work than claimed. This is a prediction about an experiment that is buildable now, not a claim about what machines can never do.

The reasoning models sharpen this point. These systems solve competition-level problems when prompted but do not independently seek out problems, do not self-direct their learning, and require external scaffolding---\hspace{0pt}prompts, reinforcement learning from human feedback, reward signals---\hspace{0pt}that functions as a surrogate for the absent motivation component. Scaling Performance and Knowledge to extraordinary levels produces extraordinary outputs on demand, but not the self-sustaining developmental trajectory that defines intelligence.

The absence of operational knowledge integration provides a further illustration. A human learner who discovers a more effective reasoning strategy can immediately apply it to subsequent problems within the same cognitive session---\hspace{0pt}the operational knowledge becomes part of the active processing, and stays there. Current language models do the first half of this: a strategy derived at one turn conditions the model's outputs at later turns of the same context, with no external scaffolding and no restart. What they do not do is the second half. The strategy is held in the context rather than written back into the system, so its life is the life of that context; when the context ends, so does it, and the next session starts where the last one started. External scaffolding can carry a strategy forward by modifying the instructions given to the model, but the cost of maintaining it then sits outside the system rather than inside it, and each restart produces, in a meaningful sense, a new system---\hspace{0pt}one that has no continuity of experience with its predecessor. The human case is one in which the strategy is retained; the machine case is one in which it is rented. This is the opposite of the recursive loop, in which operational knowledge acquired through motivated engagement continuously reshapes the cognitive process that produced it.

\subsection{A Boundary Condition, and What It Is Not}

The preceding argument is about a component and its consequences for a developmental trajectory. It says nothing about what a system's architecture must be like for that component to be present. The author holds a view on that question; the rest of this paper does not require it.

The view is this. As argued in Section~3.1, what the recursive loop draws on is not any single construct from the personality-intelligence literature but a unified evaluative function, and evaluating one's own states in the way that function describes appears to presuppose a model of oneself that can be run and inspected. In the architectural account developed separately \citep{Gruber2026}, that is what an explicit self-model is, and the conjecture is that motivation of the kind the loop needs is not economically supplied as a module bolted to a system that lacks one ---\hspace{0pt} the expectation is that any bolt-on route pays a cost the self-model route does not, not that no such route exists. Reinforcement learning from human feedback would then be the clear negative case: it shapes behaviour toward outputs humans rate as helpful without generating any endogenous evaluative process, and a system so shaped does not want to be helpful in any sense the loop can use.

Two reasons to treat the conjecture as separable. It rests on a theoretical framework that is the present author's own and has not been independently tested---\hspace{0pt}a poor foundation for a claim in intelligence research, whoever had proposed it. And it is not needed: every empirical prediction in Section~7 concerns human data, and none turns on whether the evaluative function requires a self-model or can be engineered without one. If a system with no self-model of any kind sustains the loop, this subsection is refuted and the recursive model is untouched.

What the recursive model does claim about artificial systems is the narrower thing stated in Section~5.2: that supplying an exploration drive is not the same as supplying the M component, and that the difference should show up as the absence of open-ended compounding. That claim is testable without settling the architectural question at all.


\section{The Learnability of Intelligence}

\subsection{A Structural Prediction}

The recursive model yields an implication that is, in my view, more consequential than the AI case: it predicts that intelligence is, to a large extent, \emph{learnable}.

It is a structural prediction that follows from the composition of the model's three constituents. Consider each in turn:

\textbf{Knowledge is entirely learnable.} This is true by definition. Knowledge---\hspace{0pt}both factual and operational---\hspace{0pt}is the accumulated product of learning. No one is born knowing calculus or knowing how to use spaced repetition. The entire Knowledge component, including the operational knowledge that functions as the system's multiplier (Section~4), is acquired through experience, instruction, and practice.

\textbf{Motivation is substantially learnable.} Self-Determination Theory \citep{DeciRyan2000} demonstrates that intrinsic motivation is not a fixed trait but a response to environmental conditions ---\hspace{0pt} specifically, to the satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs. Environments that support these needs cultivate intrinsic motivation; environments that thwart them extinguish it. \citeauthor{Dweck2006}'s \citeyearpar{Dweck2006} work on growth mindset shows that beliefs about the malleability of intelligence ---\hspace{0pt} themselves a form of knowledge ---\hspace{0pt} directly affect motivational persistence: students who believe intelligence is developable invest more effort and recover more effectively from failure. These beliefs are teachable \citep{YeagerDweck2012}. However, the empirical record is more complex than the initial enthusiasm suggested. \citet{MacnamaraBurgoyne2023}, in a comprehensive meta-analysis, found that growth mindset interventions produced negligible effects on academic achievement (\emph{d} = 0.05, a result that became non-significant once publication bias was accounted for), with independent researchers finding significantly smaller effects than researchers with financial ties to mindset theory. This is consistent with the recursive model: a belief about intelligence is the cheapest part of the loop to move, and moving it alone buys the least. The loop compounds through iterations that pay, and an iteration pays through the operational knowledge that makes an attempt productive; an intervention that changes what a learner is willing to attempt, without changing what the attempt returns, is priced accordingly. The failure of mindset interventions, far from undermining the importance of motivation, illustrates what happens when motivation is treated as an add-on rather than as the policy governing a recursive system. Motivation is not purely innate temperament; it is shaped, for better or worse, by the environments and feedback systems that surround the learner ---\hspace{0pt} but effective intervention must engage the full loop, not merely one belief within one component.

\textbf{Performance has a biological ceiling---\hspace{0pt}but the ceiling on the measure sits above the ceiling on the substrate, and neither is usually the binding constraint.} Cognitive processing capacity is the component most strongly influenced by genetics and neurobiology. Working memory capacity, processing speed, and the raw computational power of the neural substrate are not infinitely malleable, and the heritability of fluid ability in adulthood is among the better-replicated findings in the field. Three points qualify what follows from this, and the first two dispute none of it. First, heritability describes the sources of variance in a population across the range of environments that population happened to occupy. It does not fix the standing of any individual, and it does not establish that the observed range of environments is the possible range. Second, a fluid-reasoning score is not a direct read-out of substrate capacity. Reasoning with unfamiliar material requires constructing a model of the problem that the examinee does not have stored and then running it, and model construction is something a person becomes better at. To the extent that a Gf test scores that construction alongside the substrate carrying it, measured Gf is part capacity and part skill. This reading accommodates two findings that a pure-capacity account handles awkwardly: working memory training produces gains that are reliable on trained and near tasks while far transfer remains contested (\citealp{Jaeggi2008}; though see \citealp[][for debate about transfer]{MelbyLervag2013}), which is what one expects if what improves is a constructed procedure rather than a capacity, and expertise routinely allows individuals to circumvent apparent working memory limits through chunking and automatization \citep{ChaseSimon1973}. Neither finding shows that the substrate is elastic. What they show is that the measured quantity is not only the substrate. Third---\hspace{0pt}and this is the decisive point---\hspace{0pt}for the vast majority of people, Performance is \emph{not the bottleneck}. Average cognitive processing capacity is more than sufficient to support what most people would recognize as highly intelligent behavior in real-world contexts. The bottleneck, for most people most of the time, is Knowledge and Motivation.

The intervention record bears on the learnability claim directly. The education and working-memory-training contrast of Section 3.3, where the reasons the two literatures cannot be compared on their headline numbers are given, also locates \emph{which} component the learnable part of intelligence lives in. Among the most secure causal results in the field is that schooling raises measured intelligence ---\hspace{0pt} in the designs that control prior intelligence, roughly one point per year of education on adult outcomes, and on fluid tests in adults an effect of similar size that diminishes with age \citep{RitchieTuckerDrob2018}. The intervention that targets Performance directly, drilling working memory itself, produces ---\hspace{0pt} against controls matched for engagement and expectancy ---\hspace{0pt} no far transfer to nonverbal ability, and the point estimate at delayed follow-up is negative \citep{MelbyLervag2016}. If measured intelligence were learnable the way a capacity is trainable, the ordering should run the other way: the direct drill should outperform the diffuse institutional treatment, since schooling spends little of its time on anything resembling capacity exercise.

The recursive model predicts the observed ordering, because what the two interventions touch differs in kind, not in dose. A year of schooling adds stored content, teaches some of the operational knowledge of Section 4, and ---\hspace{0pt} for hours a day, across years, under an externally held schedule ---\hspace{0pt} points the allocation policy at material the learner would not have chosen. In the observatory's terms it is a year of scheduled telescope time: the archive grows, and the habit of the schedule is trained along with it. Polishing the optics changes neither. That the schooling effect is estimated on outcomes years downstream while the training effect has vanished by follow-up is then not incidental: what education deposits ---\hspace{0pt} content, procedure, allocation habit ---\hspace{0pt} persists in structure, while a drilled proxy score decays with the practice that maintained it. Even the fluid-test effect fits the decomposition given above: if a Gf score is part substrate and part learned model construction, an intervention rich in guided model construction should move it modestly, and without any claim on the substrate ---\hspace{0pt} which is also one reading of the effect's shrinkage with age. The learnable part of intelligence is the loop, and the interventions that move the measure are the ones that reach it.

\subsection{Why Performance Is Not the Bottleneck}

The claim runs counter to the implicit assumption of the psychometric tradition ---\hspace{0pt} that intelligence differences are primarily differences in cognitive processing capacity.

Consider the distribution of Gf in the general population. The difference between the 25th and 75th percentile in working memory capacity is real but modest. It means that one person can hold, roughly, one more chunk of information in mind than another. This difference matters at the extremes---\hspace{0pt}in theoretical physics, in certain forms of mathematical proof, in any task that requires holding several unfamiliar relations in mind at once. But it matters far less in the contexts where most people live their lives: in learning a profession, in solving practical problems, in understanding complex arguments, in acquiring expertise in a domain of interest.

What separates the person who becomes expert in their field from the person who remains a novice is overwhelmingly not a difference in working memory capacity. It is a difference in accumulated knowledge (including operational knowledge---\hspace{0pt}knowing how to learn effectively), sustained over time by a difference in motivation. The expert has iterated the recursive loop thousands of times; the novice stopped iterating early. The recursive model predicts that this difference in iteration count---\hspace{0pt}driven primarily by K and M, not P---\hspace{0pt}should dominate real-world outcomes, and within-domain evidence bears this out: expert-novice differences are differences of store, not span \citep{ChaseSimon1973}.

A person of average cognitive processing capacity who is deeply motivated and who possesses strong operational knowledge will, over a lifetime, develop intellectual capabilities that far exceed those of a person with superior processing capacity but low motivation and poor learning strategies. The recursive loop is a compound interest machine, and compound interest cares more about the rate of deposit (Motivation) and the investment strategy (operational Knowledge) than about the initial principal (Performance).

A caveat is necessary here: this argument applies to the broad middle of the cognitive distribution. At the extremes---\hspace{0pt}individuals with significant cognitive impairments, or tasks requiring exceptional processing capacity (theoretical physics, certain classes of mathematical proof)---\hspace{0pt}Performance does become the binding constraint. Expert chess, which looks like the obvious further example, is not one. What distinguishes grandmasters is the store of recognizable positions that lets them work around the working memory limit rather than exceed it, which is why their advantage in recalling a position all but disappears when the pieces are placed at random \citep{ChaseSimon1973}. That is a difference in Knowledge, not in Performance. The recursive model does not deny the reality of individual differences in Gf, and it does not treat them as wholly acquired. It argues that the loop amplifies K and M differences far more than P differences across the lifespan, and that some part of the P difference measured in adulthood is itself an output of that amplification rather than an input to it. For the broad middle of the distribution---\hspace{0pt}which is where most people are---\hspace{0pt}Performance is sufficient. It is K and M that determine the trajectory.

\subsection{Grading as an Intervention on the Motivation Component}

If intelligence is largely learnable, and if the recursive loop is driven primarily by Motivation and Knowledge, an implication follows for practice. A practice that reduces motivation in learners is, on this model, not merely failing to develop intelligence; it is acting on the component that determines how often the loop iterates, and the model predicts that such an intervention will have consequences that outlast the occasion. Conventional grading is the practice this paper is most concerned with, and the argument for treating it this way is set out here as a prediction of the model rather than as an established finding.

Consider what happens when a child receives a poor grade. The grade is presented as a measurement---\hspace{0pt}an objective assessment of ability or performance. Through the lens of the recursive model it is also an intervention on the Motivation component: it communicates \emph{you are not good enough at this}, which for a child who has not yet acquired a robust account of ability as developable translates readily into \emph{you are not intelligent enough}. The prediction is that self-efficacy falls, that intrinsic motivation to engage with the subject falls with it, and that the willingness to invest effort---\hspace{0pt}the fuel of the loop---\hspace{0pt}is reduced.

The structural consequence is what makes the claim more than a restatement of a familiar complaint. A poor grade reduces M. Reduced M means fewer iterations of the loop. Fewer iterations mean slower growth in K. Slower growth in K means worse performance on subsequent assessments, and worse performance means more poor grades. Where the loop runs forward as compounding growth, here it runs in the other direction, and the assessment system is then not only measuring an outcome but contributing to the outcome it measures---\hspace{0pt}a self-fulfilling prophecy in the technical sense \citep{Merton1948}.

The evidence bearing on this is more equivocal than accounts of it usually allow, and two constraints have to be carried, not passed over. The first concerns the teacher-expectancy pathway. \citeauthor{RosenthalJacobson1968}'s \citeyearpar{RosenthalJacobson1968} Pygmalion study has been criticized on methodological grounds, and while later work confirms that teacher expectations do influence student outcomes \citep{Rosenthal2002}, \citeauthor{JussimHarber2005}'s \citeyearpar{JussimHarber2005} review of three decades of research concludes that such effects are typically small, that they do not accumulate greatly across teachers or over time and may be more likely to dissipate than to accumulate, and that teacher expectations predict student outcomes substantially because those expectations are \emph{accurate} rather than because they are self-fulfilling. That conclusion is in direct tension with the compounding prediction of Section~6.4, and the tension should be acknowledged as such: for the expectancy pathway specifically, the best available synthesis reports the opposite of compounding. The second constraint concerns stereotype threat \citep{SteeleAronson1995}, which is often cited as the same mechanism running from the other direction; the size and robustness of that effect have since been contested on replication and publication-bias grounds, and it cannot be treated as settled support.

Two readings survive these constraints. The weaker is that the compounding claim is simply wrong. The stronger, and the one the model commits to, is that grading acts through a different route than teacher expectancy: not another person's belief about the child transmitted through differential treatment, but a repeated, explicit, institutionally sanctioned signal delivered to the child about the child's own standing, which is the pathway Self-Determination Theory identifies as competence-need frustration \citep{DeciRyan2000}. These are separable pathways with separable predictions, and the model's claim about grading should not be allowed to borrow support from the expectancy literature that the expectancy literature does not give.

The re-typing specifies what a signal of this kind does. Under the policy reading, a grade does not merely lower the level of a motivational quantity; it re-points the allocation policy ---\hspace{0pt} away from the subject matter and onto the evaluation. A graded classroom tells the learner, repeatedly and with the institution's authority, that the thing to allocate toward is the assessment, and allocation follows: study directed at the test rather than the material, effort withdrawn where the expected grade is poor, engagement priced in the evaluation's currency. This is also what separates grading from the one-shot motivational manipulations whose record Section 6.1 examines. The mindset null \citep{MacnamaraBurgoyne2023} is not a counterexample to the present claim but its calibration: a single message about ability moves a momentary willingness, and the model prices that near zero, because a schedule that any single message could re-set would not be a schedule. What changes a policy is what recurs. Grading recurs ---\hspace{0pt} term after term, subject after subject, across a school career ---\hspace{0pt} and it is sanctioned by the institution whose other signals the child is learning to trust. The contrast with the mindset result is therefore one of duration and repetition, not of magnitude: the graded signal is no stronger on any one occasion than an intervention message; it is delivered on an institutional schedule, and a schedule is the kind of instrument that can rewrite another schedule.

The claim is testable, and the quasi-experimental variation already exists. Schools and systems differ in when graded assessment begins and in whether it is used at all: Montessori and other mastery-oriented programmes replace ranked grading with criterion-referenced progression; several national systems delay numerical grading into the middle school years while comparable systems do not; and individual districts have shifted grading policy at datable moments. The prediction stated in Section~6.4---\hspace{0pt}divergence that widens with time since exposure rather than a constant offset---\hspace{0pt}is what distinguishes the recursive account from the alternative that grading simply sorts children who were already diverging. Testing it requires following the same cohorts across several years and comparing the \emph{shape} of the trajectory, not its level at any one point.


\subsection{Compounding Effects: A Structural Prediction}

The recursive model makes a specific, testable prediction about the time course of these effects: motivation-destroying interventions should produce damage that \emph{compounds} over time, not damage that remains static. A single bad grade in first grade does not merely reduce motivation in first grade; it slightly reduces the rate at which the loop iterates, which produces a slightly smaller knowledge base by second grade, which produces slightly worse performance in second grade, which produces another discouraging signal, and so on. The recursive structure predicts that early motivational damage should be visible as an accelerating divergence from peers---\hspace{0pt}a fanning out of trajectories that grows wider with each passing year.

Conversely, the model predicts that motivation-enhancing interventions---\hspace{0pt}environments that support autonomy, competence, and relatedness \citep{DeciRyan2000}; feedback systems that emphasize growth rather than fixed ability \citep{Dweck2006}; educational practices that teach operational knowledge explicitly \citep{DignathButtner2008}---\hspace{0pt}should produce benefits that \emph{compound} over time. An intervention that boosts M in first grade should show larger effects at five-year follow-up than at one-year follow-up, because the additional loop iterations accumulate.

Some evidence supports this compounding pattern. \citeauthor{Heckman2006}'s \citeyearpar{Heckman2006} analysis of early childhood interventions, including the Perry Preschool Project, shows returns that grow over time. The Perry treatment group had IQ scores no higher than the control group by age 10, yet in follow-ups to age 40 showed higher rates of high school graduation, higher salaries, higher rates of home ownership, lower rates of welfare assistance and fewer arrests. Heckman attributes the divergence not to persisting cognitive gains---\hspace{0pt}those faded---\hspace{0pt}but to the treated children being ``more motivated to learn,'' a motivational and self-regulatory gain that compounds through subsequent learning. This is precisely what a recursive model predicts and precisely what a static-trait model does not.

\subsection{Implications for Educational Practice}

If the recursive model is correct, the most effective educational interventions are not those that maximize the transmission of factual knowledge (the current default), nor those that attempt to boost raw cognitive processing capacity (which has limited malleability). The most effective interventions are those that target the two learnable components---\hspace{0pt}Knowledge (especially operational knowledge) and Motivation---\hspace{0pt}in ways designed to initiate and sustain the recursive loop.

Specifically, the model implies:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Teach operational knowledge explicitly.} Learning strategies, metacognitive skills, and reasoning heuristics should not be incidental byproducts of content instruction---\hspace{0pt}they should be a primary focus, because they function as the multiplier within the recursive loop.

\item \textbf{Treat motivation as a protected quantity.} On this model, an educational practice that reduces intrinsic motivation is acting on the component that governs how often the loop iterates, and its cost should therefore be counted over years rather than assessed at the point of delivery. Punitive grading is the case developed above; ability tracking, competitive ranking, and any practice that communicates to children that their intellectual capacity is fixed are candidates for the same treatment, though each would need testing on its own terms rather than inheriting the conclusion.

\item \textbf{Replace grades with growth feedback.} If assessment is necessary---\hspace{0pt}and it may be, for diagnostic purposes---\hspace{0pt}it should take a form that feeds the recursive loop rather than breaking it. This means feedback that is specific, actionable, and oriented toward improvement rather than toward ranking. It means communicating to learners not ``you scored 60\%'' but ``here is what you have mastered, here is what to work on next, and here is how.''

\item \textbf{Recognize that average Performance is sufficient.} Educational systems should stop treating cognitive processing capacity as the primary constraint on intellectual development. For most students, it is not. The binding constraints are motivation and operational knowledge, and both are responsive to intervention.
\end{enumerate}

None of this is entirely new. Several established educational approaches already align with the recursive model's predictions: Montessori education prioritizes autonomy and intrinsic motivation; mastery learning \citep{Bloom1968} replaces competitive grading with criterion-referenced progression that protects self-efficacy; portfolio assessment shifts the focus from summative ranking to growth documentation. What the recursive model adds is a \emph{structural explanation} for why these practices work: they target the components of the recursive loop that are both most learnable and most consequential for long-term intellectual development. The model turns what might otherwise appear to be soft pedagogical preferences into stated predictions about how the loop behaves, with the falsifiers of Sections~7.2 and~7.3 attached.


\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Relation to Established Intelligence Models}

The proposal advanced here does not require abandoning existing intelligence models. The CHC taxonomy remains a useful descriptive framework for the cognitive components of intelligence. Cattell's investment theory remains a useful account of the Gf-to-Gc developmental trajectory. What this paper proposes is that these models are \emph{incomplete}---\hspace{0pt}they describe the Performance and Knowledge components of intelligence with considerable precision while systematically excluding the Motivation component that drives the developmental dynamics.

The recursive model can be understood as an extension of Cattell's investment theory that (a) makes the investor explicit, (b) formalizes the recursive feedback structure, and (c) identifies operational knowledge as the rate-limiting factor in the loop.

The recursive model also explains a structural property of intelligence that factor-analytic approaches have difficulty accommodating: intelligence behaves as a \emph{polythetic} construct \citep{Beckner1959}. In a polythetic class, members share most but not all defining properties, and no single property is necessary or sufficient for membership. Multiple K $\times$ P $\times$ M configurations can produce equifinal outcomes---high knowledge compensates for moderate performance, sustained motivation compensates for late starts, strong operational knowledge accelerates the loop regardless of starting conditions. This is why early IQ, which samples the loop's components at a single time point, leaves substantial variance in adult intellectual achievement unexplained, and why factor-analytic models that impose a single underlying dimension systematically underfit the observed data.

Under the re-typing of Section 3.1, the polythetic character of the construct stops being an observation about intelligence and becomes a consequence of its shape. Factor analysis presupposes that the entities entering the analysis are commensurable magnitudes ---\hspace{0pt} quantities of a common kind, such that covariation across persons can be read as shared dependence on underlying amounts. The three constituents do not meet the presupposition. Performance meets it exactly: it is a capacity, bounded and present at the moment of measurement, and it is the constituent the psychometric tradition recovers most cleanly. Knowledge is a stock rather than a capacity, and a stock with two differently-behaving sides: its structural side is read well by retrieval but grows only where allocation has been pointing, so its loadings record a history rather than a magnitude. And Motivation is not a magnitude at all. A policy contributes no quantity that could covary with the other two as one trait among three; it enters the data only through its allocations, scattered across whatever context-bound instruments happened to sample it (Section 3.1). A procedure built for magnitudes, applied to one capacity, one stock and one policy, must deliver something that behaves like a family resemblance. Many K $\times$ P $\times$ M configurations reach equal outcomes, because a strong policy compensates a modest capacity and a full store compensates both ---\hspace{0pt} so no single property is necessary for membership; and none is sufficient, because each constituent is inert without the allocation that runs it through the loop. \citeauthor{Beckner1959}'s \citeyearpar{Beckner1959} polythetic classes are what remain when members are grouped by outcome while their underlying configurations vary freely, and that is precisely the grouping an intelligence test performs. Factor-analysing a season of the observatory's logs would recover aperture as a clean factor and scatter the schedule across method variance ---\hspace{0pt} not because the schedule is unreal, but because it was never a magnitude. A single-dimension model of such a system therefore underfits by type error rather than by sampling error: what its residuals contain is the mis-typing of the components, not noise.

This is consistent with recent large-cohort evidence. \Citet{Edwards2026}, analyzing two nationally representative U.S. longitudinal samples (NLSY79, \emph{n} = 11,914; NLSY97, \emph{n} = 7,008) with sibling-based within-family controls, report that specific cognitive abilities carry independent predictive weight for educational, occupational and income outcomes over and above general intelligence---\hspace{0pt}on the order of 30 to 57 percent of \emph{g}'s importance, against a conventional expectation that the residual contribution is negligible. Their sibling design removes shared family confounds. Two qualifications belong with the citation: the analysis is conducted within the classical \emph{g}-plus-group-factors psychometric tradition rather than a recursive or relational one, so it converges with the present claim without testing it; and the specific factors did not replicate cleanly across the two cohorts. What it establishes is the weaker but sufficient point that structure below the general factor carries real independent signal, which is what a single-dimension summary discards.

Independent within-person evidence indicates that this is not merely a modelling preference. \citet{Schmiedek2020}, analyzing the COGITO study---\hspace{0pt}nine cognitive tasks measured on approximately 100 occasions per participant---\hspace{0pt}found that the structure of within-person daily performance differs systematically from the between-person structure of cognitive abilities, and that the general factor recovered between persons does not describe how cognition is organized within a person. That work does not test motivation and was not conducted with the present framework in view; what it establishes is that between-person factor structure cannot be assumed to serve as a surrogate for the within-person process. This matters here because the recursive loop is a within-person process. The present model adds an account of what organizes that process: the K $\times$ P $\times$ M interaction iterating across developmental time.

The capacity the model assigns to the Performance and Motivation components---\hspace{0pt}constructing a representation of something not present and running it---\hspace{0pt}is not an import into intelligence research from outside psychology. Memory research arrived at it independently. \citet{Bartlett1932} established that remembering is reconstruction rather than replay; \citeauthor{SchacterAddis2007}'s \citeyearpar{SchacterAddis2007} constructive episodic simulation hypothesis proposes that memory is reconstructive because its function is to supply recombinable elements for the simulation of events that have not occurred; and \citet{Hassabis2007} found that patients with hippocampal amnesia were markedly impaired at imagining novel experiences, their attempts lacking spatial coherence. This is consistent with the present framework rather than a test of it. What it establishes is that remembering and prospection draw on a common constructive capacity---\hspace{0pt}the capacity the recursive loop requires, and one that no current intelligence model represents.

It would suit a process account of intelligence if the brain-network findings showed that abler individuals reconfigure their networks more flexibly under task demands. They show close to the opposite. \Citet{Hilger2017} found intelligence associated with the modular organization of \emph{intrinsic} networks; \citet{Hilger2020} found it associated with greater temporal \emph{stability} of those modules; and \citet{SchultzCole2016} found that higher intelligence predicts \emph{less} task-related network reconfiguration, not more. The picture these converge on is of a system organized efficiently enough in advance that it need not re-wire itself for each new demand.

This is not evidence against the recursive model, and it is not evidence for it. It bears on the Performance component---\hspace{0pt}the standing organization of the substrate---\hspace{0pt}on a timescale of seconds to minutes, whereas the loop is a claim about what accumulates across years. Nothing in the present framework predicts what network dynamics should look like during a single task, and a finding at one timescale is not support for a claim at the other. The recursive model's neural commitments, if it has any, remain to be derived; it currently has none (Section~7.4).

\subsection{Testable Predictions}

The recursive model generates several testable predictions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Motivation predicts long-term intellectual development beyond IQ}: Measures of intellectual curiosity and learning drive, assessed in childhood, should predict adult intellectual achievement (education, creative output, problem-solving ability) beyond what childhood IQ predicts. The existing evidence stops short of this. \Citet{vonStummChamorroPremuzic2011} is a meta-analysis of academic performance, built on a meta-analytically derived correlation matrix drawn largely from concurrent student samples; \citet{Murayama2013} follows six annual waves from Grade 5 to Grade 10 (mean baseline age 11.7) with mathematics achievement as the outcome. Both establish that motivational variables predict school-age achievement beyond intelligence, and neither carries a childhood-to-adult design or an adult outcome. The prediction over adult achievement is therefore open, and testing it requires longitudinal data of a length the literature does not yet have.

\item \textbf{Operational knowledge moderates the motivation-achievement relationship}: The effect of motivation on intellectual development should be stronger in individuals who also possess effective learning strategies. Motivation without operational knowledge produces effort without efficiency; operational knowledge without motivation produces capability without deployment.

\item \textbf{The Matthew effect should be strongest where all three components co-vary}: The rich-get-richer dynamic should be most pronounced in individuals who are simultaneously high in cognitive performance, knowledge, and motivation. The model predicts that the Matthew effect is weakest when motivation is low, even if performance and knowledge are high---\hspace{0pt}because the loop lacks the driving force to iterate.

\item \textbf{Interventions targeting operational knowledge should have outsized long-term effects}: If operational knowledge is the multiplier in the recursive loop, then teaching learning strategies should produce effects that compound over time---\hspace{0pt}larger at long follow-up than at short follow-up. Some evidence supports this \citep{DignathButtner2008}, but it has not been interpreted in the context of a recursive intelligence model.

\item \textbf{Supplying an intrinsic drive to a system with persistent state and the capacity to modify itself will not, by itself, produce open-ended cross-domain compounding}: The experiment is buildable now. Give an agent what deployed language models lack---\hspace{0pt}state that persists across episodes and write access to the parameters that generate its behavior---\hspace{0pt}and supply an intrinsic reward of the kind described in Section~5.1: prediction error or learning progress in its own world model \citep{Schmidhuber1991,Oudeyer2007,Pathak2017}, with external task reward held at zero. Run it against a matched control identical except for that reward. The recursive model predicts that the intrinsically driven agent will explore more and will improve on the distribution it explores, while the breadth of its competence saturates: performance on domains held out from its exploration flattens, and the rate at which it enters new domains declines rather than rises with run time. The quantity to track is therefore held-out cross-domain performance against run time, not within-domain score. Open-ended compounding refutes the prediction---\hspace{0pt}non-saturating gains on held-out domains, with the set of domains the agent operates in widening as it runs---\hspace{0pt}and on that outcome the Motivation component contributes less than the model claims of it. This is the claim of Section~5.2 and the only claim the recursive model makes about artificial systems; none of it turns on the architectural conjecture of Section~5.3.

\item \textbf{Motivation-destroying educational interventions should produce compounding negative effects}: The recursive model predicts that practices such as punitive grading, ability tracking, and fixed-ability labeling should show damage that accelerates over time---\hspace{0pt}with effect sizes at 5-year follow-up significantly larger than at 1-year follow-up when the same cohort is tracked longitudinally. Conversely, motivation-enhancing interventions should show compounding benefits on the same timescale, distinguishing recursive compounding from simple persistence of initial gains.

\item \textbf{Properly aggregated motivation measures should show substantially stronger correlations with intelligence than currently reported}: The existing literature reports an average motivation-intellect correlation of $r \approx .30$, with individual coefficients ranging from $0$ to $.58$ \citep{vonStummAckerman2013}. The obvious objection: if the relationship is this modest, why call it a major omission? The answer lies in Brunswik symmetry \citep{Wittmann1988,WittmannKlumb2006}. Motivation is typically assessed with single questionnaire scales capturing a narrow behavioral window---\hspace{0pt}a state-level snapshot of a broad dispositional function. These narrow measures are then correlated with broad intelligence criteria (full test batteries yielding composite scores). The resulting asymmetry between predictor bandwidth and criterion bandwidth systematically attenuates the observed correlation, exactly as Wittmann's framework predicts. \citet{WittmannKlumb2006}, analyzing the parameters of the augmented Tucker lens model equation, demonstrated that for any observed effect size in psychological research, there are six sources of underestimation (range restriction, unreliability of both treatment and criterion, lack of construct symmetry on both the treatment and the criterion side, and negative sampling error) against only two sources of overestimation (range enhancement and positive sampling error). The odds are structurally stacked toward underestimation. Applied to motivation-intelligence correlations: the reported values are almost certainly attenuated by multiple simultaneous artifacts. The present model therefore predicts that when motivation is measured with the same care for bandwidth matching that intelligence researchers apply to cognitive assessment---\hspace{0pt}aggregated across multiple contexts, occasions, and expression modalities---\hspace{0pt}the motivation-intelligence correlation will prove substantially larger than the values currently reported in meta-analyses.

Stated without a limit, an argument of this shape excuses any weak correlation. The decisive design is a multi-occasion experience-sampling motivation battery administered alongside a full cognitive battery in a longitudinal sample. If motivation aggregated symmetrically over that design ---\hspace{0pt} many occasions, several contexts, matched in bandwidth to a composite cognitive criterion ---\hspace{0pt} still correlates with intelligence at approximately the \emph{r} $\approx$ .30 currently reported, then the attenuation account is wrong and the modest correlations are substantive rather than artifactual. On that outcome the unity claim of Section 3.1 loses its principal support and the case for treating motivation as the loop's allocation policy rather than as a correlate would have to be made on other grounds or abandoned. The author regards \emph{r} $\geq$ .50 under symmetric aggregation as the result the model requires, and anything at or near the currently reported average as a disconfirmation rather than a measurement problem to be explained away.

\item \textbf{Motivation should predict behavioral consistency across intellectual engagement situations, not merely effort level on single occasions}: The recursive loop iterates through repeated engagement episodes across diverse situations. The Motivation component's primary contribution to the loop is therefore not how hard one tries on any given occasion, but how \emph{consistently} one engages across situations and over time. Wittmann's demonstration with Eysenck's personality dimensions provides the methodological model: Extraversion predicted the \emph{mean} of aggregated behavioral indicators across situations, while Neuroticism predicted their \emph{intraindividual standard deviation} \citep{Wittmann1988}. Applied to the recursive model: motivation should primarily predict the consistency (low intraindividual variability) of intellectual engagement across diverse situations. Highly motivated individuals engage consistently; low-motivation individuals engage sporadically. The loop compounds through iteration count, not iteration intensity. This predicts that the strongest longitudinal predictor of intellectual development is not peak motivation (highest observed engagement) but motivational consistency (lowest variance in engagement across occasions)---\hspace{0pt}a prediction that is directly testable with experience-sampling methods. Within-person data of the required form already exist: \citet{Brose2010}, using daily measures of effort and enjoyment alongside working memory performance in the COGITO study, found that among participants whose motivation varied, higher-motivation days were also better-performance days. The coupling was heterogeneous across individuals and weak or absent in the older sample, which is what a model treating motivation as one interacting component predicts rather than a uniform main effect. What COGITO did not do is aggregate motivation symmetrically against a matched cognitive criterion or separate the subtest classes of Section~7.3; the design required to test this prediction alongside prediction 7 is specified there.

\item \textbf{Aggregating imagination measures across modalities, not merely across occasions, should raise their convergence with fluid reasoning}: The argument of Section~3.1---\hspace{0pt}that a single function appears in the literature as a family of separate constructs because different instruments observe it in different contexts---\hspace{0pt}is not specific to motivation. The capacity to construct a model of something not present and run it is an operation rather than a location: it is carried out in whatever representational system holds the content being modelled, and is therefore instantiated separately for visual scenes, for narrative episodes, for spatial layouts, and for relational structure. Any single instrument samples one instantiation. Imagery-vividness scales sample the visual one, daydreaming inventories and absorption scales \citep{TellegenAtkinson1974} the narrative one, matrix and mental-rotation tasks the relational and spatial ones. The literature's current answer is that these do not converge with reasoning ability: \citeauthor{AckermanHeggestad1997}'s \citeyearpar{AckermanHeggestad1997} meta-analysis puts the Openness-Gf correlation at $r = .08$, against .30 with Gc, and \citet{NusbaumSilvia2011}, working with latent variables, found that the Openness aspect predicted creative behavior but not fluid intelligence while the Intellect aspect predicted fluid intelligence but not creative behavior. The present model reads that dissociation as a modality projection rather than a structural boundary, and predicts that aggregation across modalities will recover convergence that aggregation within a single modality does not. This yields a contrast that separates the two claims. Bandwidth asymmetry alone (prediction 7) predicts that observed convergence rises with the number of aggregated observations, whatever their source. The present model predicts more: that $k$ observations spanning four modalities will converge with aggregated fluid ability more strongly than $k$ observations of one modality gathered across $k$ occasions. If the two aggregation schemes perform equivalently at matched $k$, the mechanistic claim is wrong and only the psychometric one survives.
\end{enumerate}

A further consequence, which can be tested on data already collected and which specifies in advance what would disconfirm it, is developed separately in Section~7.3.

\subsection{A Direct Test: Subtest Composition and Secular Change}

The recursive model implies a partition of standard test content that cuts across the usual verbal/performance and Gf/Gc divisions. Some subtests require the examinee to construct a model of a situation that is not present and then run it. Matrix reasoning and Raven's progressive matrices require building and testing a rule that no item states; block design and mental rotation require transforming a represented figure; Similarities requires constructing a superordinate relation that neither term supplies; Comprehension items about hypothetical circumstances require simulating an agent's situation. Other subtests require retrieval from a store: Vocabulary, Information, and arithmetic-fact items. Call these simulation-loaded and retrieval-loaded. The partition is not the Gf/Gc distinction---\hspace{0pt}Similarities and Comprehension are scored as verbal, knowledge-dependent subtests---\hspace{0pt}and it is not the verbal/nonverbal distinction. Two subtests are contested. Arithmetic loads on working memory, yet its items call for the application of a taught procedure rather than the construction of a novel one, and the classification assigns it to the retrieval-loaded class. Coding is the harder case and the partition does not accommodate it: it is a speeded clerical task, requiring neither model construction nor retrieval from a knowledge store, and Flynn and Weiss attribute its gains to the tempo of visual media rather than to anything in the present account. It is therefore excluded from the classification altogether. This is a real cost, since Coding gained 18.00 points and is among the largest movers in the record; any test of the predictions below must set it aside on the stated grounds, not let it count toward the simulation-loaded class merely because it is a Performance subtest.

The partition sorts the largest secular changes on record. Across the American Wechsler standardizations from 1947--48 to 2001.75, \citet[Table 2]{FlynnWeiss2007} report a gain of 23.85 IQ points on Similarities and gains across the five Performance subtests ranging from 11.70 to 21.50 points (an upper bound that is an estimate rather than a measurement; the largest measured Performance gain is 18.00, on Coding, and 15.90 excluding it), against 4.40 points on Vocabulary, 2.30 on Arithmetic and 2.15 on Information, with Comprehension at 11.00 and a Full Scale gain of between 16.83 and 17.63 points over the whole 54.25-year period. Two features of that table qualify what can be drawn from it. The gains for Object Assembly and Picture Arrangement are Flynn and Weiss's own estimates rather than measurements, since the WISC-IV retained neither subtest, and they are bracketed as such in the source; the argument here does not rest on them. And Digit Span, which was not among the ten core subtests, shows almost no movement at all across 1972 to 2002---\hspace{0pt}one IQ point---\hspace{0pt}which is the expected result for a subtest that asks for the maintenance and manipulation of given material rather than the construction of anything. At the level of domains rather than subtests, \citeauthor{PietschnigVoracek2015}'s \citeyearpar{PietschnigVoracek2015} meta-analysis of 271 samples from 31 countries estimates annual gains of 0.41 IQ points for fluid and 0.30 for spatial performance, against 0.21 for crystallized. In the Norwegian conscript series, \citet{Sundet2004} found that from the early 1970s the gains in general ability were driven almost exclusively by the Raven-like subtest, with the language and mathematics subtests contributing little, until the gains on the Raven-like test themselves stopped in the late 1990s.

Flynn's own account \citep{FlynnWeiss2007,Flynn2007} attributes this pattern to a secular shift toward abstract and scientific habits of classification---\hspace{0pt}children have come to ``view the world through the spectacles provided by science,'' and to take seriously problems with no obvious practical payoff---\hspace{0pt}rather than to any change in motivation, and the present framework does not displace that reading. What it adds is a reason why these two classes of subtest should be the ones that separate: a simulation-loaded item asks for model construction, which is the operation the recursive loop trains, while a retrieval-loaded item asks for a lookup against a store the loop fills only incidentally.

The partition is the boundary of Section 3.1 transposed to test content. A retrieval-loaded subtest interrogates what is stored in structure ---\hspace{0pt} what a dead brain would still yield to a sufficiently good structural readout: a vocabulary item, an information item, an arithmetic fact is answered from the sediment, and no model needs to run. A simulation-loaded subtest cannot be answered that way, because nothing stored contains the answer; the generative process has to run, constructing the rule, the transformation or the hypothetical situation the item withholds, and that running is exactly what stops when the dynamics stop. The cut therefore falls where the re-typing already placed a physical boundary, which distinguishes it from the many other partitions that could be drawn through the same subtest space: the classification is not a device introduced for the secular record but the structure/process distinction meeting test content. The direction of support runs one way. The partition was arrived at from the character of the items; its separation in the American record describes the classification rather than tests it, as the disconfirmation criteria below state; and the predictions it generates stand or fall on the held-out designs whether or not the re-typing is correct. What the correspondence supplies is a principled reason for the cut ---\hspace{0pt} one fixed by the typing rather than by how any record turned out, so that if the re-typing holds, the partition was never free to fall elsewhere ---\hspace{0pt} and not an additional piece of evidence for either claim.

Three predictions follow, none of them yet tested against a classification fixed in advance.

The differential pattern should extend to the reversal. If the environmental conditions that support the loop have degraded, as \citeauthor{BratsbergRogeberg2018}'s \citeyearpar{BratsbergRogeberg2018} within-family analysis indicates, the decline should fall disproportionately on simulation-loaded content, mirroring the ascent. The Norwegian conscript archive carries its three subtests separately and can settle this directly.

The two classes should differ in trainability. Interventions that teach model construction should move simulation-loaded scores more than retrieval-loaded scores; content instruction should do the reverse. This predicts the shape of the working-memory-training record: transfer should appear where the trained operation and the criterion share the construction demand and fail where they do not.

The two classes should differ in their coupling to within-person motivational state: simulation-loaded performance should covary with momentary engagement more strongly than retrieval-loaded performance does, because the former requires sustained construction and the latter does not. This limb is not a separate request for a study. Predictions 7 and 8 and this third limb are three read-outs of one design---\hspace{0pt}a multi-occasion experience-sampling motivation battery administered alongside a cognitive battery in a longitudinal sample, with structure estimated within rather than between persons. That single design yields the symmetric aggregation that prediction 7 stakes a disconfirmation criterion on, the intraindividual variability that prediction 8 identifies as the operative quantity, and the subtest-by-engagement coupling asked for here. The COGITO study \citep{Brose2010,Schmiedek2020} is the methodological precedent for all three and demonstrates that data of this form can be collected at the required density.

Two constraints govern the test. Subtest score changes cannot be read as changes in a latent ability without invariance testing: \citet{Wicherts2004} showed that cohort gains on five intelligence batteries from three countries, including the Dutch WAIS, are often not measurement invariant, and that the observed pattern of subtest gains is not what a change in common-factor means would produce. The prediction is therefore stated over observed subtest scores, and any latent reading requires the invariance model to be fitted first. That constraint is also what makes the pattern informative here. \citet{Oberleiter2024} report something different from a failure of invariance---\hspace{0pt}score gains across measurement-invariant subscales accompanied by a weakening common factor---\hspace{0pt}and that is what the recursive model expects when scores rise without the loop being engaged.

The prediction is disconfirmed if simulation-loaded and retrieval-loaded subtests show statistically indistinguishable secular trajectories in a record the partition was not read off, with every subtest assigned to a class before its gains are inspected, or if the reversal falls on retrieval-loaded content while simulation-loaded scores hold. The first limb cannot be run on the American Wechsler standardizations reported above: the partition was constructed with that table in view, so a separation found there describes the classification rather than tests it. What the limb requires is a held-out record---\hspace{0pt}another country's standardization series, or a different test family---\hspace{0pt}classified in advance and then examined. The cohort datasets assembled by \citet{Wicherts2004} are of the right form, carrying subtest-level scores from three countries and compiled to answer a different question. Competing accounts do not generate the contrast: a test-familiarity account predicts gains concentrated on whatever content is most practiced, and a nutrition or general-health account predicts a broadly uniform lift.

\subsection{Limitations}

This paper presents a theoretical framework, not empirical data. The recursive model is offered as a conceptual tool for integrating existing findings and generating new predictions, not as a fully specified formal model. Formalizing the model mathematically---\hspace{0pt}specifying the functional forms of the recursive interactions, the role of time and developmental stage, and the boundary conditions under which the loop amplifies, stagnates, or collapses---\hspace{0pt}is the task for a subsequent paper.

The distinction between factual and operational knowledge, while intuitively clear, is not always sharp in practice. Many items of knowledge have both factual and operational aspects (e.g., knowing that spaced repetition works is factual; knowing how to implement it is operational). A more precise taxonomy of knowledge types, grounded in empirical data, would strengthen the framework.

The model also does not address the neuroscience of motivation in detail. The dopaminergic reward system, the role of the anterior cingulate cortex in effort allocation, and the neuromodulatory mechanisms that sustain engagement over time are all relevant but beyond the scope of a theoretical critique paper. More than that, and as Section~7.1 records, the framework makes no predictions at the neural level at all: it does not say what network dynamics should accompany a loop iteration, and it cannot currently be confirmed or embarrassed by the brain-network literature in either direction. A process account that could be tested neurally as well as psychometrically would be a stronger theory than this one, and deriving such predictions is the clearest route to making it so.

Finally, there is a deeper limitation that the model shares with every intelligence framework: it says nothing about what intelligence is \emph{for}. If intelligence does not result in a good life---\hspace{0pt}in well-being, in flourishing---\hspace{0pt}is it really intelligence? The recursive loop can amplify itself indefinitely, but amplification without direction is not wisdom. A complete account of intelligence may ultimately require not just a theory of cognitive dynamics but a companion theory of living well. This paper does not attempt that integration.

The model's claim about artificial systems and the author's expectation about them are different things, and the second does not inherit the standing of the first. Section~5.2 states the claim: supplying an exploration drive to a system with persistent state and self-modification will not by itself produce open-ended compounding. It says nothing about what would. The author expects that the route to artificial systems which develop themselves runs through the engineering of motivation rather than through further scaling of knowledge and performance. That is a bet and not a result, and it is lost if such development appears in a system built by scaling knowledge and performance alone, with nothing in it answering to the description in Section~5.1. Nothing in Sections~6 or 7 depends on it either way.

A final limitation concerns the boundary of the argument. The recursive loop requires cognitive learning, and cognitive learning requires the capacity to construct a model of what is not present and run it; what supplies that capacity is left open here (Section~3.4). The author's companion work on consciousness \citep{Gruber2026} proposes an architectural answer, in terms of two kinds of model---\hspace{0pt}implicit and explicit---\hspace{0pt}applied to two domains, world and self, with the explicit self-model as the locus of the evaluative function. That proposal is a candidate, not an established result, and has not been independently tested; a theory of intelligence should not be made to depend on it. Sections~5.3 and 3.1 draw on it and say so. Nothing in Sections~6 or 7 requires it. The relationship between the two frameworks is a conjecture the author intends to pursue.

\subsection{A Historical Note}

The exclusion of motivation is not an inevitable consequence of studying cognition---\hspace{0pt}it is a historical choice. \citet{Wechsler1940,Wechsler1943} explicitly called for the inclusion of non-intellective factors (Section~2.4). The psychometric tradition's reliance on factor analysis of cognitive test scores created a self-reinforcing methodological loop---\hspace{0pt}intelligence is what intelligence tests measure, intelligence tests measure cognitive abilities, therefore intelligence is a cognitive construct \citep{Sternberg1985}---\hspace{0pt}whose specific consequence for the treatment of motivation has rarely been examined directly.


\section{Conclusion}

The absence of motivation from the formal structure of intelligence models is real, and Section 2 documents it. What this paper has argued is that the absence is a symptom rather than the disease. A field that had merely forgotten a component would have added it once someone pointed at the gap, and people have been pointing since Wechsler. What kept it out was not oversight but type: the tradition's instruments measure capacities, so its constituents were typed as capacities, and motivation is not one.

Intelligence, on this account, is not a capacity either. It is a \emph{learning ability} ---\hspace{0pt} a system in which one capacity, one stock of stored and enacted knowledge, and one allocation policy interact across a lifespan, and whose course is set by how the loop iterates rather than by any single constituent measured on a single occasion. Motivation's place in that system is the schedule, not the substance: it contributes no quantity to be summed with the other two, and it decides how much of the loop runs, on what, and for how long.

The re-typing pays four times over, in results the field already has and reports without an account. Motivation resisted trait measurement for a century because there was no trait there to find. Its correlations with intelligence attenuate under narrow sampling because a policy is visible only in the allocations that fall inside the window. Schooling moves fluid ability durably while working-memory training does not, because one re-points the schedule and the other drills the instrument that estimates the capacity. And engagement should predict intellectual development through its consistency rather than its peak, because a loop compounds through iteration count.

Each claim carries a result that would defeat it. A symmetric aggregation of motivation measures that returned the currently reported average correlation would defeat the measurement argument; a demonstration that peak engagement outpredicts consistency would refute the policy reading; and the subtest partition of Section 7.3 makes a prediction about held-out designs that does not depend on the rest being right. A system built on the assumption that the learnable part of intelligence is the capacity will invest where the least movement is available; the record on where the movement is appears in Section 6.
