## Abstract

Intelligence research has produced a paradox: the field widely acknowledges that motivation influences cognitive development, yet virtually every major model of intelligence formally excludes it. The Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) taxonomy, the dominant psychometric framework, contains no motivational component. Cattell's own investment theory treats motivation as an external condition rather than a constitutive element. This paper argues that this exclusion is not merely an oversight but a systematic blind spot that distorts our understanding of intelligence in three specific ways. First, it mischaracterizes intelligence as a static trait rather than a recursive, self-reinforcing system in which knowledge, cognitive performance, and motivation form a closed amplification loop. Second, it renders invisible the role of operational knowledge — learning strategies, logical tools, and strategic thinking — which functions as the primary multiplier within this loop. Third, it leaves the field without an account of why current artificial intelligence systems, in which knowledge and computational performance have been scaled to superhuman levels, do not exhibit the self-directed development that characterizes human intelligence — a question on which the intelligence literature is silent while a separate research tradition has spent three decades engineering intrinsic motivation into artificial agents without those two programmes being joined. The paper further argues that motivation is not the family of independent constructs (need for cognition, typical intellectual engagement, openness, need for achievement) that the personality-intelligence literature treats it as, but a single evaluative function of the brain's explicit processing that appears fragmented because different measurement instruments observe it in different contexts — an interpretation supported by Brunswik symmetry analysis, which predicts that the modest motivation-intelligence correlations reported in the literature are measurement artifacts rather than evidence of weak association. This paper proposes a three-component recursive model (Knowledge × Performance × Motivation) and argues that intelligence is best understood not as a capacity but as a *learning ability* — one whose trajectory is determined by the dynamics of this recursive loop rather than by any single component measured in isolation.

**Keywords**: intelligence, motivation, recursive systems, operational knowledge, artificial intelligence, CHC theory, Cattell investment theory

## 1. Introduction: A Curious Omission

Consider two children with identical IQ scores at age six. By age thirty, one has become a research scientist, the other has not opened a book since leaving school. Standard psychometric models of intelligence struggle to account for this divergence. The six-year-old's IQ score, which captured cognitive performance under controlled test conditions, told us almost nothing about the trajectory of intellectual development that would follow. Something was missing from the measurement — and, I argue, from the models themselves.

The missing variable is not mysterious. Every teacher knows it. Every parent has observed it. Every employer screens for it. Motivation — the sustained drive to learn, to understand, to act on one's environment — is universally acknowledged as critical to cognitive achievement. The empirical literature on motivation and academic performance is vast (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Dweck, 2006; Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). Meta-analyses consistently find reciprocal relationships between motivation and achievement that strengthen over developmental time (for a recent multilevel meta-analysis, see Huang, 2024).

And yet, motivation does not appear in the formal structure of any major intelligence model.

The Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) taxonomy — the most widely used framework in intelligence research — is a hierarchical arrangement of cognitive abilities beneath a general factor *g*, descending through broad stratum abilities (fluid reasoning, comprehension-knowledge, short-term memory, processing speed, and others) to narrow abilities (McGrew, 2009; Schneider & McGrew, 2018). It is an impressive edifice. It contains no motivational component whatsoever.

Cattell's (1971) own investment theory, which proposed that fluid intelligence (Gf) is "invested" into crystallized intelligence (Gc) through learning, comes closer to acknowledging the role of motivation — after all, investment requires an investor, and investors require reasons to invest. But Cattell treated motivation as an *external condition* that modulates the investment process, not as a constitutive part of intelligence itself. In his framework, motivation is to intelligence what temperature is to a chemical reaction: it affects the rate, but it is not part of the reaction.

This paper argues that this analogy is wrong. Motivation is not external to intelligence; it is a constitutive component of a recursive system. Excluding it is not a harmless simplification — it produces a systematically distorted picture that fails to capture the self-reinforcing dynamics that define intellectual development across the lifespan.

The argument proceeds in six steps. Section 2 surveys major intelligence models and documents the systematic exclusion of motivation. Section 3 presents a recursive three-component model in which knowledge, cognitive performance, and motivation form a closed amplification loop. Section 4 examines the special role of *operational knowledge* — the knowledge about how to learn — which functions as the primary multiplier within this loop. Section 5 explores what the recursive model implies about artificial intelligence systems that lack motivation. Section 6 draws out what I consider the most consequential implication of the framework: that intelligence is, to a large extent, learnable, and that educational practices built on the contrary assumption carry a cost the model predicts will compound. Section 7 relates the framework to established models, states its testable predictions and their disconfirmation criteria, and sets out its limitations.

## 2. The Status Quo: How Intelligence Models Treat Motivation

### 2.1 The CHC Taxonomy

The Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) model is the "standard reference point" for intelligence research (McGrew, 2009, p. 1). It arranges cognitive abilities into a three-stratum hierarchy: a general factor *g* at the apex, broad abilities at stratum II (including fluid reasoning Gf, comprehension-knowledge Gc, short-term memory Gsm, processing speed Gs, long-term storage and retrieval Glr, visual processing Gv, auditory processing Ga, and others), and narrow abilities at stratum I.

This model is explicitly cognitive. It measures what the brain can do on demand, under standardized conditions, with maximum effort assumed. Motivational variation is treated as measurement noise — something to be minimized through standardized test administration, not modeled as a structural feature of intelligence (Schneider & McGrew, 2018). The implicit assumption is that motivation is a confound, not a component. The CHC framework's internal coherence has also been questioned on its own terms. Canivez and Youngstrom (2019) documented that Carroll and Horn held irreconcilable positions on the status of *g*, that confirmatory factor analyses of CHC-based test batteries show poor fit, and that the broad stratum abilities provide weak incremental validity beyond *g*. These internal difficulties suggest that the CHC taxonomy may be less settled than its widespread adoption implies — and that the systematic exclusion of motivational variables may be contributing to its interpretive limitations.

### 2.2 Cattell's Investment Theory

Cattell (1971) proposed that Gc develops from the "investment" of Gf in learning experiences. This was a significant conceptual advance: it introduced a dynamic, developmental dimension to intelligence that the purely psychometric models lacked. Gf represents the biological substrate — the raw computational power of the brain, heavily influenced by genetics, peaking in early adulthood and declining thereafter. Gc represents the accumulated product of applying that power to the world — knowledge, skills, cultural repertoire — which continues to grow throughout life.

But who decides what to invest in? Cattell's theory requires an agent who allocates Gf to particular learning domains. That agent must be motivated — must find some domains more interesting, more rewarding, or more useful than others. Without motivation, the "investment" metaphor collapses: one cannot invest without the intention to invest.

Cattell was aware of this. He discussed personality traits — particularly what he called "investment traits" — as modulators of the Gf-to-Gc pathway (see also Ziegler et al., 2012, on the relationship between investment traits and intelligence). But he kept these firmly outside the intelligence construct. The result is an investment theory without an investor.

### 2.3 Sternberg's Triarchic Theory

Sternberg's (1985) triarchic theory expanded the scope of intelligence beyond the psychometric tradition by proposing three aspects: analytical (similar to traditional IQ), creative (the ability to deal with novel situations), and practical (the ability to adapt to, shape, and select environments). This was a deliberate attempt to broaden the construct beyond what IQ tests measure.

Practical intelligence — knowing how to get things done in real-world contexts — comes close to the territory of motivation, since effectively shaping one's environment requires the drive to do so. But Sternberg framed practical intelligence as a *competence*, not a *drive*. You can know how to navigate a social environment without being motivated to do so. The triarchic theory, like CHC, treats motivation as logically separate from ability.

### 2.4 Wechsler's Unfulfilled Call

David Wechsler, whose intelligence scales remain the most widely administered in the world, was remarkably explicit about this problem. He defined intelligence as "the global capacity of a person to act purposefully, to think rationally, and to deal effectively with his environment" (Wechsler, 1944, p. 3). As early as 1943, he had made the argument explicit: "We cannot expect to measure total intelligence until our tests also include some measures of the non-intellective factors" — identifying persistence, curiosity, and goal orientation as essential components of intelligent behavior (Wechsler, 1943).

The field ignored him. The modern Wechsler scales (WAIS, WISC) remain purely cognitive instruments. Wechsler's own tests do not measure the non-intellective factors he argued were essential. This is perhaps the clearest illustration of the blind spot described here: the founder of the world's most widely used intelligence test explicitly called for the inclusion of motivational factors, and the field proceeded as if he had not spoken.

### 2.5 Gardner's Multiple Intelligences

Gardner (1983) proposed eight (later nine) relatively independent intelligences: linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalist, and (tentatively) existential. While intrapersonal intelligence — the capacity for self-understanding — includes awareness of one's own motivational states, Gardner did not identify motivation itself as an intelligence or as a structural component of his framework. The model was criticized extensively for failing to demonstrate that its proposed intelligences are truly independent rather than facets of *g* (Waterhouse, 2006), but the absence of motivation was rarely noted as a problem.

### 2.6 The General Pattern

Across all major models, the pattern is the same: motivation is acknowledged as important, treated as correlated with intelligence, and then excluded from the formal model. The reasons are methodological: intelligence tests measure *maximum performance* (what a person *can* do, not what they *will* do; Cronbach, 1949), factor-analytic models are built from cognitive test scores in which motivation does not appear, and disciplinary boundaries keep the motivation and intelligence literatures separate (for exceptions, see Murayama et al., 2013; Credé & Kuncel, 2008). Carr and Dweck (2019), in their Cambridge Handbook chapter on "Intelligence and Motivation," exemplify this pattern: they review extensive evidence of interaction between the two constructs but frame them as ontologically separate, never questioning whether the separation itself is the problem.

The problem runs deeper than disciplinary boundaries. Intelligence testing paradigms are specifically designed to suppress motivational variance: constrained time limits, rigid testing environments, no feedback, no opportunity for social interaction, and no access to aids create conditions under which personality and motivation have minimal room to express themselves (Ackerman, 2018). The measurement context is engineered to isolate "pure" cognitive capacity — and then the resulting scores are used to argue that intelligence is a purely cognitive construct. The circularity is rarely noted.

These are legitimate practical considerations. But methodological convenience should not be confused with theoretical truth. The question is not whether motivation is hard to measure alongside cognitive abilities (it is), but whether a model of intelligence that excludes motivation provides an adequate account of how intelligence actually develops and operates.

I argue that it does not.

## 3. Intelligence as a Recursive System

### 3.1 The Three Components

This paper proposes that intelligence — understood as *learning ability* (Lernfähigkeit) — is constituted by three interacting components. This recursive model was first proposed in Gruber (2015, in German) and is developed here in its first systematic English-language presentation, with significant theoretical extensions and engagement with the contemporary intelligence literature. The three components are:

1. **Knowledge** (*Wissen*): The accumulated content of learning. This includes both factual knowledge (what is known) and operational knowledge (how to learn, how to reason, how to strategize). It corresponds roughly, though not perfectly, to Cattell's Gc. It is acquired through upbringing, formal education, and independent learning.

2. **Performance** (*Leistung*): The processing capacity of the cognitive system, including working memory capacity, processing speed, and the computational power of the neural substrate. It corresponds roughly to Cattell's Gf and to Frank's (1959) short-term memory capacity C = S × D (where S is processing speed in bits/second and D is memory span in seconds). It is influenced by genetics and by training.

3. **Motivation**: The sustained drive to engage with the world in ways that produce learning. At the intuitive level, two aspects are readily distinguished: *Wissensdrang* (thirst for knowledge) — the drive to understand, to learn, to make sense of the world — and *Handlungsdrang* (urge to act) — the drive to apply knowledge, to experiment, to explore. These correspond to familiar constructs in the personality-intelligence literature: Wissensdrang maps closely to Cacioppo and Petty's (1982) need for cognition (NFC; see also Cacioppo et al., 1996, for the dispositional characterization) and Goff and Ackerman's (1992) typical intellectual engagement (TIE); Handlungsdrang maps to the exploration and risk-taking dispositions that Wittmann and Hattrup (2004) showed mediate intelligence-performance relationships by generating new learning opportunities.

   However, the relationship between these intuitive categories and their empirical proxies is more complex than a simple mapping suggests — and understanding why reveals something important about the motivation construct itself. NFC and TIE, despite being highly correlated (r ≈ .78–.87; von Stumm & Ackerman, 2013), predict different intelligence facets: NFC correlates primarily with fluid reasoning (Gf), while TIE correlates primarily with crystallized knowledge (Gc). Need for achievement, openness to experience, and sensation seeking show yet other patterns (Ackerman, 2018). The standard interpretation is that these represent genuinely distinct motivational constructs. This paper proposes an alternative: they are *measurement-context projections* of a single underlying function.

   The argument, which draws on the architectural framework developed in Gruber (2026), is as follows. Motivation — the evaluative process that assigns salience, directs attention, and sustains engagement — is a function of the brain's explicit processing: the self-model that consciously evaluates situations, weighs options, and decides where to invest effort. Ordinary usage of "motivation" reaches into subconscious drive territory as well — affective valuations, automatic approach/avoidance, drives the subject cannot articulate. The construct used here is deliberately narrower: the conscious, symbolizable evaluative layer. The substrate-level optimization that underlies it — what the companion theory of consciousness terms *will* (Gruber, 2026, §4.2.2) — is real and constitutive of behavior, but it is not what the recursive loop's multiplier measures. This is a unified computational function, not a family of independent traits. It *appears* fragmented in the empirical literature because different measurement instruments observe this function in different contexts. NFC measures motivation-as-observed-during-novel-reasoning, which naturally co-varies with the processing substrate that novel reasoning depends on (Gf). TIE measures motivation-as-observed-during-habitual-knowledge-seeking, which naturally co-varies with the accumulated knowledge store (Gc). Risk-taking measures motivation-as-observed-during-exploration, which naturally generates new learning opportunities. The constructs diverge not because the underlying motivation differs, but because the measurement context determines which intelligence facet the observation co-activates.

   Artificial intelligence is often invoked at this point. It settles less than it appears to. Deployed large language models possess vast knowledge and high processing performance while displaying none of the behaviour that any motivation construct was built to measure: no curiosity, no self-directed learning, no drive to close gaps in their own understanding (Section 5). It is tempting to read this as support for the unity claim — what is missing is not need for cognition, or typical intellectual engagement, or need for achievement, but the single function of which these are partial measures. The inference does not go through. These systems lack *all* of the constructs at once, and the simultaneous absence of many things is equally consistent with there being many things absent. A case in which everything is missing cannot discriminate one missing function from a set of missing traits, and it should not be presented as though it could.

   What the case does supply is the observation that the missing element is not supplied by scaling either of the other two components, which is a claim about the loop rather than about the structure of motivation. The unity claim has to be carried by evidence that can come apart: the aggregation contrast of prediction 9, which pits a single-function reading against a distinct-constructs reading at matched sample size, is where it is put at risk.

   This reinterpretation has a direct precedent in Wittmann's own Brunswik symmetry framework (Wittmann, 1988; Wittmann & Klumb, 2006): observed correlations are bounded by the symmetry between predictor and criterion bandwidth. Narrow motivation measures (a single questionnaire scale) correlated with broad intelligence criteria (a full test battery) will produce attenuated and criterion-specific correlations — not because motivation is weakly related to intelligence, but because the measurement design is asymmetric. Properly aggregated motivation measures, assessed across multiple contexts and occasions, should produce substantially higher and more uniform correlations with intelligence — a testable prediction of the present framework. A further attenuation arises from temporal reliability: motivation, unlike a fixed cognitive capacity, varies across situations and time. A single-occasion questionnaire captures a snapshot of a fluctuating state, and the resulting low reliability mechanically suppresses the observed correlation — an artifact of temporal sampling, distinct from and compounding the bandwidth asymmetry.

   The recursive model therefore treats Motivation as a single component with two functional expressions — the epistemic (Wissensdrang: directing engagement toward understanding) and the agentic (Handlungsdrang: directing engagement toward action and exploration) — while maintaining that these expressions reflect a unified evaluative process rather than structurally independent traits. What the personality-intelligence literature has catalogued as distinct constructs (NFC, TIE, openness, need for achievement, risk-taking) are, in this view, context-specific behavioral signatures of the same underlying function, measured at different points of its expression.

   The three components are functionally separable but not mechanistically independent, and the model should say so rather than let the notation imply otherwise. Knowledge, Performance and Motivation are distinguished by what each contributes to the loop — stored content, processing capacity, and the evaluative direction of engagement — not by running on disjoint machinery. The capacity to construct a model and run it detached from present input is drawn on by both Performance and Motivation: it is what reasoning with unfamiliar material requires, and it is also what allows a person to represent a state of affairs that does not obtain and be moved by it, which is what sustained engagement toward a distant goal consists in. The architectural account of that capacity is given in Gruber (2026). For a measurement model this shared dependence is a constraint rather than a defect. It predicts that the three components will not be found orthogonal, that attempts to purify them will strip out variance the loop actually uses, and that a decomposition succeeding in making them independent would be measuring something other than the system described here.

### 3.2 The Recursive Loop

The recursive loop described here requires a specific cognitive capacity: cognitive learning — the induction of general theories from particular observations — as distinct from reinforcement learning (Gruber, 2015). The distinction between cognitive learning and reinforcement learning, and the specific neural architecture that enables the former, is developed in Gruber (2015) and formalized in Gruber (2026). What matters here is the implication: the recursive intelligence loop can only self-sustain in systems capable of cognitive learning. What a system must be like in order to be capable of it is a further question, and the present argument does not settle it (Sections 3.4, 5.3 and 7.4).

The critical claim of this framework is that these three components are not merely additive; they form a *closed recursive loop* in which each component amplifies the others:

- **Knowledge enhances Performance**: Learning strategies, logical tools, and strategic thinking (operational knowledge) directly improve the efficiency of cognitive processing. A chess player who has learned heuristics can process positions faster than one who relies on brute-force search. A reader who has learned phonemic decoding processes text more fluently, freeing working memory for comprehension.

- **Performance enhances Knowledge**: Greater cognitive processing capacity enables faster and deeper learning. Higher working memory capacity allows the learner to hold more information in mind simultaneously, facilitating the formation of connections and the extraction of patterns.

- **Motivation enhances both Knowledge and Performance**: The motivated learner seeks out learning opportunities (expanding Knowledge) and practices cognitive skills (training Performance). Crucially, motivation sustains engagement over time, which is essential for the recursive loop to iterate.

- **Knowledge and Performance enhance Motivation**: Success in learning and problem-solving generates positive affect and self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997), which in turn sustains motivation. This is the mechanism behind the Matthew effect: early success breeds the motivation that produces further success (Stanovich, 1986).

This recursive structure produces the self-reinforcing dynamics that are empirically well-documented but theoretically under-explained. The Matthew effect in reading (Stanovich, 1986) — whereby children who read well read more, thereby becoming even better readers — is a specific instance of this general recursive loop. The divergence of Gf and Gc across the lifespan — Gf declining from early adulthood while Gc continues to grow — is explained by the recursive model as the growing dominance of the Knowledge and Motivation legs as the Performance leg (biological processing capacity) begins to decline.

### 3.3 Why This Is Not Simply "Motivation Matters"

The claim here is stronger than the trivial observation that motivation influences learning outcomes. This paper advances a specific structural claim: that intelligence is a *system* whose behavior is determined by the recursive interaction of three components, and that removing any one component from the model produces qualitatively wrong predictions about the system's dynamics.

Consider the prediction that intelligence should be a self-reinforcing process. Standard psychometric models, which treat intelligence as a relatively stable trait (albeit one that changes somewhat over the lifespan), have difficulty explaining why some individuals show dramatically increasing intellectual capability over decades while others plateau early. The recursive model predicts exactly this: small initial differences in any of the three components — even in motivation alone — should compound over time, producing the wide variance in adult intellectual achievement observed empirically.

This is not merely a statistical amplification of initial differences (as in a simple fan-spread model). It is a qualitatively different dynamic: a positive feedback loop that can be entered at any point. A child with modest initial cognitive ability but high motivation and good operational knowledge (learning strategies) can, through the recursive loop, develop intellectual capabilities far beyond what their initial "IQ" would predict. Conversely, a child with high initial cognitive ability but low motivation or poor learning strategies may stagnate. Both patterns are widely observed empirically but poorly explained by models that treat intelligence as a primarily cognitive, primarily stable construct.

Population-level evidence supports this process view. Bratsberg and Rogeberg (2018), analyzing Norwegian military conscript data, demonstrated that IQ scores rose and then declined across birth cohorts *within families* — ruling out genetic explanations and confirming environmental causation. The Flynn effect reversal, now documented across multiple countries, is incompatible with models that treat intelligence as a primarily biological trait. It is, however, precisely what a recursive model predicts: when environmental conditions that support the loop (educational quality, intellectual engagement) degrade, the loop weakens at the population level.

The structural claim also carries a measurement commitment that distinguishes it from the weaker one. If motivation is constitutive of the loop rather than a correlate of its output, it must be visible *within* a person across occasions, not only as a between-person trait correlation. Brose et al. (2010) provide that observation directly: in the COGITO study, daily fluctuations in effort and enjoyment co-varied with same-day working memory performance within individuals. The effect is not universal — it was heterogeneous across participants and weak or absent among older adults — and the recursive model does not require it to be, since a component embedded in an interacting system will not behave as a uniform main effect.

Even more striking is the dissociation reported by Gignac and Zajenkowski (2024): IQ scores rose while *g* — the general factor extracted from the correlation matrix — simultaneously declined. This dissociation is inexplicable under static-trait models but directly predicted by the recursive framework. Teaching to the test inflates Performance scores (narrow task-specific knowledge) without engaging the recursive loop. The result is higher scores on standardized measures with lower capacity for the self-directed, generalizable learning that the recursive loop produces. IQ goes up; intelligence, properly understood, goes down.

### 3.4 Relation to Existing Work

Several researchers have proposed models that move in this direction, though none, to my knowledge, has formalized the full recursive structure.

Sternberg's (2019) concept of "adaptive intelligence" emphasizes the role of goals and purpose in intelligent behavior. More recently, Sternberg et al. (2021) proposed the concept of "meta-intelligence" — intelligence that operates on itself, recursively improving its own functioning. This is structurally identical to the recursive loop proposed here, though Sternberg and colleagues do not formally integrate motivation into the recursive structure or specify the Knowledge-Performance-Motivation triad as its components. Dweck's (2006) work on mindset shows that beliefs about the malleability of intelligence affect effort investment and, through effort, actual cognitive development — a finding that only makes sense if motivation is constitutive of the intelligence development process, not merely correlated with it.

The "investment traits" literature (Ziegler et al., 2012; von Stumm & Ackerman, 2013) has documented that personality traits related to intellectual engagement — curiosity, openness to experience, need for cognition — predict intellectual development beyond what cognitive ability alone predicts. Von Stumm et al. (2011) showed in a meta-analysis that "hungry minds" — individuals high in intellectual curiosity — develop greater knowledge and skill over time. These findings are consistent with the recursive model: investment traits are proxies for the motivation component, and their predictive power for long-term intellectual development is exactly what the recursive model predicts.

Murayama et al. (2013) demonstrated that intrinsic motivation predicted mathematics achievement growth over several years, even controlling for prior achievement and intelligence — direct evidence that motivation contributes to intellectual development beyond cognitive ability. The transmission of motivational states across educational contexts (Schiefele, 2017) and the meta-analytic findings of Huang (2024) document the bidirectional relationship between motivation and achievement that the recursive model formalizes.

Several other frameworks approach the territory of the recursive model without completing the integration. Ackerman's (1996, 2018) PPIK theory (Process, Personality, Interests, Knowledge) comes closest to the present proposal: it explicitly models how personality traits and interests direct the Gf-to-Gc investment process, and its empirical program has demonstrated that these non-cognitive factors predict intellectual development beyond cognitive ability alone. The recursive model can be understood as extending PPIK by treating motivation not as a moderator of the investment process but as a constitutive component of the intelligence construct itself — a step that changes the formal structure of the model rather than merely adding predictors to it. Wittmann and Süß (1999), working within the PPIK framework, demonstrated empirically that motivation's effect on complex performance is largely indirect — mediated through knowledge rather than acting directly on performance. Their path-analytic model, which explained approximately 50% of variance in dynamic task performance, showed intelligence-as-knowledge as the strongest direct predictor, with motivational variables contributing primarily via knowledge acquisition. This is precisely the M → K → Performance pathway that the recursive model formalizes. Wittmann and Hattrup (2004) further showed that risk-taking — a motivational disposition — mediates the relationship between intelligence and performance in dynamic systems by generating new learning opportunities, providing empirical evidence for the exploration-exploitation dynamic that the recursive loop predicts. Similarly, Duckworth et al.'s (2007) "grit" and Stanovich et al.'s (2016) Rationality Quotient each capture what IQ misses — including the drive to engage effortful processing — but frame these as separate constructs rather than constitutive components of intelligence. Snow's (1996) cognitive-conative-affective framework acknowledged the interdependence of cognition and motivation in learning, but remained in educational psychology and was never integrated into mainstream intelligence theory. Notably, Ackerman's own empirical work on trait complexes points toward the recursive model's conclusion without reaching it: personality traits, he finds, "are less influential as independent predictors of learning and knowledge" but "in conjunction with other trait families, jointly influence the outcomes" (Ackerman, 2018, p. 9). This is an empirical observation of exactly the system-level dynamics the recursive model formalizes — the components are not additive predictors but interacting elements of a recursive system.

The dynamic systems approach provides deeper theoretical support. Van Geert (2020) argues that constructs like intelligence must be understood as "temporary process stabilities" rather than fixed traits; Balboni et al. (2021) argue that intelligence is an emergent property of person-task-situation interaction. What the present framework adds to these process accounts is specificity: it identifies the three components (Knowledge, Performance, Motivation), their recursive interaction, and the temporal dynamics by which these interactions compound over the lifespan.

A parallel lineage in computational cognitive science reached the constitutive claim from the direction of agent architecture, not psychometrics. Dörner's Psi-theory (Dörner, 1999; Dörner & Güss, 2013) models the psyche as an integrated system in which needs and drives are not a bolt-on module but an intrinsic aspect of cognition: a system stripped of need-pressure would lack direction and purpose. Bach's MicroPsi, whose defining description is an architecture of motivated cognition (Bach, 2009, 2015), builds motivation into the substrate as the mechanism that selects goals and steers perception and reasoning. Sun's CLARION (Sun, 2009) makes the motivational subsystem one of four core pillars, holding that motivational representations are necessary for a comprehensive computational account of cognition; and the tradition descends from an early necessity argument, Sloman and Croucher's (1981) claim that motive-driven processing is unavoidable in any resource-bounded intelligent agent. The recursive model converges with this tradition on the core point that motivation is constitutive of, not merely correlated with, intelligent cognition, and differs from it in two respects. The first is scope: these are architectures for situated *agents*, whereas the present proposal brings the constitutive-motivation claim into the theory and measurement of *intelligence* itself, the domain where, as Section 2 documents, motivation has been treated as an external predictor at best. The second is in what is asked of the claim: these architectures implement motivated cognition, whereas the present model uses the constitutive premise to generate measurement predictions about human data — the Brunswik-symmetry aggregation prediction of Section 3.1 and the subtest-composition test of Section 7.3. The lineage supplies the architectural argument; what is added here is a set of consequences that can be checked against existing psychometric records. This same tradition also bears on the paper's claim about artificial intelligence (Section 5): a research programme that spent decades building *motivated* cognitive architectures implies, by its own emphasis, that systems lacking a motivational layer are missing something architecturally consequential, not merely undertrained.

The closest formal precedents, however, are not in the personality-intelligence literature at all, and the present proposal must be positioned against them explicitly. Van der Maas et al. (2006) derived the positive manifold from *mutualism*: a set of initially uncorrelated cognitive processes that reinforce one another's growth during development will produce a general factor without any general cause, and the resulting dynamics reproduce the hierarchical structure that factor analysis recovers. Dickens and Flynn (2001) formalized reciprocal causation between IQ and environment, showing that a multiplier — in which a small initial advantage attracts a better-matched environment, which raises ability further, which attracts a better environment again — reconciles high heritability estimates with the large environmental effects the Flynn effect demonstrates. Savi et al. (2019) extended the network account into an explicitly developmental wiring model. These are the direct precedents for the present proposal, and the resemblance is close: each is a positive-feedback account of intellectual development in which compounding rather than fixed capacity does the explanatory work.

The recursive model should therefore not be read as discovering recursion in intelligence. That work is done, it is formalized, and it is two decades old. What is claimed here is narrower and comes to three points. First, the quantity that the mutualism and multiplier models leave implicit — what determines how much of the reciprocal process actually runs, and for how long — is motivation, and it belongs inside the system rather than in the environment term. Dickens and Flynn's multiplier operates through environment matching, which presupposes an agent that seeks out and holds onto matched environments; that seeking is the M component, and in their model it is a parameter rather than a construct. Second, motivation's apparent fragmentation across the personality-intelligence literature is a measurement artifact rather than a structural fact (Section 3.1) — a claim about instruments that neither mutualism nor the multiplier model makes, and one that is testable independently of either. Third, the resulting programme is a measurement programme with stated falsifiers (Sections 7.2 and 7.3) rather than a further simulation study. Where van der Maas et al. supply functional forms that the present paper does not (Section 7.4), the natural next step is to add M as a node to a mutualism network and ask whether the empirical signatures set out below follow — a study this paper motivates rather than performs.

What none of these contributions supplies is the combination this paper assembles: a recursive account in which motivation is one of the interacting components, together with the measurement consequences that placing it there entails — the Brunswik-symmetry aggregation prediction, the consistency-over-intensity prediction, and the subtest partition of Section 7.3. The system-level regularities the model is asked to accommodate, the Matthew effect and the Gf-Gc divergence among them, are accommodated by the mutualism and multiplier accounts as well; they are not what distinguishes it.

The loop turns on cognitive learning — the induction of general theories from particular observations, as against reinforcement over particular outcomes — so something must supply the capacity to construct a model of a situation that is not present and run it. That is a question about architecture, not psychometrics, and nothing below turns on the answer. The author's own answer, developed in a companion paper on consciousness (Gruber, 2026), is that the capacity arises from a specific architecture of explicit self-modeling. It is one candidate. A reader who rejects it, or prefers another, can accept everything the recursive model claims and predicts.

## 4. Operational Knowledge: The Hidden Multiplier

### 4.1 What Operational Knowledge Is

Within the Knowledge component, two categories are distinguished:

- **Factual knowledge**: Knowledge of content — facts, concepts, procedures, cultural repertoire. This is what IQ tests (and educational systems) primarily measure under the rubric of "crystallized intelligence."

- **Operational knowledge** (*Metawissen*): Knowledge about *how to learn and think* — learning strategies, reasoning heuristics, metacognitive skills, strategic planning, logical tools, and the ability to evaluate one's own understanding. This is sometimes subsumed under the label "metacognition" (Flavell, 1979) or "self-regulated learning" (Zimmerman, 2002), but these labels do not fully capture the concept, which extends to include general-purpose reasoning strategies and logical tools that are not domain-specific.

### 4.2 Why Operational Knowledge Is the Multiplier

Operational knowledge occupies a special position in the recursive loop because it amplifies the *rate* of knowledge acquisition. Factual knowledge is additive: learning a new fact adds one fact to the store. Operational knowledge is multiplicative: learning a new learning strategy improves the efficiency of all subsequent learning.

Consider a concrete example. A student who learns the technique of *spaced repetition* (distributing practice over time rather than massing it) does not merely acquire one new fact. She acquires a tool that increases the retention rate of all subsequent learning. This is a fundamentally different kind of knowledge — it is knowledge that accelerates the recursive loop itself.

The intelligence literature has noted the existence of metacognitive skills and learning strategies. But it has not applied this insight. The distinction between factual and operational knowledge is noted and then set aside — treated as a curiosity rather than as the structurally decisive feature it is. In the present framework, operational knowledge is the transmission gear of the recursive loop: it determines how efficiently the turning of one component (Motivation → effort) translates into the turning of another (Knowledge → capability).

### 4.3 Implications for the AI Age

This distinction becomes acutely consequential in the age of artificial intelligence. When factual knowledge is instantly available to anyone with an internet connection, and when computational performance is available for the cost of an API call, the relative importance of the three components shifts dramatically.

Factual knowledge, which educational systems have traditionally spent most of their time transmitting, is no longer scarce. Performance, in the narrow sense of computational processing, is no longer exclusively biological. What remains uniquely human — and uniquely valuable — is the combination of intrinsic motivation and operational knowledge: the drive to learn *and* the meta-skill of knowing how to learn effectively.

If this analysis is correct, it implies that the most valuable thing an educational system can transmit is not factual knowledge but operational knowledge — the strategies, heuristics, and metacognitive skills that allow a learner to learn independently. In the AI age, *learning how to learn* is close to the only thing still worth teaching.

## 5. The AI Implication: What Machines Are Missing

The recursive model makes a specific prediction about artificial intelligence systems: they should exhibit a characteristic failure mode in which high Knowledge and high Performance do not produce the self-directed development that characterizes human intelligence.

Current artificial intelligence systems provide an unintended but remarkably clean test case for the recursive model — one that allows us to examine, facet by facet, what happens when Knowledge and Performance are scaled to superhuman levels while Motivation remains entirely absent.

### 5.1 A Facet-by-Facet Comparison

Consider the major facets of the CHC taxonomy and how current AI systems perform on each:

**Memory and retrieval.** Large language models (LLMs) trained on trillions of tokens have access to a store of factual knowledge that dwarfs any individual human's. In terms of sheer breadth of stored information, AI is unambiguously superior. This corresponds to the Knowledge component of the recursive model — and it is precisely the component that can be supplied externally, without recursion.

**Processing speed.** AI systems process information orders of magnitude faster than biological neural networks. Where a human working memory system operates at roughly 7 ± 2 chunks (Miller, 1956) with processing cycles measured in hundreds of milliseconds, LLMs process millions of tokens per second. This corresponds to the Performance component — and again, it can be engineered without recursion.

**Reasoning.** This is where the comparison becomes interesting. The most recent generation of "reasoning models" (e.g., OpenAI's o1 and o3 series, released 2024–2025) achieve performance on mathematical competition problems, graduate-level science exams, and complex logical tasks that would have been considered impossible for AI just two years ago. Yet these systems exhibit systematic failures that are structurally revealing. They "hallucinate" — generating confident but false outputs that superficially resemble correct reasoning. These hallucinations bear a striking resemblance to what Kahneman (2011) described as System 1 errors: fast, associative processing that produces plausible-sounding but incorrect results. Human intelligence addresses such errors through System 2 — slow, deliberate, effortful reasoning that monitors and corrects System 1 outputs. But System 2 is itself driven by motivation: the willingness to invest cognitive effort in checking one's own outputs, to tolerate the discomfort of uncertainty, to persist in reasoning when the easy answer is available. Gigerenzer (2007) has argued that the boundary between "error" and "adaptive heuristic" depends on the ecology in which the reasoning occurs — a point that underscores the role of the agent's goals and motivations in determining what counts as intelligent behavior in context. It is worth noting that the "reasoning" in reasoning models is not a structural innovation but a quantitative one: these systems use the same transformer architecture as their predecessors, trained via reinforcement learning to produce longer chain-of-thought sequences before generating a final answer. More computation per response — more tokens of intermediate "thinking" — but no qualitative change in the underlying neural architecture, though the inference procedure is significantly modified. The improvement is real but reveals its nature under pressure: the system allocates more processing to a problem, not different processing. AI systems can also be engineered to "check their work" through chain-of-thought prompting and verification loops, but these are externally imposed scaffolds — surrogate motivation supplied by the human designer, not endogenous drive arising from the system itself.

**Motivation.** Here the comparison is one of kind, not degree — but the usual form of the claim, that artificial systems have no motivation at all, is false. A substantial research programme has engineered exactly this. Schmidhuber (1991) proposed implementing curiosity and boredom directly in a model-building controller, rewarding the agent for improvement in its own world model; Oudeyer and Kaplan (2007) provided a typology of the resulting computational approaches; Pathak et al. (2017) showed that an intrinsic reward defined as prediction error in a learned feature space is sufficient to drive exploration in the complete absence of external reward. In the active-inference framework the point is stronger still: epistemic value — the expected reduction in uncertainty — falls out of the objective itself rather than being added to it, so that exploration is constitutive of inference and not an extra term (Friston et al., 2015). A theorist working in either tradition reads the claim that motivation must sit inside the model of cognition as something their framework already asserts.

Two things follow. The paper's thesis is not novel *as a claim about artificial agents*; it is novel as a claim about the theory and measurement of human intelligence, where, as Section 2 documents, the exclusion is real. And what these systems supply is an exploration drive over a fixed task distribution, while the deployed systems whose behaviour Section 5.2 describes — the large language models now doing the work that prompts comparisons with human intelligence — carry none. Between queries they do nothing: they do not seek out information, practise skills, or return to a problem left unresolved. The gap is therefore not that intrinsic motivation is unengineerable, but that the systems in which Knowledge and Performance have been scaled to superhuman levels are not the systems in which motivation has been engineered, and the two research programmes have not been joined at scale.

### 5.2 The Predicted Failure Mode

According to the recursive model, a system in which the M component is not present should not sustain the loop, whatever the levels of K and P. In deployed language models this is what is observed: they do not improve themselves between training runs, do not independently seek out areas of ignorance and address them, and do not show progressive intellectual development over time. Their capability is static, entirely determined by training, with no endogenous drive to extend it.

On its own the observation is weak: a purely architectural account predicts it equally well, since these systems have no persistent state between sessions, so nothing could accumulate even if something were driving it. The content is in the conditional. The recursive model predicts that supplying an intrinsic drive of the kind Section 5.1 describes to a system that *does* have persistent state and the capacity to modify itself will not, by itself, produce a self-sustaining developmental trajectory — that the resulting system will explore, and will improve on the distribution it explores, without the open-ended compounding across domains that the loop is meant to describe. If such a system does compound in that way, the model's claim about what the M component contributes is wrong, and the loop is doing less work than claimed. This is a prediction about an experiment that is buildable now, not a claim about what machines can never do.

The reasoning models sharpen this point. These systems solve competition-level problems when prompted but do not independently seek out problems, do not self-direct their learning, and require external scaffolding — prompts, reinforcement learning from human feedback, reward signals — that functions as a surrogate for the absent motivation component. Scaling Performance and Knowledge to extraordinary levels produces extraordinary outputs on demand, but not the self-sustaining developmental trajectory that defines intelligence.

The absence of operational knowledge integration provides a further illustration. A human learner who discovers a more effective reasoning strategy can immediately apply it to subsequent problems within the same cognitive session — the operational knowledge becomes part of the active processing. Current LLMs cannot do this. External scaffolding systems can modify the instructions given to an LLM (analogous to updating a set of rules the system follows), but such modifications do not integrate into the system's processing in the way that learned strategies integrate into human cognition. The system must be restarted with new instructions, at which point it is, in a meaningful sense, a new system — one that has no continuity of experience with its predecessor. This is the opposite of the recursive loop, in which operational knowledge acquired through motivated engagement continuously reshapes the cognitive process that produced it.

### 5.3 A Boundary Condition, and What It Is Not

The preceding argument is about a component and its consequences for a developmental trajectory. It says nothing about what a system's architecture must be like for that component to be present. The author holds a view on that question; the rest of this paper does not require it.

The view is this. As argued in Section 3.1, what the recursive loop draws on is not any single construct from the personality-intelligence literature but a unified evaluative function, and evaluating one's own states in the way that function describes appears to presuppose a model of oneself that can be run and inspected. In the architectural account developed separately (Gruber, 2026), that is what an explicit self-model is, and the conjecture is that motivation of the kind the loop needs cannot be supplied as a module bolted to a system that lacks one. Reinforcement learning from human feedback would then be the clear negative case: it shapes behaviour toward outputs humans rate as helpful without generating any endogenous evaluative process, and a system so shaped does not want to be helpful in any sense the loop can use.

Two reasons to treat the conjecture as separable. It rests on a theoretical framework that is the present author's own and has not been independently tested — a poor foundation for a claim in intelligence research, whoever had proposed it. And it is not needed: every empirical prediction in Section 7 concerns human data, and none turns on whether the evaluative function requires a self-model or can be engineered without one. If a system with no self-model of any kind sustains the loop, this subsection is refuted and the recursive model is untouched.

What the recursive model does claim about artificial systems is the narrower thing stated in Section 5.2: that supplying an exploration drive is not the same as supplying the M component, and that the difference should show up as the absence of open-ended compounding. That claim is testable without settling the architectural question at all.

## 6. The Learnability of Intelligence

### 6.1 A Structural Prediction

The recursive model yields an implication that is, in my view, more consequential than the AI case: it predicts that intelligence is, to a large extent, *learnable*.

This is not a feel-good platitude. It is a structural prediction that follows directly from the composition of the model's three components. Consider each in turn:

**Knowledge is entirely learnable.** This is true by definition. Knowledge — both factual and operational — is the accumulated product of learning. No one is born knowing calculus or knowing how to use spaced repetition. The entire Knowledge component, including the operational knowledge that functions as the system's multiplier (Section 4), is acquired through experience, instruction, and practice.

**Motivation is substantially learnable.** This claim requires more argument, but the evidence is strong. Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 2000) demonstrates that intrinsic motivation is not a fixed trait but a response to environmental conditions — specifically, to the satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs. Environments that support these needs cultivate intrinsic motivation; environments that thwart them extinguish it. Dweck's (2006) work on growth mindset shows that beliefs about the malleability of intelligence — themselves a form of knowledge — directly affect motivational persistence: students who believe intelligence is developable invest more effort and recover more effectively from failure. These beliefs are teachable (Yeager & Dweck, 2012). However, the empirical record is more complex than the initial enthusiasm suggested. Macnamara and Burgoyne (2023), in a comprehensive meta-analysis, found that growth mindset interventions produced negligible effects on academic achievement (*d* = 0.05 after correction for publication bias), with independent researchers finding significantly smaller effects than researchers with financial ties to mindset theory. This is consistent with the recursive model: changing beliefs about intelligence (a narrow sub-component of Motivation) without simultaneously addressing Knowledge and operational Knowledge cannot restart the recursive loop. The failure of mindset interventions, far from undermining the importance of motivation, illustrates what happens when motivation is treated as an add-on rather than as a constitutive component of a recursive system. Motivation is not purely innate temperament; it is shaped, for better or worse, by the environments and feedback systems that surround the learner — but effective intervention must engage the full loop, not merely one belief within one component.

**Performance has a biological ceiling — but the ceiling on the measure sits above the ceiling on the substrate, and neither is usually the binding constraint.** Cognitive processing capacity is the component most strongly influenced by genetics and neurobiology. Working memory capacity, processing speed, and the raw computational power of the neural substrate are not infinitely malleable, and the heritability of fluid ability in adulthood is among the better-replicated findings in the field. Three points qualify what follows from this, and the first two dispute none of it. First, heritability describes the sources of variance in a population across the range of environments that population happened to occupy. It does not fix the standing of any individual, and it does not establish that the observed range of environments is the possible range. Second, a fluid-reasoning score is not a direct read-out of substrate capacity. Reasoning with unfamiliar material requires constructing a model of the problem that the examinee does not have stored and then running it, and model construction is something a person becomes better at. To the extent that a Gf test scores that construction alongside the substrate carrying it, measured Gf is part capacity and part skill. This reading accommodates two findings that a pure-capacity account handles awkwardly: working memory training produces gains that are reliable on trained and near tasks while far transfer remains contested (Jaeggi et al., 2008; though see Melby-Lervåg & Hulme, 2013, for debate about transfer), which is what one expects if what improves is a constructed procedure rather than a capacity, and expertise routinely allows individuals to circumvent apparent working memory limits through chunking and automatization (Chase & Simon, 1973). Neither finding shows that the substrate is elastic. What they show is that the measured quantity is not only the substrate. Third — and this is the decisive point — for the vast majority of people, Performance is *not the bottleneck*. Average cognitive processing capacity is more than sufficient to support what most people would recognize as highly intelligent behavior in real-world contexts. The bottleneck, for most people most of the time, is Knowledge and Motivation.

### 6.2 Why Performance Is Not the Bottleneck

This claim deserves elaboration, because it runs counter to the implicit assumption of the psychometric tradition — that intelligence differences are primarily differences in cognitive processing capacity.

Consider the distribution of Gf in the general population. The difference between the 25th and 75th percentile in working memory capacity is real but modest. It means that one person can hold, roughly, one more chunk of information in mind than another. This difference matters at the extremes — in theoretical physics, in certain forms of mathematical proof, in competitive chess at the grandmaster level. But it matters far less in the contexts where most people live their lives: in learning a profession, in solving practical problems, in understanding complex arguments, in acquiring expertise in a domain of interest.

What separates the person who becomes expert in their field from the person who remains a novice is overwhelmingly not a difference in working memory capacity. It is a difference in accumulated knowledge (including operational knowledge — knowing how to learn effectively), sustained over time by a difference in motivation. The expert has iterated the recursive loop thousands of times; the novice stopped iterating early. The recursive model predicts that this difference in iteration count — driven primarily by K and M, not P — should dominate real-world outcomes, and it does.

To put it bluntly: a person of average cognitive processing capacity who is deeply motivated and who possesses strong operational knowledge will, over a lifetime, develop intellectual capabilities that far exceed those of a person with superior processing capacity but low motivation and poor learning strategies. The recursive loop is a compound interest machine, and compound interest cares more about the rate of deposit (Motivation) and the investment strategy (operational Knowledge) than about the initial principal (Performance).

A caveat is necessary here: this argument applies to the broad middle of the cognitive distribution. At the extremes — individuals with significant cognitive impairments, or tasks requiring exceptional processing capacity (theoretical physics, grandmaster-level chess) — Performance does become the binding constraint. The recursive model does not deny the reality of individual differences in Gf, and it does not treat them as wholly acquired. It argues that the loop amplifies K and M differences far more than P differences across the lifespan, and that some part of the P difference measured in adulthood is itself an output of that amplification rather than an input to it. For the broad middle of the distribution — which is where most people are — Performance is sufficient. It is K and M that determine the trajectory.

### 6.3 Grading as an Intervention on the Motivation Component

If intelligence is largely learnable, and if the recursive loop is driven primarily by Motivation and Knowledge, an implication follows that is worth stating precisely, because it is easy to state too strongly. A practice that reduces motivation in learners is, on this model, not merely failing to develop intelligence; it is acting on the component that determines how often the loop iterates, and the model predicts that such an intervention will have consequences that outlast the occasion. Conventional grading is the practice this paper is most concerned with, and the argument for treating it this way is set out here as a prediction of the model rather than as an established finding.

Consider what happens when a child receives a poor grade. The grade is presented as a measurement — an objective assessment of ability or performance. Through the lens of the recursive model it is also an intervention on the Motivation component: it communicates *you are not good enough at this*, which for a child who has not yet acquired a robust account of ability as developable translates readily into *you are not intelligent enough*. The prediction is that self-efficacy falls, that intrinsic motivation to engage with the subject falls with it, and that the willingness to invest effort — the fuel of the loop — is reduced.

The structural consequence is what makes the claim more than a restatement of a familiar complaint. A poor grade reduces M. Reduced M means fewer iterations of the loop. Fewer iterations mean slower growth in K. Slower growth in K means worse performance on subsequent assessments, and worse performance means more poor grades. Where the loop runs forward as compounding growth, here it runs in the other direction, and the assessment system is then not only measuring an outcome but contributing to the outcome it measures — a self-fulfilling prophecy in the technical sense (Merton, 1948).

The evidence bearing on this is more equivocal than accounts of it usually allow, and two constraints have to be carried, not passed over. The first concerns the teacher-expectancy pathway. Rosenthal and Jacobson's (1968) Pygmalion study has been criticized on methodological grounds, and while later work confirms that teacher expectations do influence student outcomes, Jussim and Harber's (2005) review of three decades of research concludes that such effects are typically small, that they do not accumulate greatly across teachers or over time and may be more likely to dissipate than to accumulate, and that teacher expectations predict student outcomes substantially because those expectations are *accurate* rather than because they are self-fulfilling. That conclusion is in direct tension with the compounding prediction of Section 6.4, and the tension should be acknowledged as such: for the expectancy pathway specifically, the best available synthesis reports the opposite of compounding. The second constraint concerns stereotype threat (Steele & Aronson, 1995), which is often cited as the same mechanism running from the other direction; the size and robustness of that effect have since been contested on replication and publication-bias grounds, and it cannot be treated as settled support.

Two readings survive these constraints. The weaker is that the compounding claim is simply wrong. The stronger, and the one the model commits to, is that grading acts through a different route than teacher expectancy: not another person's belief about the child transmitted through differential treatment, but a repeated, explicit, institutionally sanctioned signal delivered to the child about the child's own standing, which is the pathway Self-Determination Theory identifies as competence-need frustration (Deci & Ryan, 2000). These are separable pathways with separable predictions, and the model's claim about grading should not be allowed to borrow support from the expectancy literature that the expectancy literature does not give.

The claim is testable, and the quasi-experimental variation already exists. Schools and systems differ in when graded assessment begins and in whether it is used at all: Montessori and other mastery-oriented programmes replace ranked grading with criterion-referenced progression; several national systems delay numerical grading into the middle school years while comparable systems do not; and individual districts have shifted grading policy at datable moments. The prediction stated in Section 6.4 — divergence that widens with time since exposure rather than a constant offset — is what distinguishes the recursive account from the alternative that grading simply sorts children who were already diverging. Testing it requires following the same cohorts across several years and comparing the *shape* of the trajectory, not its level at any one point.

### 6.4 Compounding Effects: A Structural Prediction

The recursive model makes a specific, testable prediction about the time course of these effects: motivation-destroying interventions should produce damage that *compounds* over time, not damage that remains static. A single bad grade in first grade does not merely reduce motivation in first grade; it slightly reduces the rate at which the loop iterates, which produces a slightly smaller knowledge base by second grade, which produces slightly worse performance in second grade, which produces another discouraging signal, and so on. The recursive structure predicts that early motivational damage should be visible as an accelerating divergence from peers — a fanning out of trajectories that grows wider with each passing year.

Conversely, the model predicts that motivation-enhancing interventions — environments that support autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Deci & Ryan, 2000); feedback systems that emphasize growth rather than fixed ability (Dweck, 2006); educational practices that teach operational knowledge explicitly (Dignath & Büttner, 2008) — should produce benefits that *compound* over time. An intervention that boosts M in first grade should show larger effects at five-year follow-up than at one-year follow-up, because the additional loop iterations accumulate.

Some evidence supports this compounding pattern. Heckman's (2006) analysis of early childhood interventions, including the Perry Preschool Project, shows returns that grow over time — larger effects at age 27 than at age 7 — not because initial cognitive gains persist (they often fade), but because motivational and self-regulatory gains compound through subsequent learning. This is precisely what a recursive model predicts and precisely what a static-trait model does not.

### 6.5 Implications for Educational Practice

If the recursive model is correct, the most effective educational interventions are not those that maximize the transmission of factual knowledge (the current default), nor those that attempt to boost raw cognitive processing capacity (which has limited malleability). The most effective interventions are those that target the two learnable components — Knowledge (especially operational knowledge) and Motivation — in ways designed to initiate and sustain the recursive loop.

Specifically, the model implies:

1. **Teach operational knowledge explicitly.** Learning strategies, metacognitive skills, and reasoning heuristics should not be incidental byproducts of content instruction — they should be a primary focus, because they function as the multiplier within the recursive loop.

2. **Treat motivation as a protected quantity.** On this model, an educational practice that reduces intrinsic motivation is acting on the component that governs how often the loop iterates, and its cost should therefore be counted over years rather than assessed at the point of delivery. Punitive grading is the case developed above; ability tracking, competitive ranking, and any practice that communicates to children that their intellectual capacity is fixed are candidates for the same treatment, though each would need testing on its own terms rather than inheriting the conclusion.

3. **Replace grades with growth feedback.** If assessment is necessary — and it may be, for diagnostic purposes — it should take a form that feeds the recursive loop rather than breaking it. This means feedback that is specific, actionable, and oriented toward improvement rather than toward ranking. It means communicating to learners not "you scored 60%" but "here is what you have mastered, here is what to work on next, and here is how."

4. **Recognize that average Performance is sufficient.** Educational systems should stop treating cognitive processing capacity as the primary constraint on intellectual development. For most students, it is not. The binding constraints are motivation and operational knowledge, and both are responsive to intervention.

None of this is entirely new. Several established educational approaches already align with the recursive model's predictions: Montessori education prioritizes autonomy and intrinsic motivation; mastery learning (Bloom, 1968) replaces competitive grading with criterion-referenced progression that protects self-efficacy; portfolio assessment shifts the focus from summative ranking to growth documentation. What the recursive model adds is a *structural explanation* for why these practices work: they target the components of the recursive loop that are both most learnable and most consequential for long-term intellectual development. The model transforms what might otherwise appear to be soft pedagogical preferences into hard predictions about the dynamics of a formal system.

## 7. Discussion

### 7.1 Relation to Established Intelligence Models

The proposal advanced here does not require abandoning existing intelligence models. The CHC taxonomy remains a useful descriptive framework for the cognitive components of intelligence. Cattell's investment theory remains a useful account of the Gf-to-Gc developmental trajectory. What this paper proposes is that these models are *incomplete* — they describe the Performance and Knowledge components of intelligence with considerable precision while systematically excluding the Motivation component that drives the developmental dynamics.

The recursive model can be understood as an extension of Cattell's investment theory that (a) makes the investor explicit, (b) formalizes the recursive feedback structure, and (c) identifies operational knowledge as the rate-limiting factor in the loop.

The recursive model also explains a structural property of intelligence that factor-analytic approaches have difficulty accommodating: intelligence behaves as a *polythetic* construct (Beckner, 1959). In a polythetic class, members share most but not all defining properties, and no single property is necessary or sufficient for membership. Multiple K × P × M configurations can produce equifinal outcomes — high knowledge compensates for moderate performance, sustained motivation compensates for late starts, strong operational knowledge accelerates the loop regardless of starting conditions. This is why early IQ (which primarily captures Performance) is a poor predictor of adult intellectual achievement, and why factor-analytic models that impose a single underlying dimension systematically underfit the observed data.

This is consistent with recent large-cohort evidence. Edwards and DeYoung (2026), analyzing two nationally representative U.S. longitudinal samples (NLSY79, *n* = 11,914; NLSY97, *n* = 7,008) with sibling-based within-family controls, report that specific cognitive abilities carry independent predictive weight for educational, occupational and income outcomes over and above general intelligence — on the order of 30 to 57 percent of *g*'s importance, against a conventional expectation that the residual contribution is negligible. Their sibling design removes shared family confounds. Two qualifications belong with the citation: the analysis is conducted within the classical *g*-plus-group-factors psychometric tradition rather than a recursive or relational one, so it converges with the present claim without testing it; and the specific factors did not replicate cleanly across the two cohorts. What it establishes is the weaker but sufficient point that structure below the general factor carries real independent signal, which is what a single-dimension summary discards.

Independent within-person evidence indicates that this is not merely a modelling preference. Schmiedek et al. (2020), analyzing the COGITO study — nine cognitive tasks measured on approximately 100 occasions per participant — found that the structure of within-person daily performance differs systematically from the between-person structure of cognitive abilities, and that the general factor recovered between persons does not describe how cognition is organized within a person. That work does not test motivation and was not conducted with the present framework in view; what it establishes is that between-person factor structure cannot be assumed to serve as a surrogate for the within-person process. This matters here because the recursive loop is a within-person process. The present model adds an account of what organizes that process: the K × P × M interaction iterating across developmental time.

The capacity the model assigns to the Performance and Motivation components — constructing a representation of something not present and running it — is not an import into intelligence research from outside psychology. Memory research arrived at it independently. Bartlett (1932) established that remembering is reconstruction rather than replay; Schacter and Addis's (2007) constructive episodic simulation hypothesis proposes that memory is reconstructive because its function is to supply recombinable elements for the simulation of events that have not occurred; and Hassabis et al. (2007) found that patients with hippocampal amnesia were markedly impaired at imagining novel experiences, their attempts lacking spatial coherence. This is consistent with the present framework rather than a test of it. What it establishes is that remembering and prospection draw on a common constructive capacity — the capacity the recursive loop requires, and one that no current intelligence model represents.

It would suit a process account of intelligence if the brain-network findings showed that abler individuals reconfigure their networks more flexibly under task demands. They show close to the opposite. Hilger et al. (2017) found intelligence associated with the modular organization of *intrinsic* networks; Hilger et al. (2020) found it associated with greater temporal *stability* of those modules; and Schultz and Cole (2016) found that higher intelligence predicts *less* task-related network reconfiguration, not more. The picture these converge on is of a system organized efficiently enough in advance that it need not re-wire itself for each new demand.

This is not evidence against the recursive model, and it is not evidence for it. It bears on the Performance component — the standing organization of the substrate — on a timescale of seconds to minutes, whereas the loop is a claim about what accumulates across years. Nothing in the present framework predicts what network dynamics should look like during a single task, and a finding at one timescale is not support for a claim at the other. The recursive model's neural commitments, if it has any, remain to be derived; it currently has none (Section 7.4).

### 7.2 Testable Predictions

The recursive model generates several testable predictions:

1. **Motivation predicts long-term intellectual development beyond IQ**: Measures of intellectual curiosity and learning drive, assessed in childhood, should predict adult intellectual achievement (education, creative output, problem-solving ability) beyond what childhood IQ predicts. Existing evidence supports this (von Stumm et al., 2011; Murayama et al., 2013), but more long-term longitudinal studies are needed.

2. **Operational knowledge moderates the motivation-achievement relationship**: The effect of motivation on intellectual development should be stronger in individuals who also possess effective learning strategies. Motivation without operational knowledge produces effort without efficiency; operational knowledge without motivation produces capability without deployment.

3. **The Matthew effect should be strongest where all three components co-vary**: The rich-get-richer dynamic should be most pronounced in individuals who are simultaneously high in cognitive performance, knowledge, and motivation. The model predicts that the Matthew effect is weakest when motivation is low, even if performance and knowledge are high — because the loop lacks the driving force to iterate.

4. **Interventions targeting operational knowledge should have outsized long-term effects**: If operational knowledge is the multiplier in the recursive loop, then teaching learning strategies should produce effects that compound over time — larger at long follow-up than at short follow-up. Some evidence supports this (Dignath & Büttner, 2008), but it has not been interpreted in the context of a recursive intelligence model.

5. **AI systems will not exhibit self-directed intellectual development until they have functional motivation analogues**: This is a prediction about the future trajectory of AI development. It implies that the path to artificial general intelligence runs through motivation engineering, not merely through scaling Knowledge and Performance.

6. **Motivation-destroying educational interventions should produce compounding negative effects**: The recursive model predicts that practices such as punitive grading, ability tracking, and fixed-ability labeling should show damage that accelerates over time — with effect sizes at 5-year follow-up significantly larger than at 1-year follow-up when the same cohort is tracked longitudinally. Conversely, motivation-enhancing interventions should show compounding benefits on the same timescale, distinguishing recursive compounding from simple persistence of initial gains.

7. **Properly aggregated motivation measures should show substantially stronger correlations with intelligence than currently reported**: The existing literature reports motivation-intelligence correlations of approximately r = .20–.35 (von Stumm & Ackerman, 2013). A reviewer might reasonably ask: if the relationship is this modest, why call it a major omission? The answer lies in Brunswik symmetry (Wittmann, 1988; Wittmann & Klumb, 2006). The modest correlations are, in large part, measurement artifacts. Motivation is typically assessed with single questionnaire scales capturing a narrow behavioral window — a state-level snapshot of a broad dispositional function. These narrow measures are then correlated with broad intelligence criteria (full test batteries yielding composite scores). The resulting asymmetry between predictor bandwidth and criterion bandwidth systematically attenuates the observed correlation, exactly as Wittmann's framework predicts. Wittmann and Klumb (2006), analyzing the parameters of the augmented Tucker lens model equation, demonstrated that for any observed effect size in psychological research, there are six sources of underestimation (range restriction, unreliability of both treatment and criterion, lack of construct symmetry, and negative sampling error) against only two sources of overestimation (range enhancement and positive sampling error). The odds are structurally stacked toward underestimation. Applied to motivation-intelligence correlations: the reported values of r ≈ .20–.35 are almost certainly attenuated by multiple simultaneous artifacts. The present model therefore predicts that when motivation is measured with the same care for bandwidth matching that intelligence researchers apply to cognitive assessment — aggregated across multiple contexts, occasions, and expression modalities — the motivation-intelligence correlation will prove substantially larger than the values currently reported in meta-analyses.

Stated without a limit, an argument of this shape excuses any weak correlation. The decisive design is a multi-occasion experience-sampling motivation battery administered alongside a full cognitive battery in a longitudinal sample. If motivation aggregated symmetrically over that design — many occasions, several contexts, matched in bandwidth to a composite cognitive criterion — still correlates with intelligence at approximately the *r* = .20 to .35 currently reported, then the attenuation account is wrong and the modest correlations are substantive rather than artifactual. On that outcome the unity claim of Section 3.1 loses its principal support and the case for treating motivation as a constitutive component rather than a correlate would have to be made on other grounds or abandoned. The author regards *r* ≥ .50 under symmetric aggregation as the result the model requires, and anything at or below the currently reported range as a disconfirmation rather than a measurement problem to be explained away.

8. **Motivation should predict behavioral consistency across intellectual engagement situations, not merely effort level on single occasions**: The recursive loop iterates through repeated engagement episodes across diverse situations. The Motivation component's primary contribution to the loop is therefore not how hard one tries on any given occasion, but how *consistently* one engages across situations and over time. Wittmann's demonstration with Eysenck's personality dimensions provides the methodological model: Extraversion predicted the *mean* of aggregated behavioral indicators across situations, while Neuroticism predicted their *intraindividual standard deviation* (Wittmann, 1988). Applied to the recursive model: motivation should primarily predict the consistency (low intraindividual variability) of intellectual engagement across diverse situations. Highly motivated individuals engage consistently; low-motivation individuals engage sporadically. The loop compounds through iteration count, not iteration intensity. This predicts that the strongest longitudinal predictor of intellectual development is not peak motivation (highest observed engagement) but motivational consistency (lowest variance in engagement across occasions) — a prediction that is directly testable with experience-sampling methods. Within-person data of the required form already exist: Brose et al. (2010), using daily measures of effort and enjoyment alongside working memory performance in the COGITO study, found that among participants whose motivation varied, higher-motivation days were also better-performance days. The coupling was heterogeneous across individuals and weak or absent in the older sample, which is what a model treating motivation as one interacting component predicts rather than a uniform main effect. What COGITO did not do is aggregate motivation symmetrically against a matched cognitive criterion or separate the subtest classes of Section 7.3; the design required to test this prediction alongside prediction 7 is specified there.

9. **Aggregating imagination measures across modalities, not merely across occasions, should raise their convergence with fluid reasoning**: The argument of Section 3.1 — that a single function appears in the literature as a family of separate constructs because different instruments observe it in different contexts — is not specific to motivation. The capacity to construct a model of something not present and run it is an operation rather than a location: it is carried out in whatever representational system holds the content being modelled, and is therefore instantiated separately for visual scenes, for narrative episodes, for spatial layouts, and for relational structure. Any single instrument samples one instantiation. Imagery-vividness scales sample the visual one, daydreaming inventories and absorption scales (Tellegen & Atkinson, 1974) the narrative one, matrix and mental-rotation tasks the relational and spatial ones. The literature's current answer is that these do not converge with reasoning ability: Ackerman and Heggestad's (1997) meta-analysis puts the Openness-Gf correlation at *r* = .08, against .30 with Gc, and Nusbaum and Silvia (2011), working with latent variables, found that the Openness aspect predicted creative behavior but not fluid intelligence while the Intellect aspect predicted fluid intelligence but not creative behavior. The present model reads that dissociation as a modality projection rather than a structural boundary, and predicts that aggregation across modalities will recover convergence that aggregation within a single modality does not. This yields a contrast that separates the two claims. Bandwidth asymmetry alone (prediction 7) predicts that observed convergence rises with the number of aggregated observations, whatever their source. The present model predicts more: that *k* observations spanning four modalities will converge with aggregated fluid ability more strongly than *k* observations of one modality gathered across *k* occasions. If the two aggregation schemes perform equivalently at matched *k*, the mechanistic claim is wrong and only the psychometric one survives.

A further consequence, which can be tested on data already collected and which specifies in advance what would disconfirm it, is developed separately in Section 7.3.

### 7.3 A Direct Test: Subtest Composition and Secular Change

The recursive model implies a partition of standard test content that cuts across the usual verbal/performance and Gf/Gc divisions. Some subtests require the examinee to construct a model of a situation that is not present and then run it. Matrix reasoning and Raven's progressive matrices require building and testing a rule that no item states; block design and mental rotation require transforming a represented figure; Similarities requires constructing a superordinate relation that neither term supplies; Comprehension items about hypothetical circumstances require simulating an agent's situation. Other subtests require retrieval from a store: Vocabulary, Information, and arithmetic-fact items. Call these simulation-loaded and retrieval-loaded. The partition is not the Gf/Gc distinction — Similarities and Comprehension are scored as verbal, knowledge-dependent subtests — and it is not the verbal/nonverbal distinction. Two subtests are contested, and both are declared here, before the data are inspected. Arithmetic loads on working memory, yet its items call for the application of a taught procedure rather than the construction of a novel one, and the classification assigns it to the retrieval-loaded class. Coding is the harder case and the partition does not accommodate it: it is a speeded clerical task, requiring neither model construction nor retrieval from a knowledge store, and Flynn and Weiss attribute its gains to the tempo of visual media rather than to anything in the present account. It is therefore excluded from the classification altogether. This is a real cost, since Coding gained 18.00 points and is among the largest movers in the record; any test of the predictions below must set it aside on the stated grounds, not let it count toward the simulation-loaded class merely because it is a Performance subtest.

The partition sorts the largest secular changes on record. Across the American Wechsler standardizations from 1947–48 to 2001.75, Flynn and Weiss (2007, Table 2) report a gain of 23.85 IQ points on Similarities and gains ranging from 11.70 to 21.50 points across the five Performance subtests, against 4.40 points on Vocabulary, 2.30 on Arithmetic and 2.15 on Information, with Comprehension at 11.00 and a Full Scale gain of between 16.83 and 17.63 points over the whole 54.25-year period. Two features of that table qualify what can be drawn from it. The gains for Object Assembly and Picture Arrangement are Flynn and Weiss's own estimates rather than measurements, since the WISC-IV retained neither subtest, and they are bracketed as such in the source; the argument here does not rest on them. And Digit Span, which was not among the ten core subtests, shows almost no movement at all across 1972 to 2002 — one IQ point — which is the expected result for a subtest that asks for the maintenance and manipulation of given material rather than the construction of anything. At the level of domains rather than subtests, Pietschnig and Voracek's (2015) meta-analysis of 271 samples from 31 countries estimates annual gains of 0.41 IQ points for fluid and 0.30 for spatial performance, against 0.21 for crystallized. In the Norwegian conscript series, Sundet et al. (2004) found that from the early 1970s the gains in general ability were driven almost exclusively by the Raven-like subtest, with the language and mathematics subtests contributing little, until the gains on the Raven-like test themselves stopped in the late 1990s.

Flynn's own account (Flynn & Weiss, 2007; Flynn, 2007) attributes this pattern to a secular shift toward abstract and scientific habits of classification — children have come to "view the world through the spectacles provided by science," and to take seriously problems with no obvious practical payoff — rather than to any change in motivation, and the present framework does not displace that reading. What it adds is a reason why these two classes of subtest should be the ones that separate: a simulation-loaded item asks for model construction, which is the operation the recursive loop trains, while a retrieval-loaded item asks for a lookup against a store the loop fills only incidentally.

Three predictions follow, none of them yet tested against a classification fixed in advance.

The differential pattern should extend to the reversal. If the environmental conditions that support the loop have degraded, as Bratsberg and Rogeberg's (2018) within-family analysis indicates, the decline should fall disproportionately on simulation-loaded content, mirroring the ascent. The Norwegian conscript archive carries its three subtests separately and can settle this directly.

The two classes should differ in trainability. Interventions that teach model construction should move simulation-loaded scores more than retrieval-loaded scores; content instruction should do the reverse. This predicts the shape of the working-memory-training record: transfer should appear where the trained operation and the criterion share the construction demand and fail where they do not.

The two classes should differ in their coupling to within-person motivational state: simulation-loaded performance should covary with momentary engagement more strongly than retrieval-loaded performance does, because the former requires sustained construction and the latter does not. This limb is not a separate request for a study. Predictions 7 and 8 and this third limb are three read-outs of one design — a multi-occasion experience-sampling motivation battery administered alongside a cognitive battery in a longitudinal sample, with structure estimated within rather than between persons. That single design yields the symmetric aggregation that prediction 7 stakes a disconfirmation criterion on, the intraindividual variability that prediction 8 identifies as the operative quantity, and the subtest-by-engagement coupling asked for here. The COGITO study (Brose et al., 2010; Schmiedek et al., 2020) is the methodological precedent for all three and demonstrates that data of this form can be collected at the required density.

Two constraints govern the test. Subtest score changes cannot be read as changes in a latent ability without invariance testing: Wicherts et al. (2004) showed that the Wechsler scales are not measurement invariant over time, and that the observed pattern of subtest gains is not what a change in common-factor means would produce. The prediction is therefore stated over observed subtest scores, and any latent reading requires the invariance model to be fitted first. That constraint is also what makes the pattern informative here. A secular change not carried by the common factor is what the Gignac and Zajenkowski (2024) dissociation reports, and it is what the recursive model expects when scores rise without the loop being engaged.

The prediction is disconfirmed if simulation-loaded and retrieval-loaded subtests show statistically indistinguishable secular trajectories once the classification is fixed in advance, or if the reversal falls on retrieval-loaded content while simulation-loaded scores hold. Competing accounts do not generate the contrast: a test-familiarity account predicts gains concentrated on whatever content is most practiced, and a nutrition or general-health account predicts a broadly uniform lift.

### 7.4 Limitations

This paper presents a theoretical framework, not empirical data. The recursive model is offered as a conceptual tool for integrating existing findings and generating new predictions, not as a fully specified formal model. Formalizing the model mathematically — specifying the functional forms of the recursive interactions, the role of time and developmental stage, and the boundary conditions under which the loop amplifies, stagnates, or collapses — is the task for a subsequent paper.

The distinction between factual and operational knowledge, while intuitively clear, is not always sharp in practice. Many items of knowledge have both factual and operational aspects (e.g., knowing that spaced repetition works is factual; knowing how to implement it is operational). A more precise taxonomy of knowledge types, grounded in empirical data, would strengthen the framework.

The model also does not address the neuroscience of motivation in detail. The dopaminergic reward system, the role of the anterior cingulate cortex in effort allocation, and the neuromodulatory mechanisms that sustain engagement over time are all relevant but beyond the scope of a theoretical critique paper. More than that, and as Section 7.1 records, the framework makes no predictions at the neural level at all: it does not say what network dynamics should accompany a loop iteration, and it cannot currently be confirmed or embarrassed by the brain-network literature in either direction. A process account that could be tested neurally as well as psychometrically would be a stronger theory than this one, and deriving such predictions is the clearest route to making it so.

Finally, there is a deeper limitation that the model shares with every intelligence framework: it says nothing about what intelligence is *for*. If intelligence does not result in a good life — in well-being, in flourishing — is it really intelligence? The recursive loop can amplify itself indefinitely, but amplification without direction is not wisdom. A complete account of intelligence may ultimately require not just a theory of cognitive dynamics but a companion theory of living well. This paper does not attempt that integration, but it would be dishonest not to flag it.

A final limitation concerns the boundary of the argument. The recursive loop requires cognitive learning, and cognitive learning requires the capacity to construct a model of what is not present and run it; what supplies that capacity is left open here (Section 3.4). The author's companion work on consciousness (Gruber, 2026) proposes an architectural answer, in terms of two kinds of model — implicit and explicit — applied to two domains, world and self, with the explicit self-model as the locus of the evaluative function. That proposal is a candidate, not an established result, and has not been independently tested; a theory of intelligence should not be made to depend on it. Sections 5.3 and 3.1 draw on it and say so. Nothing in Sections 6 or 7 requires it. The relationship between the two frameworks is a conjecture the author intends to pursue.

### 7.5 A Historical Note

The exclusion of motivation is not an inevitable consequence of studying cognition — it is a historical choice. Binet stressed that motivation affects test results; Wechsler (1940, 1943) explicitly called for the inclusion of non-intellective factors (Section 2.4). The psychometric tradition's reliance on factor analysis of cognitive test scores created a self-reinforcing methodological loop — intelligence is what intelligence tests measure, intelligence tests measure cognitive abilities, therefore intelligence is a cognitive construct (Sternberg, 1985) — whose specific consequence for the treatment of motivation has not been systematically examined until now.

## 8. Conclusion

The systematic exclusion of motivation from intelligence models is not a harmless simplification. It produces a fundamentally incomplete picture: one that cannot explain the self-reinforcing dynamics of intellectual development, that renders invisible the crucial role of operational knowledge, and that leaves the field unable to account for the characteristic limitations of artificial intelligence systems.

Intelligence, I argue, is not a capacity. It is a *learning ability* — a recursive system in which Knowledge, Performance, and Motivation interact to produce a self-reinforcing developmental trajectory whose course is determined by the dynamics of the loop as it iterates across the lifespan, not by any single component measured at a single point in time.

The recursive model implies that intelligence is largely learnable — that two of its three components are highly responsive to environmental influence and intervention, and that the recursive structure amplifies gains over time. Including motivation is not merely a matter of completeness but of structural adequacy: a model of intelligence that excludes motivation is like a model of combustion that includes fuel and oxygen but excludes heat. And the cost of this exclusion is not theoretical. When educational systems, hiring practices, and social institutions build on a model that reduces intelligence to cognitive performance, they create environments that systematically destroy the very component that drives intellectual development — measured in children who stop trying, in potential never realized, and in a collective future diminished by every mind that was told it was not enough.
