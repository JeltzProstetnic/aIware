No major model of intelligence carries motivation inside its formal structure; the closest, Ackerman's PPIK, lets interests and personality direct intellectual investment while keeping both outside the intelligence construct. The field has treated this as an omission to be repaired by adding a component. This paper argues that the omission is a symptom rather than the problem. What went wrong is a matter of type: the tradition typed every constituent of intelligence as a capacity, because that is what psychometric instruments were built to measure. On the account developed here they are not all the same kind of thing. Performance — processing capacity — is the only one of the three that is a capacity in the psychometric sense. Knowledge is not one constituent but two, divided by content into what is known and what is known about learning; the physical boundary between a stored disposition and its running exercise is a second distinction, cutting across all three constituents and dividing none. And motivation is a constituent of a third type: it sits in the loop, and the loop's outcomes revise it, but it is not a capacity — it is the policy that allocates the loop's time, a schedule rather than a substance. Re-typing motivation changes what should be expected of measurement, which is why it is not a relabelling. Four results the field currently reports without an account follow from it directly: motivation's long resistance to trait measurement, which is what an instrument finds when it looks for the magnitude of something that has none; the attenuation of motivation-intelligence correlations under narrow single-occasion sampling, which becomes a consequence of what is being sampled and not an excuse for a disappointing number; the contrast between the durable effect of schooling on measured intelligence and the absence of far transfer from working-memory training against active controls, which is what follows if education re-points the schedule while training drills the capacity measure; and the prediction that motivation should predict the consistency of intellectual engagement rather than its peak intensity, because a loop compounds through iteration count. Two results would defeat it: a symmetric aggregation of motivation measures that still returned the currently reported average correlation, and a demonstration that peak engagement outpredicts consistency over long-term intellectual development. The recursive structure is not claimed as novel — mutualism and multiplier accounts formalized reciprocal causation two decades ago. What is claimed is that one of the three things being reciprocally caused has been the wrong kind of thing all along, and that correcting its type explains why the instruments behaved as they did.
