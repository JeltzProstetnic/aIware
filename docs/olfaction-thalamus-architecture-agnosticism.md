# Olfaction-Bypasses-Thalamus as Empirical Reinforcement of FMT Architecture-Agnosticism

## The argument

Within a single biological system, olfaction provides a natural experiment for architecture-agnostic theories of consciousness:

1. Olfaction is the only sensory modality whose **first-order** cortical projection bypasses the thalamus (olfactory bulb → piriform cortex direct).
2. A secondary loop exists via mediodorsal thalamus (MD) → orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), but it is *not* on the first-order route.
3. Despite this, olfactory experience is fully phenomenally conscious and binds with thalamically-routed taste and somatosensation into the unified percept of flavor.

If consciousness required a specific anatomical pathway (e.g., thalamocortical relay), this would be anomalous. On a functional-closure account — FMT's claim — it is exactly what should be expected: integration is at the level of the implicit model, not the wiring.

## Empirical anchor: MD thalamus lesions do NOT abolish conscious smell

This is the load-bearing empirical claim. Verdict from the human lesion literature: **MD thalamus is NOT constitutive for phenomenal olfactory experience.** Its role is in higher-order processing — identification, hedonic valence, attentional orientation — downstream of conscious detection.

| Study | N | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Sela et al. (2009).** *J Neurosci* 29:12059. PMID 19793964 | 17 thalamic lesion patients | **Detection spared**; identification impaired; hedonics impaired (right lateralized); sniff-vigor modulation lost |
| Stevenson & Miller (2010). *Neurocase* 17:148. PMID 20818539 | 6 MDNT patients | General olfaction mostly normal; olfactory attention impaired |
| Tham, Stevenson & Miller (2011). *Brain Cogn* 77:246. PMID 21705130 | 4 MDNT patients | Olfactory attention impaired; oral capture enhanced |
| Rousseaux et al. (1996). *Stroke* 27:2328. PMID 8969802 | 1 bilateral paramedian | Hedonic quality altered at 2yr follow-up; identification preserved |
| **Courtiol & Wilson (2015).** *Front Neural Circuits* 9:49. PMID 26441548 | Review | Consensus: "MDT damage does not produce anosmia or detection deficit" |
| **Li & Gottfried et al. (2010).** *Psychol Sci* 21:1454. PMID 20817780 | 1 right OFC lesion | **Complete conscious anosmia** with preserved "blind smell" — OFC, not thalamus, is the consciousness-determining lesion site |
| Shepherd (2005). *Neuron* 46:166. PMID 15848795 | Theoretical anchor | "Perception without a Thalamus: How Does Olfaction Do It?" — olfactory cortex gating states sufficient |

**The Li & Gottfried (2010) result is the keystone.** Complete conscious olfactory loss required a *right OFC* lesion, not a thalamic one — and unconscious olfactory processing ("blind smell") was preserved. This is the strongest evidence that the thalamic relay is not the consciousness-determining bottleneck for smell; the cortical destination is.

## Methodological hedge

Paramedian thalamic infarcts typically involve more than MD nucleus alone — intralaminar nuclei, mammillothalamic tract, and adjacent structures often co-infarct (Rousseaux 1996 explicitly notes extension to "adjacent lamella medialis and intralaminar nuclei"). The co-damaged intralaminar nuclei contribute to arousal/attention broadly, so attention deficits attributed to MD loss may partly reflect general arousal disruption.

This *strengthens* the detection-sparing finding: even with substantial (sometimes bilateral) thalamic damage extending beyond MD, conscious smell detection survives.

Caveat: all human studies have small N (4–17); none used phenomenal consciousness measures in the strict (philosophical) sense. The "not anosmic" consensus is solid; the "conscious experience fully intact" claim is the most parsimonious reading of the available data, not directly measured.

## Defensible formulation for the FMT paper

> "Within a single biological system, olfaction provides a natural experiment: its primary cortical access bypasses the thalamus, yet olfactory experience is fully conscious and binds with thalamically-routed taste and somatosensory signals into the unified percept of flavor. The human lesion literature confirms that mediodorsal thalamus damage does not abolish phenomenal odor detection — only higher-order olfactory functions (identification, hedonic valence, attentional orientation) are impaired (Sela et al. 2009; Courtiol & Wilson 2015). Complete conscious anosmia requires a cortical lesion in right OFC (Li & Gottfried 2010), not a thalamic one. If consciousness depended on a specific anatomical pathway, this would be anomalous. On a functional-closure account, it is exactly what should be expected: integration is at the level of the implicit model, not the wiring. Olfaction is a within-organism existence proof that the same kind of conscious experience can be achieved via different anatomical scaffolding — a half-step toward the substrate-independence FMT predicts; full substrate-independence is a further prediction that only non-biological implementations can decisively test."

## What FMT can and cannot claim

**Can claim:**
- Olfaction is a within-organism existence proof that the same functional outcome (conscious experience binding with other modalities into a unified percept) can be implemented via different anatomical scaffolding.
- This is empirical reinforcement for FMT's architecture-agnostic commitment: closure is at the *model* level, not the wiring level.
- The thalamocortical-relay variant of consciousness theories (Edelman/Tononi dynamic core; Llinás resonance; strong biological IIT) must explain away an anomaly that FMT predicts as natural.

**Cannot claim:**
- This is *not* unique evidence for FMT over other functionalist theories. GWT, HOT, attention schema, predictive processing, Metzinger's PSM, and Bach's MicroPsi are all consistent with it.
- It does not validate the specific 2×2 IWM/EWM/ISM/ESM typology or the Class-4 criticality requirement.
- It is a piece of *family* evidence (for substrate-/architecture-agnostic functionalism) on top of which FMT's specific differentiators must be argued separately.
