## Proposal predictions under test

- **P1** Felt necessity tracks consolidation, not validity.
- **P2** A consolidated invalid inference pattern feels compelling or obvious.
- **P3** An unconsolidated pattern feels like effortful step-by-step work. Compulsion arrives with practice.
- **P4** Insight arrives with a sense of rightness attached to the *arrival*, so it is also present for wrong insights.
- **M** Mechanism: the competence that produces the inference is absent from the scene *as producer*.

## (a) Prediction by prediction

**P1 — felt necessity tracks consolidation, not validity.**
- *Consistent:* Thompson et al. 2011 (FOR is predicted by fluency); Shynkaruk & Thompson 2006 (confidence–accuracy correlations near zero); Wang & Thompson 2019; Stewart et al. 2023; the truth-effect literature (Hasher et al. 1977 [secondary], Dechêne et al. 2010) as the propositional analogue.
- *Complicates:* Evans et al. 1983 found logic effects as large as belief effects. Acceptance tracks validity *and* something else, so "not validity" must be read as "not validity *as such*," i.e. validity matters only insofar as valid patterns are the ones that got consolidated. Morsanyi & Handley 2012 claimed a felt signal of validity, but Klauer & Singmann 2013 attribute it to content confounds, so P1 survives. Nobody has measured *consolidation* directly. Every study uses fluency or response time as a proxy, which matches the proposal's "consolidation" only by assumption.

**P2 — a consolidated invalid pattern feels compelling.**
- *Consistent:* Thompson, Evans & Campbell 2013 is the cleanest case: matching-bias answers are fast, high in FOR, and rarely rethought. Belief bias (Evans et al. 1983) shows that invalid-but-believable conclusions get accepted.
- *Complicates:* in both cases the driver is a heuristic or prior *belief in the conclusion*, not a practised invalid *inference rule*. No study found here trains people on an invalid rule (e.g. affirming the consequent) until it is fluent and then measures felt necessity. The chapter's "familiar experience of an invalid inference feeling obvious" is supported in spirit, not by a study of that exact form.

**P3 — unconsolidated = effortful; compulsion arrives with practice.**
- *Consistent in direction:* Boissin et al. 2021 (short training moves correct responding to the intuitive stage, lasting two months); Bago & De Neys 2017 (fast correct responses carry high confidence); Raoelison et al. 2020 and Thompson et al. 2018 (in capable reasoners the logical answer is the intuitive one).
- *Complicates:* (i) Raoelison & De Neys 2019: repetition alone did not consolidate the correct pattern, and where learning happened it was *abrupt*, not a gradual migration. (ii) Shynkaruk & Thompson 2006: confidence *rose* with deliberation time, i.e. when the steps were in the scene. That is the opposite of what a naive reading of P3 predicts, unless "confidence" and "felt necessity" are kept apart. (iii) No primary study found here measures felt *necessity* or *obviousness* (as distinct from confidence or FOR) as a function of practice, and none uses the chapter's example of proof by contradiction. The chapter's learner example is an illustration, not an empirical claim it can cite.

**P4 — rightness attaches to the arrival, so it is present for wrong insights.**
- *Consistent:* Laukkonen et al. 2020, which fits the exact wording (an Aha raises the truth ratings of unrelated false statements, d ≈ .63); Danek & Wiley 2017 and Grimmer et al. 2022 (false insights exist).
- *Complicates / counterpoint:* Salvi et al. 2016 (insight solutions are *more* accurate than analytic ones); Danek & Wiley 2017, Grimmer et al. 2022 and Danek et al. 2020 (Aha and certainty are *stronger* for correct solutions). The defensible statement is: "the sense of rightness is present for wrong insights too, and can be transferred to unrelated content; it is on average stronger for correct ones." The chapter's phrase "the known weakness of the experience" overstates the case and should be softened. Salvi et al. read the same offstage-completion structure as the reason insight is *reliable*.

**M — absent producer.** No study found tests it as distinct from fluency. Stewart et al. 2023 locates FOR in internal *generation* rather than execution, which is compatible with M but equally with fluency.

## (b) Does any study discriminate M from plain fluency?

**No, as far as found.** Every result above is predicted with the same sign by a processing-fluency account (Thompson et al. 2011; Reber & Unkelbach 2010; Topolinski & Reber 2010 for Aha) and by precision-weighting (Laukkonen et al. 2023). The two accounts make the same predictions because, in natural conditions, "the producer is absent from the scene" and "production was fluent" go together: consolidated competence is both fast and unrepresented. Reber & Unkelbach also supply the reliability story the chapter uses for its scope paragraph, without any appeal to self-model architecture. As the evidence stands, the proposal is a re-description of the fluency findings in FMT vocabulary. It adds an explanation of the "from no perspective" phenomenology (§105), which fluency accounts do not address, but that explanation is not yet empirically distinguished.

## (c) One discriminating test

**Design: "scaffolded-producer" manipulation with fluency held constant.**

1. Train participants to fluency on two inference forms, one valid (e.g. modus tollens) and one invalid (e.g. affirming the consequent, trained via a cover story with reinforcing feedback). Train to matched response-time criteria, so fluency is equated across forms and conditions.
2. Test trials in two conditions, **within subject, with identical premises, conclusion and display timing**:
   - *Producer-absent:* premises → conclusion, as trained.
   - *Producer-present:* the same, but before the conclusion the participant must **select the rule being applied** from a menu (e.g. "rule R7: from *if p then q* and *not q*, infer *not p*"). This places the competence in the scene *as a producer*. Response time is held constant with a fixed-duration selection screen, and checked with a yoked control in which participants select an irrelevant label of equal difficulty.
3. DVs: felt necessity ("could this conclusion have been otherwise, given the premises?", 1–7), "would hold for anyone" (perspective-freedom item, §105), FOR, and accuracy. Manipulation check: time from conclusion onset to rating, which must not differ between conditions.
4. **Predictions.** M predicts that felt necessity and perspective-freedom **drop** in the producer-present condition for both forms, even at equal fluency. Plain fluency predicts **no difference** (or a rise, since rule labelling adds a further fluent cue). The yoked-label control separates "producer in the scene" from "any extra step."
5. A cheaper variant without training: compare FOR and felt necessity for the *same* syllogisms with and without an attached, externally supplied derivation of equal reading time. This version is confounded, because a derivation also adds justification. Shynkaruk & Thompson 2006 already suggest that more processing *raises* confidence, which is a warning that this cheap version may come out against M for reasons unrelated to M.

**Has it been run?** Nothing of this form was found. The closest neighbours are Wang & Thompson 2019 (anchoring moves FOR without moving fluency, so FOR and fluency *can* be pulled apart) and Stewart et al. 2023 (FOR depends on generation, not execution). Neither places the producing rule in the scene. The finding that FOR is manipulable independently of fluency is useful: it shows the dissociation logic is workable in this paradigm.

## Could not verify / caveats

- **Hasher, Goldstein & Toppino 1977:** bibliographic details verified (Crossref). Abstract elided by the publisher, so the finding is stated from the standard secondary description.
- **Topolinski & Reber 2010:** bibliographic details verified. Abstract not retrieved; content stated from secondary knowledge.
- **Boissin et al. 2021:** whether confidence ratings were collected, and how they changed after training, is not in the abstract. Full text needed.
- **Franiatte, Boissin, Delmas & De Neys (2023), "Boosting debiasing: Impact of repeated training on reasoning," *Learning and Instruction* 89, 101845, doi:10.1016/j.learninstruc.2023.101845:** title and venue verified, abstract not available. Possibly relevant to P3 (repeated training); not assessed.
- **Thompson et al. 2011:** the abstract reports FOR→rethinking and fluency→FOR but not the FOR–*accuracy* relation. The often-quoted claim that "FOR is unrelated to accuracy" rests here on Shynkaruk & Thompson 2006 (confidence, not FOR) and on Thompson, Evans & Campbell 2013 (high FOR for wrong answers). The 2011 full text was not read.
- **Danek & Wiley:** the citation year is ambiguous. Published online January 2017, but Crossref lists *Front. Psychol.* volume 7 (2016) with article number 2077. Cite as 2017, vol. 7, 2077.
- **Primary evidence that the felt *obviousness* of a mathematical or logical step increases with practice:** not found. Searches turned up proof-aesthetics work (e.g. Inglis & Aberdein, *Philosophia Mathematica* 2014, "Beauty is not simplicity"), which was not assessed and is not about obviousness. P3's proof-by-contradiction example has no direct empirical citation.
- No study found trains an *invalid* inference rule to fluency and measures felt necessity (the direct test of P2).
