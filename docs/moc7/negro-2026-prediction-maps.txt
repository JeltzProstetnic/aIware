Abstract 
Scientific theories aim to explain and predict phenomena, yet assessing how experimental evidence 
impacts a theory remains a significant challenge. This is particularly evident in the psychological 
and cognitive sciences, where a lack of formal models and precise quantitative predictions often 
obscures the "centrality" of a given hypothesis for the theory that generates it. Because auxiliary 
hypotheses are often omitted or vaguely related to core ideas of a theory, researchers can easily 
safeguard core theo retical tenets against falsified predictions, rendering theory testing largely 
ineffective. To address this problem, we introduce prediction maps: a novel methodological 
approach that represents scientific theories as networks of beliefs structured in a core -periphery 
way. They visualize theoretical claims and empirical predictions as nodes, with  inferential 
relationships as edges. By applying graph -theory and network analysis metrics, we can quantify 
the centrality of specific predictions. This allows for a systematic evaluation of "evidential 
weight," identifying which experimental results would be  the most meaningful tests for a theory. 
We demonstrate the utility of this approach within the science of consciousness  – a field 
characterized by high theoretical fragmentation and underspecified models. We show how 
prediction maps can be used to design more informative experiments, compare competing 
frameworks, and assess empirical success. Finally, we present an open -access online platform 
designed to help res earchers across disciplines structure their theories. Prediction maps offer a 
transparent, structural analysis of the link between theory and evidence, providing a rigorous tool 
for navigating the complex landscape of theory testing. 
Keywords: Theory testing; prediction maps; centrality; scientific theories; consciousness

Introduction 
One of the central aims of scientific theories is to explain and predict true states of affairs 
(Douglas, 2009; Hofstadter, 1951; Salmon, 1978), with predictive accuracy often considered the 
hallmark of good scientific theories (Kuhn, 1981; McMullin, 1982). Consequently, theory testing 
is one of the central aspects of scientific practice. In the most common method for theory testing, 
termed “the hypothetico-deductive method” (Sprenger, 2011), predictions are extracted from 
theories, and tested experimentally in order to assess their strength. Yet, determining whether 
experimental evidence accords with a theory’s predictions, and to what extent, is not always 
straightforward (Crupi, 2021; Howson & Urbach, 1989; Sprenger, 2011). Indeed, although 
statistical methods allow testing whether the experimental data fit with a specific hypothesis, it is 
often unclear how the results reflect on the theory from which the hypothesis was derived. That 
is, refuting hypotheses that are central to a given theory should challenge the theory more than 
refuting less central predictions (Grim et al., 2022). The key problem is that it is not always clear 
which prediction is central and which is not, providing theory leaders substantial leeway in 
safeguarding their theory in the case of falsified hypotheses (Lakatos, 1976). 
This is particularly true in the psychological and cognitive sciences, where operationalization of 
theoretical constructs often requires many assumptions, and commonly does not involve formal 
models that enable precise quantitative predictions (Eronen & Bringmann, 2021; Guest & 
Martin, 2021; Meehl, 1967, 1978; Oberauer & Lewandowsky, 2019; Van Rooij & Blokpoel, 
2020). While progress has been made in improving methods for hypothesis testing (i.e., 
quantifying the extent to which experimental data fit a hypothesis, see Cumming, 2014; Morey et 
al., 2014; Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017), there is often insufficient clarity about the centrality of the 
hypothesis to the theory generating it. The lack of formal models to mediate theory and evidence 
makes it particularly difficult to assess the meaningfulness of a test for a given theory, 
contributing to the “theory crisis” in psychology (Oberauer & Lewandowsky, 2019; see also 
Eronen & Bringmann, 2021). To allow for theory assessment, empirical testing must be paired 
with an analysis that clarifies the impact of any piece of evidence on a theory. This requires 
elucidating the logical links between theoretical constructs and testable predictions.  
Here, we adopt a novel approach for doing so, thereby advancing theory testing: “prediction 
maps”, which are representations of scientific theories as networks of beliefs (Grim et al., 2022; 
=== PAGE 3 ===
3 
Henderson et al., 2010; Quine, 1951). The different claims and empirically testable predictions 
made by the theories are represented as nodes of the network, while the edges between the nodes 
represent the logical relation between the theory’s claims.  
This approach follows a Lakatosian framework (Lakatos, 1976), which considers scientific 
theories as networks of beliefs centered around core principles and various levels of peripheral 
auxiliary hypotheses. These auxiliary hypotheses render the core ideas empirically testable, 
while also shielding them from direct falsification: for every failed prediction, the theorist can 
reject the auxiliary hypothesis that made the empirical prediction possible, while holding on to 
the core idea on which the prediction was based. Prediction maps articulate these hypotheses, as 
well as their relation to the core. In this way, these maps can be used to navigate the complex 
relationship between core principles and observable consequences of a theory, offering a novel 
methodology for systematically assessing the relevance of experimental data for a general theory 
or framework.    
Our approach assumes that (dis)confirmatory relationships between theoretical constructs and 
empirical evidence can be represented as logical relationships between statements (for criticism, 
see Bogen & Woodward, 2005; for a reply, see Votsis, 2011); though we do not require that 
these relationships be deductive (our framework is thus compatible both with the standard 
hypothetico-deductive method and the inductive methodology of Bayesian confirmation theory). 
In virtue of these logical connections, falsifying an empirical statement jeopardizes the integrity 
of the network (Feigl, 1970; Grim et al., 2022; Henderson et al., 2010; for general discussion on 
the distinction between theoretical and observation statements, see Boyd & Bogen, 2026). This 
implies that, depending on the structure of the theory and the interconnectivity among its claims, 
refuting specific hypotheses amounts to damaging certain “areas” of the theory more than others: 
the more central and interconnected a prediction is, the more difficult it should be to amend the 
overall framework in light of disconfirmatory evidence. 
Visualizing scientific theories as prediction maps centered around some core principles allows 
one to identify more central claims and predictions that are particularly influential to the theory. 
The centrality, or importance, of each claim can be calculated using graph-theory and network 
analysis metrics (Borgatti & Everett, 2006) and used as a proxy for evaluating the evidential 
weight that a given piece of evidence has over a theory. This, in turn, provides a more informed 
=== PAGE 4 ===
4 
framework for assessing the meaning of existing data, as well as novel experimental designs, for 
evaluating the theory. For example, researchers can use prediction maps to decide which claim is 
better to target when designing an experiment (e.g., by determining which prediction would be 
more “disruptive” to the theory), to compare similarities and dissimilarities between theories, and 
to assess their empirical success.    
In this paper, we demonstrate how to construct prediction maps for scientific theories, using the 
science of consciousness as a case in point. We further provide an online platform (accessible 
here: https://xk29y8.csb.app/) that allows researchers of all disciplines to create new maps for 
theories in their field, and show – using this case study – how this could be a major tool for 
advancing theory testing and understanding. The study of consciousness is especially suited for 
this purpose, as it is populated by many different theories, all trying to account for how the brain 
relates to conscious experience (Seth & Bayne, 2022). These theories are quite heterogeneous, 
indicating that consciousness science has not yet found a dominant paradigm (Kuhn, 1970). This 
theoretical fragmentation is partly due to the fact that most theories are still underspecified 
frameworks, rather than rigorous and formalized models (Mudrik et al., 2025; Signorelli et al., 
2021). Moreover, different theories often make incompatible assumptions about the nature of 
consciousness (Mudrik et al., 2025; Seth & Bayne, 2022). As a result, they develop by following 
different research questions, with some methods being more prone to confirm specific 
hypotheses (Yaron et al., 2022). When the relationship between theory and evidence is not 
constrained by strict logical relations and quantitative predictions, theory evaluation is 
particularly challenging (Chis-Ciure et al., 2024; Fazekas et al., 2024; Kirkeby-Hinrup, 2024; 
Negro, 2024). This renders prediction maps especially useful, as they pair empirical evidence 
with a conceptual and structural analysis of the theory and the informative value of its 
predictions.     
Notably, prediction maps are, in essence, models of models: they are idealized, abstracted, and 
simplified representations of scientific theories, much like scientific models are idealized 
representations of a target phenomenon (Bokulich, 2011; Chirimuuta, 2024). This implies that 
the particular way a theory is represented as a prediction map will partially depend on the 
choices and epistemic aims of the modeler (Giere, 2004; Massimi, 2018). Building the maps 
involves some interpretation, which inevitably entails subjective judgement. Here, we tried to 
=== PAGE 5 ===
5 
minimize such judgement by aiming for inter-modeler agreement and by conferring with theory 
proponents to make sure the maps accurately represent their theories.  
Methods 
Selection of theories 
As a starting point and a proof of concept for this approach, we selected four prominent 
neuroscientific theories of consciousness: the Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (GNWT), 
Integrated Information Theory (IIT), Perceptual Reality Monitoring (PRM), and Recurrent 
Processing Theory (RPT). Though there are many other theories that aim at explaining 
consciousness, these four are often referred to as highly influential (Cleeremans et al., 2025; Del 
Pin et al., 2021; Doerig et al., 2021; Lau & Rosenthal, 2011; Mudrik et al., 2025; Northoff & 
Lamme, 2020; Seth & Bayne, 2022; Storm et al., 2024; Yaron et al., 2022), with relatively 
extensive literature available on each. An exception is PRM, which is a fairly young theory that 
has been systematically formulated only in recent years (Lau, 2022; Michel, 2023); however, it 
belongs to a family of theories called “higher-order theories” (HOT; see Brown et al., 2019 for a 
review) that have emerged as empirical paradigms in the past couple of decades, whose 
philosophical lineage traces back to Locke and Kant (Carruthers & Gennaro, 2001). Here, we 
selected PRM as the prime example of HOT because it currently seems to be the most 
comprehensive empirical version of HOT.  
Following the creation of these maps, we worked with proponents of additional theories (Brown, 
2015; Cleeremans et al., 2020; Fleming, 2020; Pennartz, 2022; Whyte et al., 2026), all tested 
under adversarial collaboration initiatives (Potgieter, 2024), to create additional maps. As these 
were not established as part of this work, we include them in the supplementary materials 
(Supplementary S3), for interested readers.

2019; for RPT: Lamme, 2006, 2010, 2020). From this literature, we derived further papers about

Extraction of claims and structuring of the maps 
We extracted critical theoretical claims and empirical predictions, ensuring that the statements 
constituting each prediction map adhered as much as possible to the formulation found in the 
original publications. After the initial extraction, an iterative process of revisions and discussions 
began, where the claims were placed on the maps, and additional theoretical and experimental 
papers were added, until a consensus was reached. This process was also aimed at minimizing 
subjectivity in designing the maps. 
We structured each map around a core claim, representing the principal idea of the theory for 
explaining consciousness. From this core claim stem five different levels of auxiliary hypotheses, 
=== PAGE 7 ===
7 
with each level further specifying the information present in the previous level (Henderson et al., 
2010). Thus, the first level after the core (L1) unpacks the concepts in the core, the second level 
(L2) develops the concepts in L1, and so on (Figure 1). The maps further distinguish between 
four classes of claims: cognitive (i.e., claims about cognitive/psychological functions; colored in 
cyan), substrate-general (i.e., claims about a generally defined “physical substrate”, without 
assuming that it must be a brain; green), brain-specific (i.e., claims about the brain and its 
properties; pink), and extrapolation (conjectures about the distribution of consciousness in 
domains beyond that of healthy adult humans; see Baetu, 2024; Bayne et al., 2024; Negro & 
Mudrik, 2025; yellow).  
The classification of each level on the map depends on the nature of the theory. For theories 
centered mostly around cognitive/psychological claims (GNWT, RPT, and PRM), the third level 
(L3) captures cognitive predictions. In this case, L4 focuses on theoretical claims about the 
neural implementation of the cognitive claims (L3), while the fifth and outermost level is 
devoted to experimentally testable neuroscientific predictions. On the other hand, if the central 
regions of the map are dominated by substrate-general claims (IIT), the third level is composed 
of brain-specific claims, the fourth of neuroscientific predictions, and the fifth of cognitive 
predictions.

We represented the logical relations between core, auxiliary claims, and predictions through a 
generic “in virtue of” relation, conveying the logical (but not necessarily deductive) dependence 
of a claim on other claims. This relation can occur both between claims at two different levels 
(represented by a solid line arrow) or between claims belonging to the same level (represented by 
a dashed line arrow).  
To minimize the subjective aspect of map construction and to make sure our reconstruction was 
not biased or mistaken, we contacted the theory-proponents and asked them to provide feedback 
on the maps. The final versions of the prediction maps, reported here, are the result of several 
rounds of revisions and informative exchanges with theory-proponents.  
Centrality measures 
To quantitatively evaluate the structural role of each claim within the prediction maps, we 
applied graph-theoretical centrality measures within a network analysis framework (Borgatti & 

=== PAGE 9 ===
9 
Everett, 2006; Freeman, 1978). Centrality is one of the main indicators of influential nodes based 
on the topological properties that drive network dynamics (Bloch et al., 2023; Ghalmane et al., 
2019; Gurfinkel & Rikvold, 2021). Such analysis assigns a score to each claim based on different 
aspects of its structural position and potential influence in the network (Rodrigues, 2019).  
In the context of prediction maps, centrality measures allow us to address the following 
questions (Borgatti & Everett, 2006): How widely does a given claim influence other parts of the 
theory? How quickly can its influence propagate? Does it serve as a critical connector between 
separate parts of the map? Is it supported by highly influential claims? Correspondingly, we 
computed four complementary centrality measures, which are applied at the level of single 
claims in the prediction maps (as opposed to the whole network level): Local Reaching 
Centrality and Harmonic Centrality, which assess the breadth and immediacy of a claim’s 
downstream influence; Betweenness Centrality, which highlights its function as a connector 
between theoretical components; and PageRank, providing an estimate of the importance of each 
node based on the quality of its upstream support. Below we define and explain each measure 
(Figure 2). 
Local Reaching Centrality (LRC) and Harmonic Centrality (HC) 
LRC captures the reachability of a given node by calculating the total number of nodes it can 
reach (Mones et al., 2012, Equation 1; figure 3a).

This measure highlights nodes that can reach a large portion of the map, thereby identifying 
claims that serve as starting points for broad logical implications. Because of its intuitive 
interpretation, LRC is particularly useful for comparing how far different claims extend within 
the structure of the theory (Mones et al., 2012). Yet, it only offers a partial view: it does not 
reflect how far away those nodes lie and how efficiently they connect to the rest of the network.

HC goes beyond LRC by taking into account how far away the nodes are from each other: 
instead of treating all reachable nodes equally, HC weights each node by the inverse of its 
shortest distance. Specifically, it sums the reciprocals of the shortest path lengths from each node

in fewer steps contribute more strongly (e.g., 1/1 for a direct neighbor) than those reached via 
longer paths (e.g., 1/3 if three steps away) (Rochat, 2009). To account for network size, this sum 
is normalized by the maximum possible number of nodes that could be reached.

In the context of prediction maps, HC highlights claims whose influence can propagate quickly 
through the network. Unlike LRC, which simply counts how many nodes a claim can reach, HC 
also considers how far away those nodes are, giving higher weight to claims that reach others in 
fewer steps. It captures both the breadth of influence and the efficiency of reach, making it a 
valuable complement to LRC. Accordingly, we report them together here. 
Betweenness Centrality (BC) 
BC measures the importance of a node in connecting other nodes in the network. Namely, it 
indicates to what extent a certain claim serves as a “logical bridge”. It quantifies how often a 
node is present on the shortest paths between other pairs of nodes (Freeman, 1977, Equation 3; 
figure 3c). Therefore, BC captures the role of a node as a crucial link in the transmission of 
information.

When applied to prediction maps, BC indicates which claims serve as crucial logical 
interconnectors between different parts of the theory. Thus, providing empirical evidence against 
nodes with high BC amounts to disabling crucial logical connections in the theory’s structure.

results. PR recursively evaluates each node based on the number and importance of nodes 
linking to it, reflecting the idea that a claim is important if it is supported by other influential 
claims, thus capturing a form of authority within the theoretical structure rather than sheer 
connectivity (Page et al., 1999, Equation 4; figure 3d). PR is formalized in the following way:

PR first assigns equal weights to all nodes and then repeatedly updates these weights using the

In prediction maps, PR highlights claims that are repeatedly justified by other well-supported 
claims, thereby identifying downstream nodes that play a central role in supporting the overall 
theoretical framework. This is important because challenging predictions with high PR 
reverberates directly to other highly influential upstream claims.

Prediction Maps 
In this section, we present the prediction maps for the four theories, and analyze the centrality of 
each of their claims (for the full centrality analysis, see Supplementary S1). For convenience, 
claims are labelled alphanumerically, based on the level they belong to (i.e., A for the core, B for 
claims in L1, etc.), while the numbering progresses clockwise from the top-left claim in each 
level. Thus, the labels should not be mistaken to convey logical connections, which are 
represented by the arrows (so, for example, C4 can lead to C3, in which case an arrow will go 
from the former to the latter). Each claim is supported by a numbered reference, according to the 
reference lists reported in Table 1. In addition, though we attempted to expose auxiliary 
assumptions, we focused on those relevant to the theory, and did not attempt to exhaust all 
possible assumptions. For example, transitioning between cognitive or substrate-general claims 
and neural-specific claims requires the hidden assumption that the human brain is the most likely 
organ to sustain the relevant cognitive function (but see Rouleau & Levin, 2023). This 
assumption is based on decades of neuroscientific research that cannot be represented in 
prediction maps, and is accordingly left out.

The black rectangle at the center of the map (Figure 3), denoting the core claim of GNWT (A1), 
highlights its functionalist standpoint by suggesting that the function of consciousness is making 
information available by broadcasting it into a global workspace (GW) that breaks modularity. 
Consequently, most of the theory’s claims in the layers closer to the core (L1 and L2) are 
cognitive, specifying the processes through which the GW operates: B1 states that the GW 
functions as an information bottleneck; B2 claims that the GW is needed to integrate between 
different modules; B3 states that the GW enables maintenance of percepts over time; B4 
specifies the role of attentional processes – they mobilize information and broadcast it into the 
GW.

Two of the claims in L1 (B2 and B3) lead to many other cognitive claims down the line, 
suggesting that they are important “hubs” for the theory. The B2 cluster includes claims about 
unconscious processes and how they are affected by the GW. Within this cluster, claims C4 
(“Information outside GW is processed unconsciously by separate modules in parallel”) and C3 
(“GW can influence unconscious processes, which are not all fully automatic”), lead to two  
cognitive predictions (D4, D5). These two predictions capture important aspects of how the 
theory considers unconscious processes: D4 asserts that subliminal effects, contrary to purely 
automatic processes, require attention; D5 stresses that if stimuli involving resonant loops within 
medium range connections are unattended, they remain preconscious. 
The cluster stemming from B3, instead, focuses on the maintenance of information over time. 
The fecundity of this cluster is evidenced by the number and types of claims B3 generates. 
Indeed, B3 leads to cognitive claims C5 (“UC responses to stimuli are short-lived”), C6 (“Items 
in GW are items in Working Memory”), and C7 (“Conscious processes can be long-lived”), as 
well as to the neural implementation claim E3 (“Long-range and feedback connections are 
needed for consciousness because they maintain information in the GW by amplifying the 
signal”). Among these claims, the most consequential one is C6, which brings about the neural 
implementation claim E6 and three cognitive predictions (D7, D8, D9) about specific instances 
where certain capacities (trace conditioning, detection of global violations over time and flexible 
mental chaining) can only take place if the information is consciously maintained. 
LRC and HC analysis 
The centrality analysis shows that not all claims connected to the core have a similar importance, 
as within L1, there is high variability between the different claims. B4, which describes the role 
of attention in the theory, connects the core to more downstream claims than any other claim in 
the map, despite not belonging to a thematically unified cluster (B4LRC= 0.465; B4HC= 0.260). In 
contrast, claim B1 (concerning GW operating as an information bottleneck) is not connected to 
many nodes in the map (B1LRC= 0.116; B1HC= 0.066), and its HC score is lower than that of the 
ensuing C10, which specifies how the bottleneck actually operates (C10LRC= 0.093; C10HC= 
0.081). This suggests that B1’s impact on the overall theory is mediated by C10. Together, the 
LRC and HC measures indicate that for GNWT, claims about attention, information maintenance

and integration reach more claims than the claim that the GW acts as a bottleneck, despite the 
prominence of this claim in discussions surrounding GNWT (Dehaene et al., 2003; Dehaene & 
Changeux, 2005).  
In L2, four more claims (C2, C8, C6, C9) can be identified as having relatively high levels of 
LRC and HC (proportional to the general levels within the map). Out of these four, claim C2 has 
the highest reach within its level (C2LRC= 0.186; C2HC= 0.130), suggesting that the claim that 
information in modules outside the GW is encapsulated is highly consequential. Moreover, E2 
has LRC and HC levels (E2LRC= 0.116; E2HC= 0.081) that resemble those of claim B1, despite 
B1 being close to the core and E2 being a brain-specific claim (L4). This highlights the centrality 
of this claim, concerning long-axon pyramidal neurons allowing global broadcasting of 
information. It also exemplifies that consequential claims are not constrained to the levels that 
are closest to the theory’s core: implementation claims can be highly influential if they have 
multiple empirical consequences.   
BC analysis 
The BC analysis indicates that in GNWT, the claims receiving a high BC value are mostly the 
ones highlighted above (B2-B4, C10, C2 and also C8 and E1), with B4 reaching the highest 
value (0.008). This shows that in GNWT, claims that have far-reaching consequences also act as 
connectors between different areas of the map. Claim E1, which describes the frontoparietal 
network as a likely implementation site of the GW, is particularly interesting: because of its high 
BC score (0.007), it can be seen as the crucial connector between key theoretical cognitive 
concepts of GNWT and its neural predictions. 
PR analysis 
Finally, the PR analysis shows that the highest level of PR (0.084) is found for neural prediction 
F3, which concerns unconscious information eliciting local short-lived neural responses. This 
claim is the only one derived from three other important claims (D4-D6). Accordingly, falsifying 
this prediction would have a strong impact on the map. Other predictions that score high in this 
metric are the neural predictions F4 (PR =0.054) and F5 (PR =0.041). Falsifying either one of

The transition from the principles of IIT, specified in CORE, L1, and L2, to neural terms requires

The map also reveals groups of related claims within IIT. For example, a cluster of claims targets

neurophysiological edge cases (E15) and clinical settings (E14, D6).  
A different cluster, emerging from claim C2, concerns the relationship between the type of 
experience and the architecture of the corresponding substrate. This cluster characterizes the 
types of contents in a conscious experience, thus targeting the question of what an experience 
feels like (e.g., its temporal structure (D2), its duration (E3), its spatial character (D3), etc.).

At the core of various Higher-Order-Thought theories is the idea that first-order states are not 
conscious unless they are referred to in a specific manner by a higher-order state (Brown et al., 
2019). The specific relationship between the first- and higher-order states that constitutes 
conscious awareness is different for each version of HOT. According to PRM, on which we 
focus here (for other HOTs, see supplementary S3), the higher-order state functions as “reality 
monitoring”, and it indexes first-order states according to whether they reliably reflect the world 
(Figure 5). Thus, the core claim of PRM, A1, is that a first-order state is conscious if it is 
indexed, via a pointer, by a higher-order state, as reliably reflecting the world as it is now. The 
next level, L1, comprises five cognitive claims (B1-B5) elaborating on the core and specifying 
the functional relationship between first-order and higher-order states.   
L1 serves as a gateway for addressing key questions about consciousness (e.g., phenomenal vs 
access consciousness, see Block, 1995; the richness of conscious experience, see Kouider et al., 
2010; the function of consciousness, see Shepherd & Bayne, 2026), that are then fleshed out by 
claims in L2. 
The map reveals two major clusters of claims. The first stems from the cognitive claims B1 and 
B2, and is mostly concerned with the neural implementation of the theory in the human brain. 
This cluster has multiple connections to other paths in the map. For example, one of the 
neuroscientific predictions belonging to this cluster is F8 (“Impairing the functioning of the 
dorsolateral prefrontal cortex should change subjective report about awareness without affecting 
task performance”), which is also connected to claims B5 (“First-order states represent the 
world, higher-order states track the reliability of first-order states”) and D10 (“Given the right 
paradigm, discrimination performance should be the same with and without conscious 
perception”), which do not belong to the same thematic cluster. Another example is C2 (“Inner 
awareness must discriminate between neural activity due to external stimulus and noise”), which 
branches out to three claims concerning signal-noise discrimination. 
The second cluster, stemming from B4 (“Higher-order states can incorrectly index the reliability 
of first-order states”), focuses on richness and inflation. It is mediated by the claim (C6) that 
indexing the reliability of first order states incorrectly leads to inflation, and includes the claims

Discussion 
This work introduces prediction maps as means to expose the logical relationship between core 
claims of scientific theories and their empirical predictions. These maps visualize the 
informativeness of different claims of the theory (e.g., which claims yield more downstream 
claims, whether each claim is supported by a single or multiple “parent” claims, etc.). Thus, 
experimentalists can use prediction maps to design direct tests of a theory, theorists might 
employ them to revise their theories in light of contradictory evidence, and scholars in general 
can use them to compare and assess the structure of competing scientific theories. 
A key advantage afforded by the maps is the ability to quantify the centrality of each claim using 
graph-theory measures (Borgatti & Everett, 2006), thereby understanding its importance for 
theory evaluation. Notably, these measures go beyond assessing the closeness of a given claim to 
the core and instead quantify its connectivity profile. Given the different meanings of each 
centrality measure, researchers should focus on the measure of interest based on their 
experimental and theoretical goals: LRC and HC score how far-reaching each claim is (for HC, 
this is weighted by the “distance” between claims), BC measures the extent to which a claim 
works as a crucial connector, while PR indicates which nodes are the most important given the 
importance of their parents.  
Centrality measures are informative insofar as the network to which they are applied accurately 
represents the corresponding theory. In this sense, they are sensitive to subjective modeler 
decisions (Parker & Winsberg, 2018), and their interpretation depends on how a theory is 
represented in the first place. Accordingly, centrality-based inferences should be taken with a 
grain of salt. In our case, we tried to minimize subjectivity by having the maps constructed by 
multiple modelers, and by discussing the maps with theory leaders. We suggest that future 
applications of our approach take similar steps to mitigate this concern.  
We hold that applying prediction maps to scientific theories can advance theory evaluation 
across many disciplines and fields. It might be especially suited for disciplines like psychology 
and neuroscience, given the qualitative nature of central theoretical constructs and the lack of 
=== PAGE 28 ===
28 
formal models regulating the relationship between these constructs (Meehl, 1967; Oberauer & 
Lewandowsky, 2019). Specifically, here we focused on theories of consciousness, which are still 
“works in progress” (Mudrik et al., 2025), underspecified and empirically under-constrained 
(Cleeremans et al., 2025; Fazekas et al., 2024; Kirkeby-Hinrup et al., 2025; Negro et al., 2025). 
Below, we accordingly use the insights derived from this process to demonstrate how prediction 
maps can accelerate scientific progress, by advancing theory understanding and evaluation. 
First, visual inspection of the four maps we generated reveals the deep theoretical and conceptual 
divergence between the theories: GNWT and PRM have a functional/cognitive core, surrounded 
by purely cognitive claims. In contrast, RPT’s core idea is informed by both cognitive and neural 
constructs, and accordingly four out of the five claims in L1 are hybrid claims, combining 
cognitive and neural constructs. Finally, IIT stands out, since its core is based on its 
phenomenological axioms and the explanatory identity posited between properties of 
phenomenology and the properties of a cause-effect structure. Thus, its L1 claims are substrate-
general.  
Second, the maps also allow us to more readily detect convergence points between the theories, 
while also assessing the differential value of such points for each theory. For example, both RPT 
and GNWT posit that NMDA receptors play a role in generating consciousness (for RPT, see E1 
and F1; for GNWT, see F6). With centrality measures, we can now assess the relative 
importance of such convergence points for each theory: for this example, RPT’s E1 (“NMDA 
receptors, which enable cortico-cortical feedback and synaptic plasticity, are needed for C”) 
scores relatively high in both LRC (0.241; 90th percentile: 0.248; median: 0.017) and HC (0.149; 
90th percentile: 0.193; median: 0.017). On the contrary, GNWT’s F6 (“AMPA receptors 
modulate feedforward connections while NMDA receptors modulate feedback connectivity, 
making them necessary for C”) has a medium value across all centrality measures (LRC =0.023; 
HC =0.023; BC =0.001; PR =0.019; all values are around the median for each measure - see 
Table 17 in Supplementary S1). This demonstrates that although both GNWT and RPT agree 
that NMDA receptors are necessary for consciousness, this claim plays a much bigger role in 
RPT than in GNWT.  
Third, despite being merely descriptive (rather than evaluative), prediction maps can be used for 
further evaluation, and specifically for detecting weak links in the current formulations of the 
=== PAGE 29 ===
29 
theories. For example, for GNWT it is unclear why the construct of ignition leads to the 
prediction that the disappearance of a stimulus should lead to an ignition-like response, creating 
a gap between E6 and F9. For IIT, there seems to be a wide gap between B2 and C7: additional 
claims are needed to more explicitly justify why similarity between Phi-structures and 
experiences should derive from the fact that changes in the state of the PSC determine changes in 
experience. For PRM, it is unclear why decodability of inflated perception should be high in later 
stages of the visual hierarchy, rendering the transition between D8 and F7 obscure. Finally, for 
RPT, there seems to be a weak link between E1 and F1, since it is not specified what counts as 
“large-scale” recurrent processing - how “large” is large enough?  
Moreover, the centrality analysis (see Supplementary S1), and especially the PR scores, provides 
strong bases for evaluating existing attempts to test theories. In the case of theories of 
consciousness, several adversarial collaborations have been trying to evaluate such theories 
(Corcoran et al., 2025; Fleming et al., 2025; Melloni et al., 2021; Olcese et al., 2024; Potgieter, 
2024). The first published work emanating from these efforts (Cogitate et al., 2025), testing 
predictions of IIT and GNWT, has been criticized for not targeting predictions that are equally 
informative to the tested theories (IIT-Concerned et al., 2025). Specifically, it was argued that 
“the tests are problematic and not meaningful because they do not address the core claims of IIT” 
(IIT-Concerned et al., 2025, p. 689). However, our analysis shows that IIT’s E10, which 
corresponds to the third prediction tested in the Cogitate experiment, actually reaches the highest 
PR score in the IIT map (0.049), suggesting it is the most important prediction to target if one 
wants to meaningfully test IIT (interestingly, it was not confirmed by the data). The other two 
IIT predictions tested by the Cogitate experiment are E9, which has a medium-high PR value 
(0.031; 90th percentile: 0.037; median: 0.022), and E1, with a medium PR value (0.020). In 
comparison, the GNWT predictions tested by the Cogitate experiment seem to be less central to 
the theory. F9, the second prediction in the first Cogitate experiment, reaches a medium-high PR 
value (0.030; 90th percentile: 0.033; median: 0.018), while the first (F1) and third (F11) have a 
medium PR value (F1PR= 0.020; F11PR= 0.023). This suggests that if anything, the predictions 
tested by Cogitate in the first experiment were somewhat more central to IIT than to GNWT. 
This seems to be the case also for Experiment 2 (see Mudrik et al., 2024), which tested the same 
predictions for GNWT (F1, F9 and F11), and an additional prediction for IIT, E11 (“Similarity 
metrics should be higher for content-specific activation than task-specific activation in some 
=== PAGE 30 ===
30 
posterior areas”), achieving a medium-high PR score of 0.028. Irrespective of the debate on the 
Cogitate results, this example showcases that prediction maps and centrality analyses can inform 
debates surrounding theory testing, paving the way towards more systematic conceptual and 
quantifiable assessments (See Supplementary S2 for a similar analysis based on predictions 
tested by other adversarial collaborations).  
On the other hand, the informativeness of theoretical predictions for theory evaluation is not only 
defined by centrality; it is also influenced by other properties (Syrjänen, 2024) like novelty 
(Douglas & Magnus, 2013; Worrall, 1989), boldness (Mayo, 1991; Popper, 1959), and precision 
(Hitchcock & Sober, 2004; but see Elliott-Graves, 2020). Taking a wider perspective, theory 
evaluation is not confined to the predictive power of a theory, and involves other theoretical 
virtues like parsimony and coherence with background knowledge (Keas, 2018; Longino, 1979). 
And at an even higher level, scientific progress can be attained pre-theoretically, even without 
theory assessment and theory comparison (Chang, 2004). Thus, the current approach, focused on 
the centrality of predictions and their relevance for scientific theories, is only one way to achieve 
scientific progress when facing unresolved questions and unexplained phenomena (Figure 7).  
 
Figure 7.  A possible path towards scientific progress. Progress is partly driven by theory assessment, which 
requires establishing, among other things (e.g., explanatory power, parsimony, coherence with background 
knowledge, etc.), the predictive power of scientific theories, which is a multidimensional concept comprising the 
importance of the centrality, novelty, boldness, and precision of predictions for theory testing. Here, we focus on the 
centrality dimension.

Finally, we acknowledge the limitations of the prediction maps approach, and suggest future 
directions that can compensate for those limitations. First, they are tuned to challenging theories 
rather than confirming them (as the entire approach is inspired by Lakatosian falsificationism; 
see Lakatos, 1976). Bayesian confirmation theory can be used to complement our approach in 
that respect (Grim et al., 2022; Henderson et al., 2010; Corcoran et al., 2023; Kirkeby-Hinrup, 
2024). Second, the maps represent one point in time. Instead, theories can be represented 
diachronically, with different prediction maps representing the different versions of the theory at 
different time points (e.g., IIT has been updated several times: Tononi, 2004, 2012; Oizumi et al., 
2014; Albantakis et al., 2023). This could enable a Lakatos-inspired assessment of whether the 
research program has progressed over time (Negro, 2024). Third, our prediction maps do not 
differentiate between prediction and accommodation; while the former is formulated without 
considering the results, the latter is designed to fit the already obtained data (Hitchcock & Sober, 
2004). In our maps, we call “prediction” any empirical statement logically derived from 
upstream claims. Future maps might want to include the distinction between predictions and 
accommodations. Fourth, these maps do not include all auxiliary assumptions that further specify 
the relationship between a given hypothesis and the claims from which it is derived. Fifth, our 
graph-theoretic analysis focused exclusively on within-network measures (i.e., by asking how 
influential a claim is for this network). Whole-network measures could be also exploited to 
compare network structures, thus enabling deeper theory-comparison. Taken together, these 
future directions highlight how our approach, applied here to theories of consciousness, can be 
extended to exploring other questions and theoretical dimensions. We hope prediction maps will 
thus become a key tool for theory testing and evaluation across different domains and 
disciplines.

Supplementary S2: Centrality analysis for predictions tested by INTREPID and ETHOS 
INTREPID and ETHOS are two consortia testing theories of consciousness via adversarial 
collaborations. INTREPID tests predictions of IIT and various theories inspired by predictive 
processing accounts (Olcese et al., 2024; more information in supplementary S3), while ETHOS 
tests adversarial hypotheses of different higher-order theories of consciousness (Fleming et al., 
2025), including PRM. Here, we analyze the centrality of IIT’s predictions tested by INTREPID 
and PRM’s predictions tested by ETHOS.  
IIT’s tested predictions are E2 (‘Local strengthening or weakening of horizontal connections in 
topographic areas should lead to a local distortion of experienced visual space), E6 
(‘Deactivating already inactive neurons of retinotopic cortex should remove part of spatial 
experience’), and F2 (‘Because mental action is not essential to consciousness, in a motion 
induced blindness task actively looking for a stimulus should not facilitate conscious access’). 
E2 and E6 reach a high PR value (E2PR=0.045; E6PR= 0.038), while F2 has a medium value 
(0.023). This indicates that E2 and E6 are meaningful tests for IIT, whereas F2 is much less 
informative for the theory.  
Instead, the ETHOS experiments seem less significant for PRM: here, the relevant predictions 
are D11 (‘Adaptation should affect reality monitoring system, leading to shifts in awareness 
without changes in discrimination performance’), F4 (‘Cross-decoding of neural populations 
responsible for perceptual and imagery vividness should be weak or absent’), and D9 
(‘Phenomenological control should be able to alter reliability of FOS, but not their content’). 
D11 reaches a medium-high PR value (0.036), while F4 and D9 achieve only medium PR scores 
(F4PR=0.022; D9PR=0.020).
