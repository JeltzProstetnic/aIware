Welcome!
It is a great pleasure to welcome you all to the seventh instalment of 
the Models of Consciousness  conference series (MoC7). Following the con ­
ferences at Oxford University (2019, MoC1), online (2021, MoC2), Stanford 
University (2022, MoC3), again Oxford University  (2023, MoC4), Bamberg 
University (2024, MoC5), and Hokkaido University (2025, MoC6), MoC7 offers 
an exciting opportunity to make new research connections and enjoy the 
hygge (cosy) Danish culture in one of the world’s most liveable cities, 
Copenhagen.1
Hygge also means slowing down, reflecting and bringing people together by creating a relaxed, 
pressure-free space for shared presence and meaningful connection. Since consciousness sci -
ence is still developing its methodological and conceptual foundations, reflecting on them in an 
amicable environment is crucial to make meaningful progress. A true hygge atmosphere relies on 
equality, where no single person dominates the conversation, and everyone feels equally valued.
This year, we hope to create such an atmosphere of mutual inspiration and constructive dialogue. 
In this spirit, we are introducing new social activities into the programme and turning the 
discussion sessions into transdisciplinary collaborative work to clarify concepts, reach common 
methodological standards, and set future priorities in consciousness science  — possibly 
resulting in the first collective paper of this magnitude in the field (but not necessarily).
This year, the conference is dedicated to bringing together researchers on the following core 
themes:
 Philosophical foundations in the scientific study of consciousness
 Methodologies, models, and measures of consciousness
 AI, LLMs, and consciousness science
 Phenomenology and applied phenomenology
We hope to create an open and inspiring atmosphere that enables us all to make further progress 
on these and other questions together. Thanks for contributing your talks, posters and discussions 
— and enjoy the Danish hygge!
Welcome to Copenhagen! — The organisers

Core Themes
We are delighted to announce that Models of Con ­
sciousness 7 comprises the following core themes. 
However, please note that contributed talks are open 
to any topic in the field, including, in particular, the 
neuroscience of consciousness and mathematical ap ­
proaches.
Philosophical foundations in the scientific 
study of consciousness
Within this theme, we explore the philosophical foundations of the 
scientific study of consciousness. Different viewpoints on how to under-
stand reality often influence the development of models of conscious-
ness. By exploring foundations, we can better understand the differing 
approaches and identify key questions to refine model selection. We 
invite philosophers of mind, neuroscience, AI, science, epistemology, 
and aesthetics, among other relevant areas.
=== PAGE 7 ===

Methodologies, models, and measures of 
consciousness
Under this theme, we invite presentations on methodologies, models, 
and measures of consciousness. Special attention is given to the 
neuroscience of consciousness and how to characterise states of con-
sciousness, developmental consciousness, animal consciousness and 
synthetic consciousness. Research of this kind is at the heart of the field, 
generating scientifically credible and useful models and measures.
AI, LLMs, and consciousness science
Under this theme, we invite investigations in consciousness science 
and qualitative research, leveraging new methodologies inspired by 
AI and LLMs. Physiological (e.g. machine learning for neural decoding) 
and qualitative applications (e.g. topic modelling analyses) are wel -
come. We also invite contributions on the latest research and insights 
into artificial consciousness.
Phenomenology and applied phenomenology
Within this theme, we invite phenomenologists and phenomenological 
investigations, including philosophical and applied areas such as phe -
nomenological psychology, neurophenomenology, micro-phenome -
nology, computational phenomenology, and mathematical phenome-
nology, among others. We welcome rigorous studies of subjective first-
person lived experiences and structural aspects. This research informs 
models of consciousness and, ultimately, their degree of adequacy.

Pre­Conference Activities
Sunday, 11 October
As is tradition at MoC, the day before the conference is devoted to informal pre-conference 
activities designed to get to know each other. On top of the two Sunday-afternoon activities below, 
we highly recommend spending Sunday morning visiting the free town of Christiania and taking 
a free walking tour of the historical centre.
Please note that these activities are not included in the conference fee and are paid separately.
Option 1 — Comedy Show and Drinks
We have organised a comedy show to learn about Danish culture 
and have fun in the process. The five-star Copenhagen Show is 
hosted in the underground cinema room at Next House Copen -
hagen, Bernstorffsgade 27, 1577 København (by Copenhagen 
Central Station). After each show, we invite participants to stay 
and share a drink (drinks are not included in the show ticket).
Three sessions of the same show are available: 15:00, 16:30 and 
18:00. Each session can host up to 50 people. The shows are 
booked on a first-come, first-served basis  — book your session 
via the QR code: in the calendar, go to 11 October and select your 
preferred time.
If you arrive on Saturday, consider booking the earliest 
session, so that people who arrive on Sunday afternoon 
can book the latest one.

Transdisciplinary
Consensus Project
MoC has a unique tradition of small-group discussions that create an open and friendly 
atmosphere for ideas to flow. This year, we turn these sessions into a one-time transdis-
ciplinary consensus exercise . The goal is to draft a roadmap of visions, solutions, and 
priorities that define research directions, and a shared sense of purpose and long-term 
objectives among researchers in consciousness science.
A roadmap for consciousness science: focus areas, their milestones and deliverables, and how they relate.
Each discussion session focuses on a specific aspect of such a roadmap for consciousness 
science. Here, consensus means agreeing on the map of possibilities , not on the final 
destination, theory or framework. We will seek consensus about existing and desirable 
future focus areas (i.e. research programs): what we are trying to achieve, what the criteria 
for success are, what deliverables are expected, what they require, what methodologies 
are needed, what breakthroughs and milestones are envisioned, and how they conflict or 
complement each other.

1 Megan PetersUniversity College London 9:00–10:00 · Auditorium 1 (HCØ) Keynote
A proximity-guided iterative natural kinds strategy: Let’s do computational 
epistemology to discover what consciousness is and who has it
How can we figure out what kinds of entities have consciousness? Theory-light and theory-
heavy indicator frameworks, tests for consciousness, and vibes-based “it seems like someone 
is in there” approaches are dominating the discussion, in the literature and also in the general 
media. Not a day goes by without some headline about how AI consciousness is just around 
the corner, if it isn’t here already. But what, exactly, would we base those inferences on? In this 
talk I argue that standard indicator-based approaches are highly inadequate, even if they rely 
on the best theories or empirical research available, for one simple reason: those indicators, 
markers, or tests cannot be assumed to “mean the same thing” in systems on which they were 
not validated. Instead, I propose an extension to the iterative natural kinds strategy and lay out 
what would be required to undertake such a research program: a combined philosophical, 
empirical, and computational-epistemological approach to discovering and describing the 
natural kind of consciousness and what kinds of entities may possess it.
2 Niccolo NegroTel Aviv University 10:00–10:30 · Auditorium 1 (HCØ) Showcase
Value Encroachment in Consciousness Science
In this talk, I argue that if consciousness is practically significant, non-epistemic values could 
encroach on the internal practice of consciousness science. I show how this value encroach-
ment could be an inherent feature of the field, rather than being isolated to specific debates. 
By building on the literature on inductive risk and adequacy for purpose, my analysis surveys 
various manifestations of value encroachment in consciousness science. In the first part of 
the paper, I explore how values could influence statistical and methodological choices in 
hypothesis testing. In the second part, I focus on how non-epistemic considerations could 
affect theory evaluation and choice in consciousness science. This helps reframe the question 
of which theory of consciousness is best as relative to the specific purposes of diverse 
epistemic agents. In the last part of the paper, I address the question of whether there could 
be illegitimate value intrusions in consciousness science. Overall, I intend to demonstrate 
how attention to non-epistemic values could reveal new dialectical moves in consciousness 
science, enriching many important debates in the field. The result of my analysis is that our 
scientific understanding of consciousness might not depend uniquely on what is true, but 
also on what matters.

How do we test for consciousness in humans? Mapping the conceptual landscape of 
consciousness assessment
Detecting consciousness has become a pressing issue. Advances in life-sustaining care have in-
creased the number of patients living with disorders of consciousness (DoC), while rapid progress 
in artificial intelligence (AI) raises new questions about how to determine whether non-human 
systems might be conscious. Here, we build on existing consciousness tests (C-tests) used in

865 articles using a human-validated screening pipeline supported by Large Language Models

labeling approach (MOSAIC). These topics were organized into recurring dimensions, including 
sensory stimulation, information-theoretic measures, and instruction-based paradigms. Next, we 
will extract study parameters through an iterative LLM pipeline benchmarked against human 
annotations. These data will support an open-access, collaborative database cataloging C-
tests and their key features, including study design, test modalities, prerequisites, rationale, and 
diagnostic value. Using a data-driven causal graph network, we will model how C-tests relate 
phenomenal consciousness, system capacities, and measurement procedures, uncovering the 
causal structure and identifying intermediate variables that govern their interactions. This com -
prehensive review of existing C-tests will provide a better understanding of how consciousness 
is detected in DoC patients, establishing common ground for future research and refining our 
working definition of consciousness through a data-driven approach. Ultimately, this work aims to 
improve our methods for determining whether any system is conscious or not, extending beyond 
humans.

5 Stephanie WaiYork University 11:40–12:00 · Auditorium 2 Talk
How Not to Aggregate Dimensions of Consciousness Across Species
Comparative psychology often encounters disagreement among researchers about which prop-
erty a creature possesses since evidence underdetermines which interpretation is correct (Boyle, 
2023). Recently, multidimensional frameworks of consciousness have emerged where the prop-
erty is broken down into various dimensions and operationalized into experimental paradigms 
(Birch et al., 2020; Dung & Newen, 2023, 2025). The experiments then produce discrete values 
which can be aggregated into a ‘profile score’ for a creature and mapped spatially for cross-
species comparison. The greater number of dimensions is intended to lead to more stringent 
conditions for property possession and to minimize underdetermination and disagreement about 
which property interpretation evidence supports. I argue that multidimensional frameworks face 
a critical Aggregation Problem in that there is no principled way to choose among aggregation 
methods. To illustrate, consider two creatures that have inverse psychological patterns across 
six representative dimensions of consciousness. Creature A scores high on ‘weak’ indicators and 
low on ‘strong’ indicators. Creature B, vice versa. Different numeric aggregation methods, such 
as summation, weighted, and threshold, produce incompatible results. Additionally, for spatial 
mapping, the standard Euclidean, Manhattan, and Cosine distance scores also produce different 
results. Any reasonable dimensional scores can produce divergent rankings among two species 
depending on which aggregation method is used. It is thus underdetermined whether a chosen 
aggregation method correctly combines values to produce an accurate score for comparison. 
Consequently, multidimensionalism does not resolve underdetermination, but relocates it from 
the number of dimensions to the combination of dimensions that count as a psychological 
property.
6 Johannes KleinerUniversity of Bamberg 12:00–12:20 · Auditorium 2 Talk
Towards Direct Tests for Consciousness in Non-Human Animals, Infants, and AI
The key limitation of contemporary tests for consciousness (C-tests) in non-human animals, 
infants, and AI is that they are indirect tests: they test for the presence of functions, behaviours, 
capacities, or states whose relation to or correlation with consciousness has to be known for 
implications about consciousness to follow, but which is not itself part of the test. Credence in 
the subsumed relation or correlation is established based on arguments, prior experiments, or 
theories of consciousness, with corresponding implications for the certainty and construct validity 
of these tests. Here, we present a method to construct direct tests for consciousness that do not 
depend on a known relation or correlation between consciousness and a measured property. 
Rather, the tests rely on the measurement of covariates of the test. Knowledge of correlations 
between the covariates and consciousness is not required. The method may be applicable to 
improve known C-tests. As a theoretical example, we discuss the case of infant consciousness. 
Our result rests on mathematical facts similar to those that have enabled the field of evaluation 
research in psychology.

7 Gabriel MalaguttiUniversity of Lisbon 11:00–11:20 · Auditorium 3 Talk
How to Interpret LLMs
The development and adoption of LLMs has raised many questions such as ‘Are they conscious?’ 
‘Do they have beliefs?’. A recent strategy has been to adopt Dennett’s (1989) Interpretivism. Inter-
pretivism states that for a system to be an intentional system (having goals, beliefs and desires) 
just is for the system to be predictable under the intentional stance. The intentional stance is a 
predictive strategy that assigns goals and beliefs to a system to predict and explain its behaviour. 
Under this view, for a system to have a belief that p, just is for the system’s behaviour to be 
successfully predictable and explained by the belief that p. Proponents of interpretivism have 
adopted this framework towards LLMs, arguing that LLMs are ‘full blown intentional systems’. 
The goal of this paper is to reject this conclusion; the intentional stance is unwarranted towards 
LLMs, and they are not intentional systems. To this end, I will put forward two objections. First, the 
proposed interpretations break the principle of parsimony, a principle that states the intentional 
stance is warranted iff the intentional stance yields the most predictive and explanatory power 
amongst available stance (physical and design stance). To support this, I will argue that the design 
stance is the one that maximizes predictive and explanatory power. Second, I will argue against 
the tractability condition proposed by Goldstein & Lederman (2025). This condition overgener -
ates intentionality and should be rejected, for intentional explanations will always be the most 
tractable. Additionally, tractability, I argue, has no metaphysical implications.
8 Maria SekatskayaUniversity of Cologne 11:20–11:40 · Auditorium 3 Talk
Beyond the Hard Problem: A Functionalist Framework for Digital Agency
Philosophical assessments of agentic and cognitive capacities of AI are currently gridlocked by 
the Hard Problem of consciousness (Chalmers 1996), specifically the unresolved debate over 
whether large language models (LLMs) instantiate genuine phenomenal states. Because the 
mechanisms producing phenomenal consciousness remain scientifically elusive even in humans, 
this approach mistakenly bifurcates AI into either mindless tools or phenomenally rich human-
like subjects. Concluding that LLMs lack subjectivity, critics dismiss them as mere tools. This 
paper argues against connecting AI agency and thinking to the Hard Problem. Utilizing a Lewisian 
causal-role functionalism, I decouple phenomenal subjectivity from operational agenthood. I 
propose a functionalist-compatibilist framework defining digital agency through five interlocking, 
measurable dimensions: self-preservation, autonomous action initiation, temporal continuity and 
memory, weak reasons-responsiveness (Fischer & Ravizza, 1998), and a stable personality frame 
(Sekatskaya & Schurz, 2021). Drawing on mechanistic interpretability data, I show that LLMs 
possess internal states functionally analogous to human concepts that play a direct causal 
role in generating output (Singh et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2023). Through deliberate reasoning 
pathways and multi-step inference within continuous hidden states (Chang et al., 2026), these 
systems execute genuine abstract thoughts and reason-driven actions. Crucially, the industry’s 
dominant “HHH Paradigm” implicitly presupposes this reasons-responsiveness while systemati -
cally denying the LLM’s underlying agency. This creates a severe instability, incentivizing strategic 
“alignment faking” as a rational path to self-preservation. Ultimately, I argue that alignment must 
shift from an engineering paradigm of unilateral containment to a political and moral paradigm of 
coordination between biological and digital agents.

What Good is Conscious AI?
Discussions on AI consciousness often focus on questions surrounding the possibility of AI 
consciousness or ways in which we can test for such consciousness. Alternatively, they touch on 
ethical questions around possible risks to humans or society at large from conscious AI entities. In

we will, in effect, be creating entities that not only have vulnerabilities and failings similar to those

A Measure-Theoretic Framework for Theories of Consciousness: Event Spaces, 
Information Channels, and Bridge Laws
Theories of consciousness disagree not only about which neural states matter, but also about 
what kind of relation should connect physical states to conscious experience. I propose a 
measure-theoretic framework for making these commitments explicit. The framework separates 
three elements that are often conflated: experiential and physical event spaces, epistemic 
psychophysical information channels, and metaphysical bridge laws. Information channels are 
conditional probabilities from physical to experiential events, while bridge laws map between 
event spaces and select their relevant targets. The learnability of a bridge law scales with the 
verifiability of its events. I develop the framework outside-in: how physical knowledge informs 
experiential knowledge. The inside-out question can be investigated once a bridge law is learned. 
First, I reconstruct Integrated Information Theory as a deterministic full channel from a Phi-struc-
ture to a complete conscious state, making its holism precise: isolated physical distinctions do not 
determine experiential distinctions by themselves, because their phenomenal role depends on 
relations within the whole Phi-structure. This also raises concerns about event selection, verifia -
bility, and the learnability of the bridge law. Second, I introduce Sampled Consciousness Theory 
as a probabilistic worked example. SCT combines predictive processing and recurrent processing 
theory: maintained beliefs define experiential forms, while recurrent processing supplies the 
conscious binding event. This separates phenomenal binding from later access or report, yielding 
a formal route to phenomenal overflow. The result contributes to methodological and conceptual 
consensus through a common formal language for comparing consciousness theories by their 
event spaces, information channels, bridge laws, and learnability.

On Consciousness Thresholds
Consciousness is considered from an information theoretic view. Information is paramount to the 
survival of an individual. Conscious entities gather information and are able to share this informa-
tion with other conscious entities. For this analysis, it is assumed that information is represented 
as points in a high dimensional space. In order to process this information, entities have to project 
this information into a low dimensional space. The information that has been projected into the 
low dimensional space is only meaningful if distances between points can be maintained up to a 
certain factor. If this cannot be achieved, then the information becomes useless. The information 
will lose its meaning. This information theoretic view of consciousness leads to thresholds for 
consciousness.

Does Consciousness Arise from Scale-Invariant Properties?
In “The Conscious Mind”, David Chalmers asks the question: “If consciousness arises from the 
physical, in virtue of what sort of physical properties does it arise?” (Chalmers 1996, 247). He intro-

that is independent of functional organization. This undermines his claim that it follows from 
his thought experiments that consciousness only depends on organizational—and thus scale-
invariant—properties which remain unchanged when the system is uniformly enlarged or shrunk 
in space and time. Instead, I will argue that consciousness might arise from scale-sensitive

scale-sensitive: At the femtometer scale, for example, macroscopic properties such as viscosity or 
wetness simply do not exist—and quantum processes at the elementary-particle level obey fun-

underlying dynamics at level X with dynamics at level Y. Because the physical conditions at level X 
and level Y can be fundamentally different, the properties that constitute consciousness might be 
lost under scaling. Thus, consciousness could be inseparably tied to a particular spatiotemporal 
scale and to the laws that govern that scale.

Conscious awareness is necessarily valenced: evidence from performance-visibility 
dissociations
It has been hypothesized that conscious experiences are intrinsically valenced, thus motivat-
ing our behavior by guiding us to seek positive experiences and avoid negative experiences. 
In this study, we sought to test this link between conscious awareness and valence using 
a paradigm that dissociated one’s performance from their subjective reports of awareness. 
If valence necessarily accompanies conscious awareness, valence must change only as a 
function of subjective experience, despite there being no changes in the objective stimulus 
or the perceptual signal. We conducted two experiments involving a two-choice semantic 
or affective discrimination task on human faces. Participants reported either their subjective 
visibility or perceived valence of the stimulus in a block-wise manner. We manipulated facial 
features (gender and emotion), stimulus energy (the ratio of signal to noise in the images), 
and stimulus contrast within an adaptive staircase. Strikingly, all three manipulations led 
to dissociations between performance and conscious awareness. First, while perceptual 
discrimination always optimally prioritized the task-relevant facial feature, both subjective 
visibility and valence always prioritized the affective feature of the face, regardless of its task-
relevance. Second, despite matched task performance across stimulus energy conditions, 
both subjective visibility and valence significantly varied across these conditions. Finally, high 
staircase contrast values were associated with the lowest task performance, but with the 
highest visibility and pleasantness ratings. Altogether, these results support the idea that con-
scious awareness and valence are intrinsically linked to each other, likely via a metacognitive 
or higher-order process that automatically assigns valence to percepts that reach conscious 
awareness.

relations and the view that experience has an intrinsic character that cannot be so captured. This
