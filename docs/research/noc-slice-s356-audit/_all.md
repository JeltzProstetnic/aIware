Is there more to consciousness than computation? This paper answers yes, and locates the extra ingredient in computation itself: a second-order regime in which a system's modelling takes its own modelling as an object, run by a self-model that takes itself for the being it models. The account rests on two claims. The enabling claim is a necessity: consciousness requires free modelling, open-ended computation that the system recruits for its own world- and self-models, such that a feedforward model of matched capacity trained on the same history falls behind it on novel input as compositional depth grows. In cortex, free modelling usually shows as near-critical dynamics, and the state-transition literature on anaesthesia, sleep, seizure and disorders of consciousness records that signature; the relation runs from free modelling to the signature, so a near-critical cortex can be unconscious and a subcritical system conscious, and criticality data corroborate the account without testing it. The constitutive claim says what experience is: information from the system's implicit models is integrated into its explicit models and fed to an explicit self-model that takes the modelled self for the subject. On the computationalism–implementationism axis the theory is computationalist: what makes a system conscious is the form of computation it runs, and the neural signature is a contingent trace of that computation in one substrate. The paper states the falsifier for the necessity claim, a matched-capacity novel-input probe, and shows the account's consistency with integrated world-modelling, recursive self-modelling and higher-order frameworks.


Two claims carry the argument. The enabling claim is a necessity: second-order modelling requires free modelling, open-ended computation that the system recruits for its own world- and self-models, and a system without it has no consciousness, whatever its dynamics. The constitutive claim says what experience is: the integration of implicit-model content into the explicit models and the self-model's conflation of itself with its bearer.

Free modelling is necessary and not sufficient. In cortex it usually shows as near-critical dynamics, and the direction of that relation matters for everything that follows: free modelling causes the near-critical signature in brains of a certain kind, under loads of a certain size, so the signature may be absent in other implementations and absent part of the time even in human conscious processing, while a near-critical cortex running heavy unconscious modelling is expected rather than anomalous. Criticality therefore enters the account as the usual cortical trace of the enabling condition, a fact the state-transition literature has recorded for two decades and that the theory explains without resting on it.

The question behind this paper is often posed as computationalism against implementationism: whether consciousness depends on the computations a system performs or on the physical details of how they are implemented. The four-model theory is computationalist. What makes a system conscious is that it runs a self-referential, second-order computation, a regime that is multiply realizable in principle, and the near-critical signature of that computation in spiking cortex is a fact about one substrate. A different substrate realizing the same computation on different physics can display none of the avalanche statistics of cortex, a point Section 4 develops and Section 8 bounds.


Where can such computation run? Universal computation carries no criticality condition: it is proved for rate networks (Siegelmann & Sontag, 1995) and spiking networks (Maass, 1996) across a broad operating range, and in echo-state models memory and computation co-peak in the driven-stable, ordered regime and collapse at the chaotic edge (Gruber, 2026b). What consciousness requires is narrower than universality and different from it: free modelling, forward simulation over structured representations of situations other than the present one, recruited by the system itself, with no depth of self-reference fixed in advance. Stated at the budget every organism has, the requirement reads: a feedforward model of matched capacity, trained on the system's own history, falls behind the system on novel input as compositional depth grows (Gruber, 2026a, Table 1b).

In cortex this capability usually shows as dynamics held near the edge of chaos, where active information storage and information transfer are jointly high (Langton, 1990; Bertschinger & Natschläger, 2004) and homeostasis holds the set-point a controlled distance below the branching transition (Priesemann et al., 2014; Ma et al., 2019). The relation runs from free modelling to that signature. A human brain engaged in conscious cognition recruits far more tissue than the modelling task needs and keeps survival work running alongside, and that recruitment is a plausible reason the signature becomes measurable at all; a smaller or differently built system meeting the same requirement may leave no such trace, and whether some human conscious processing runs subcritically part of the time is open.


Why, then, is cortex near-critical, and why does the set-point co-vary with conscious level? Optimisation explains the first: critical dynamics maximise dynamic range (Shew et al., 2009), susceptibility and correlation length (Chialvo, 2010), and homeostasis tunes toward them (Ma et al., 2019; Hengen & Shew, 2025). The four-model theory adds a reason for the second that leaves the optimisation account intact: free modelling, the enabling condition of Section 3.1, recruits in a human brain far more tissue than the modelling task needs and keeps survival work running alongside, and that recruitment is what shows as near-critical dynamics across the cortex. When the explicit models are abolished, as under propofol or in deep sleep, the recruitment ends and the dynamics usually settle toward the stable side; when they return, the signature returns with them. The correlation the literature has recorded is, on this reading, the trace free modelling leaves in one kind of substrate under loads of a certain size, which is why it announces no cognitive advantage of its own, and why the theory allows it to fail at both margins: a near-critical cortex running unconscious modelling, and a conscious system small or efficient enough to model freely below the signature's threshold.


## 5. What would disconfirm the necessity claim

The necessity claim is that consciousness requires free modelling, and its falsifier targets that capability rather than any dynamical statistic: robust consciousness in a system whose behaviour on novel input is matched, as compositional depth grows, by an unrolled feedforward model of equal capacity trained on the same history (Gruber, 2026a, Table 1b). The probe is a comparison between the system and a matched model, so it applies now to artificial systems whose weights and dynamics are inspectable and to nervous systems as they become so; in humans its behavioural form compares performance on compositionally novel tasks with the best fixed-capacity predictor of the subject's prior behaviour, a design this paper proposes and leaves to be built. Criticality statistics are recorded alongside the probe as the signature's own validation, with the convergence requirements the criticality literature has developed: avalanche exponents near −3/2 with subsampling-invariant estimators (Priesemann et al., 2014), detrended-fluctuation exponents of 0.6 to 0.9 (Hardstone et al., 2012), the directional edge-of-chaos margin (Toker et al., 2022), and the power-law caveats of Touboul and Destexhe (2017) and Clauset, Shalizi, and Newman (2009). They bear on the claim in one way. A conscious state in which the explicit models are demonstrably running while the cortex sits far from criticality would show that the signature is more conditional than the human data so far suggest, and would leave the necessity claim untouched.

The constitutive claim has a separate test. If the level of consciousness is set by the organisation of the explicit models rather than by a global dynamical variable, a perturbation that degrades the explicit self-model while sparing the substrate should move the fidelity of self-modelling and of world-modelling orthogonally, where a scalar account predicts that they covary; the signal-detection form of that test, with first-order sensitivity free to vary, is set out elsewhere [reference withheld for review]. The state-transition data of Section 3.2 remain post-hoc corroborations shared with the whole criticality literature; the theory's addition is the direction of the relation and the probe that puts the requirement itself at risk.


The requirement is stated at the level that is substrate-neutral: open-ended computation recruited for autonomous self-modelling. A neural substrate meeting it usually exhibits near-critical dynamics, because of how much tissue a brain recruits and how it manages the energy of doing so; a digital realisation of the same second-order computation can meet it with none of the avalanche statistics of cortex, because its open-endedness is supplied architecturally; and a small nervous system meeting it may do so below the signature's threshold. A separate treatment develops the substrate-neutral side directly, testing closure and open-ended computation as enabling conditions for world-modelling in an artificial architecture (Gruber, 2026b). The two treatments describe one condition at two grains, and the neural signature is the local, contingent appearance of the condition in one of them.


Is there more to consciousness than base computation? Yes, and the more is made of computation: a second-order regime in which computation operates on computation, closed upon a self-model that takes itself for the being it occupies. The enabling claim is a necessity: this regime requires free modelling, open-ended computation that the system recruits for its own models, and a system without it has no consciousness whatever its dynamics. In cortex free modelling usually shows as near-critical dynamics, so the field's decades-old criticality signature reads as the trace of the enabling condition in one substrate, present where human conscious processing is and allowed to be absent in smaller or differently built systems that meet the same requirement. The constitutive claim says what experience is: the integration of implicit into explicit models and the self-model's conflation of itself with its bearer. The optimisation programme explained why criticality is useful; the four-model theory adds what the criticality literature was measuring, and puts the requirement itself at risk: find robust consciousness in a system that a matched feedforward model keeps pace with on novel input of growing compositional depth, and the necessity claim is wrong.
