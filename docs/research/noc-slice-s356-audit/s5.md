## 5. What would disconfirm the necessity claim

The necessity claim is that consciousness requires free modelling, and its falsifier targets that capability rather than any dynamical statistic: robust consciousness in a system whose behaviour on novel input is matched, as compositional depth grows, by an unrolled feedforward model of equal capacity trained on the same history (Gruber, 2026a, Table 1b). The probe is a comparison between the system and a matched model, so it applies now to artificial systems whose weights and dynamics are inspectable and to nervous systems as they become so; in humans its behavioural form compares performance on compositionally novel tasks with the best fixed-capacity predictor of the subject's prior behaviour, a design this paper proposes and leaves to be built. Criticality statistics are recorded alongside the probe as the signature's own validation, with the convergence requirements the criticality literature has developed: avalanche exponents near −3/2 with subsampling-invariant estimators (Priesemann et al., 2014), detrended-fluctuation exponents of 0.6 to 0.9 (Hardstone et al., 2012), the directional edge-of-chaos margin (Toker et al., 2022), and the power-law caveats of Touboul and Destexhe (2017) and Clauset, Shalizi, and Newman (2009). They bear on the claim in one way. A conscious state in which the explicit models are demonstrably running while the cortex sits far from criticality would show that the signature is more conditional than the human data so far suggest, and would leave the necessity claim untouched.

The constitutive claim has a separate test. If the level of consciousness is set by the organisation of the explicit models rather than by a global dynamical variable, a perturbation that degrades the explicit self-model while sparing the substrate should move the fidelity of self-modelling and of world-modelling orthogonally, where a scalar account predicts that they covary; the signal-detection form of that test, with first-order sensitivity free to vary, is set out elsewhere [reference withheld for review]. The state-transition data of Section 3.2 remain post-hoc corroborations shared with the whole criticality literature; the theory's addition is the direction of the relation and the probe that puts the requirement itself at risk.
