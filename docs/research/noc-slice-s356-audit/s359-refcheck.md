Two entries truncate authors with "et al.", and 2 Cell Press page ranges need the ".e" suffix. On the in-text claims, three cite the wrong source: two use Priesemann et al. 2014 and one uses Ma et al. 2019 for something those papers don't say.

## 2. In-text claims

| Claim (draft line) | Source says | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Toker 2022.** Waking near the edge on its chaotic side; propofol deeper into chaos; generalised seizures across into the periodic phase; LSD closer to the edge; "three generalised seizures so far measured" (l. 51, 67, 89) | "slow cortical oscillations may specifically operate on the chaotic side of this critical point during normal waking states"; "transitions … either into the chaotic phase, as our evidence suggests is the case for GABAergic anesthesia, or into the periodic phase, as our evidence suggests is the case for generalized seizures"; LSD "may tune slow cortical dynamics closer to the edge-of-chaos critical point". Seizures: "two human epilepsy patients … generalized seizures" (ECoG) plus "a third … generalized seizure" (MEG), i.e. 3 subjects. | **OK.** Two nuances. (a) Toker hedges ("may", "our evidence suggests"), and the directional placement of each state comes from their mean-field model, confirmed empirically as changes in chaoticity ("significant increases in chaoticity … anesthesia", "significant reductions in both chaoticity and Lempel–Ziv complexity during generalized seizures"). (b) The anaesthesia condition was "propofol or propofol and sevoflurane", in humans and macaques. "Propofol" alone is acceptable shorthand. |
| **Priesemann 2013.** Deep sleep closer to the branching critical point than waking, with larger avalanches (l. 53) | "slow wave sleep (SWS) shows large avalanches, wakefulness intermediate, and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep small ones"; "α was closest to zero for SWS, indicating that SWS was closest to the critical state"; "These changes tune the brain closer to criticality (SWS) or farther away (REM)". | **OK.** The "if anything" hedge fits. Strictly, the ordering rests on α (deviation from power-law scaling) and their SOC model, all within a subcritical regime, not on the branching parameter σ alone. |
| **Meisel 2013.** Sustained wakefulness "depletes avalanche signatures that sleep restores" (l. 53) | "signatures of criticality are progressively disturbed during wake and restored by sleep"; the power laws are "increasingly disarranged"; the authors read this as a "progressive distance from criticality toward states characterized by an imbalance toward excitation where larger events prevail". | **Minor wording fix.** "Depletes" is not the source's claim: the signatures are disturbed, and the drift is toward excitation, with larger events. Suggest "disrupts avalanche signatures that sleep restores". |
| **Ma 2019.** Homeostatic restoration over about two days (l. 49) | "network criticality was severely disrupted and subsequently restored to criticality over 48 h" (rat V1, visual deprivation, 9-day recordings). | **OK.** |
| **Priesemann 2014 + Ma 2019.** "homeostasis holds the set-point a controlled distance below the branching transition" (l. 45) | Priesemann 2014 says nothing about homeostasis; it finds in vivo activity best matched by a "driven, slightly sub-critical" model. Ma 2019 says networks were "robustly organized around criticality" and were "restored to criticality". It gives no below-transition offset. | **Mismatch.** Neither source alone supports "homeostasis + a controlled distance below". Either split the claim (slightly subcritical: Priesemann 2014; homeostatic: Ma 2019) or cite a source that joins the two, e.g. Wilting & Priesemann (2018). |
| **Priesemann 2014.** "avalanche exponents near −3/2 with subsampling-invariant estimators" (l. 79) | Priesemann 2014: "there is not a single study that demonstrated power laws for spikes in awake animals". It proposes no subsampling-invariant estimator. | **Mismatch.** −3/2 is Beggs & Plenz (2003). The subsampling-invariant (multistep-regression) estimator is Wilting, J., & Priesemann, V. (2018). | **Kleiner & Ludwig 2024.** No-go theorem; premise of dynamical relevance; verified hardware (l. 111) | "under a general assumption, called dynamical relevance, AI systems that run on contemporary computer chips cannot be conscious"; the chips are "designed and verified to adhere to computational dynamics that systematically preclude or suppress deviations". Dynamical relevance is defined against a reference theory: it holds "only if it is dynamically relevant according to the 'true' ToC with respect to some reference theory". | **Theorem: OK.** **"The price the authors attach to rejecting the premise": overstated as paraphrased.** The authors' stated cost is broader: "empirical tests of theories of consciousness presume that consciousness is dynamically relevant … consciousness must make a difference to the data". They do not say consciousness must then "be read from architecture". The paper also states that whether dynamical relevance holds is something "this paper does not establish". Suggest attributing the "read from architecture" step to the four-model theory, and acknowledging that K&L would count any data-based test, including closure ablation, as presupposing dynamical relevance with respect to *some* reference theory. |
| **Basri, BarAvi & Hemmo 2026.** Reject the unfolding argument's second premise; approximation guaranteed only on compact domains (l. 73) | Abstract: "We argue that the UA is unsound, because the universal approximation theorems don't support it." | **Partly verified.** The rejection of the unfolding argument via the approximation theorems is in the abstract. The compact-domain restriction is standard for universal-approximation theorems, so it is plausible, but check it against the full text before submission. |
| **Fleming 2014, Rounis 2010, Lehmann 2022.** meta-d′ lowered without a detectable first-order change; each design held d′ near-constant (l. 81) | **Fleming 2014:** the anterior PFC group showed "a selective deficit in perceptual metacognitive accuracy (meta-d'/d' …)" "despite performing equivalently"; a "one-up two-down staircase" held performance constant. **Rounis 2010:** "a change in metacognitive sensitivity, not just response bias … TMS did not change stimulus discrimination performance". **Lehmann 2022:** "ketamine-induced deterioration in metacognitive performance, whereas no significant effects were observed for perceptual performance". | **Broadly OK, three cautions.** (1) Fleming reports meta-d′/d′ (M-ratio), not meta-d′.
