Two claims carry the argument. The enabling claim is a necessity: second-order modelling requires free modelling, open-ended computation that the system recruits for its own world- and self-models, and a system without it has no consciousness, whatever its dynamics. The constitutive claim says what experience is: the integration of implicit-model content into the explicit models and the self-model's conflation of itself with its bearer.

Free modelling is necessary and not sufficient. In cortex it usually shows as near-critical dynamics, and the direction of that relation matters for everything that follows: free modelling causes the near-critical signature in brains of a certain kind, under loads of a certain size, so the signature may be absent in other implementations and absent part of the time even in human conscious processing, while a near-critical cortex running heavy unconscious modelling is expected rather than anomalous. Criticality therefore enters the account as the usual cortical trace of the enabling condition, a fact the state-transition literature has recorded for two decades and that the theory explains without resting on it.

The question behind this paper is often posed as computationalism against implementationism: whether consciousness depends on the computations a system performs or on the physical details of how they are implemented. The four-model theory is computationalist. What makes a system conscious is that it runs a self-referential, second-order computation, a regime that is multiply realizable in principle, and the near-critical signature of that computation in spiking cortex is a fact about one substrate. A different substrate realizing the same computation on different physics can display none of the avalanche statistics of cortex, a point Section 4 develops and Section 8 bounds.
