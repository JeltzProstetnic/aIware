# Can the agent detect AI vs human text reliably? (AIW-341) — Fable 5.1 research, S357 2026-10-23

Requested by MG: *"find a working ai detector for YOU, meaning some way for opus or fable to detect reliably ai vs original human txt, i cant be the only one with this issue."* Read-only web research plus the two measured datapoints from S356/S357. Vendor self-reported figures are marked **(vendor)**. Predecessor: `ai-detector-selection-s356.md`.

## Verdict

No detector the agent can run, local or remote, reliably answers the question MG is actually asking. For *fully machine-written* English prose the best commercial classifier (Pangram) now measures at 0–2 % false positives in four independent 2025–26 evaluations, so "detectors are random" is no longer true. But MG's texts are not that case: the ideas and the German are his, the English words are chosen by Claude. Every detector, Anthropic's own watermark included, measures *who chose the words*, and for a Claude translation the honest answer is "Claude". A 100 % AI score on the English is therefore a correct reading of the surface and a false positive about authorship at the same time; no classifier can separate the two, and the one idea-level detector that exists (IdeaLens, Oct 2026) would score the same text as human-ideated. GPTZero additionally called MG's hand-typed German draft and a passage from his printed 2015 book 100 % AI, which disqualifies it as a measuring stick for this author on any text. LLM-as-judge (asking Opus or Fable) is documented at roughly 90 % F1 with 3–4 % false positives on generic short texts in the best 2026 study and was near-useless in 2023; nothing supports it on non-native or polished academic prose. What defends MG is not a score but provenance (the German originals, the 2015 book, dictation and git history), a precise disclosure, and the publishers' own guidance that a detector flag alone must not decide a manuscript. The ranked recommendation is at the end.

Karr et al. 2026 (below) supply one: scores track academic word density and non-STEM register. Philosophy of mind is the worst case on both.

### 2b. Non-native writers

- Liang et al., *Patterns* 2023 (arXiv 2304.02819): 7 detectors, 91 TOEFL vs 88 US 8th-grade essays; TOEFL FPR 61.22 % average, 19.78 % flagged by all seven, 97.80 % by at least one; after ChatGPT "enhance the word choices" FPR fell to 11.77 %. Mechanism: lower perplexity.
- Park, Jeong & Kim, arXiv 2608.26710 (EMNLP 2026 Industry), 135,389 non-native manuscripts and their professionally edited versions, 2018–2025: FPR on the originals Binoculars 0.0 %, DetectLLM-LRR 0.2 %, BiScope 0.6 %, MAGE 16.7 %, Fast-DetectGPT 25.2 % (31.6 % after native editing), RADAR 88.3 %, RoBERTa 93.1 %, log-rank/GLTR/entropy ≈ 100 %. The *same* edits raised scores in token-statistics detectors and lowered them in classifiers. Style, not origin, moves the needle.
- Pratama et al., *PeerJ CS* 2025 (10.7717/peerj-cs.2953): GPTZero, ZeroGPT, DetectGPT on abstracts; non-native authors "more frequently misclassified as entirely AI-generated".
- Pangram **(vendor)**, Emi & Spero 2024 (arXiv 2402.14873): FPR 0 % on 91 TOEFL essays, 0 % on 3,907 ELLIPSE essays, 0.09 % on 5,600 ICNALE essays. VUB 2026 is the independent counterpart (40 ESL-authored papers, all cleared by all four tools; the ESL attribution comes from Pangram's summary of the study, the abstract itself says "fully human-written").
- Al Ali et al. 2026: no perplexity gap for non-native Czech.

Reading: for *unpolished* non-native English, the 2023 bias is real for perplexity-based and old classifier detectors and largely gone in Pangram and Binoculars. That is not MG's case.

### 2c. Human content, machine-translated or LLM-polished (MG's case)

- Saha & Feizi, *Findings of ACL 2025* (APT-Eval, 15 K samples, 12 detectors): on pure human text FPR 1–8 % (Pangram 0.00 %, Binoculars 1.8 %, Fast-DetectGPT 6 %, GPTZero 7.5 %); after **extremely minor** polishing with Llama-2-7B, flagged share jumped to ZeroGPT 32.31 %, **Pangram 42.56 %, GPTZero 64.71 %**; GPT-4o minimal polish flagged 10–75 % depending on detector; GLTR flags 26.85 % of texts with 1 % AI edits; detectors cannot tell minor from major polish.
- Karr, Khvatskii, Hua & Chawla, arXiv 2608.11256 (Aug–Oct 2026): guideline-compliant light editing flagged 38–80 %; unmodified 2023–25 human texts 9–15 %; non-STEM significantly higher FPR; scores correlate with academic word density and token length; humanizer tools leave < 4 % flagged. Conclusion: honest assisted writers face more risk than evaders.
- Wang et al., *JAAD International* 2025 (via Du & Koga's response, PMC12721168): research letters 97–100 % "human" before, 75–85 % "AI" after ChatGPT polishing for readability; non-native authors received fewer high-confidence "human" labels at baseline.
- Originality.ai **(vendor)**, Jun 2026: human English round-tripped through Google Translate flagged 28.02 % (Spanish) / 27.84 % (Portuguese) vs 0.40 % baseline.
- Pangram 3.0 label definitions (Dec 2025, **vendor**): "Light AI assistance" explicitly includes "translation". Pangram's own taxonomy puts MG's workflow in an AI-assisted class by design.

Reading: for polished or translated human text there is no detector with a low false-positive rate, and the best classifier for case 2a is among the worst here (Pangram 42.56 %). This is exactly where our material sits, and worse: ours is not polish but full translation, where every English word is model-chosen.

### 2d. LLM-as-judge: can Opus or Fable just tell?

- Bhattacharjee & Liu, arXiv 2308.01284 (2023): GPT-4 zero-shot labelled 97–100 % of AI text as AI and misclassified over 95 % of human text as AI on TuringBench; "not a reliable method".
- Ji et al., arXiv 2502.12743 (2025, six models): self-detection beats cross-detection but both "suboptimal"; explanations rely on inaccurate features and hallucinations.
- Cross-generation study, arXiv 2609.34691 (Sep 2026, 15 LLMs incl. Claude Opus 4.7, GPT-5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, 1,000 M4GT-Bench human texts of 150–300 words): mean F1 69.5 %, accuracy 76.5 %; 2026-generation detectors reach F1 ≈ 90 % with FPR 3.6 % on human text; self-detection gives no advantage (G3 self 90.1 vs cross 90.4); explanations "may be post-hoc justifications".
- Panickssery, Bowman & Feng, NeurIPS 2024 oral: self-recognition exists but is weak and is the mechanism of self-preference bias, not a usable detector.

Reading: the best documented LLM judge is a 90 % F1 / 3.6 % FPR instrument on generic short texts, untested on non-native or translated academic prose, and it cannot see Anthropic's watermark (key-based, private preview). It is unusable as a verdict. It remains usable as a *register critic* (naming phrases that read as LLM-isms), which is a different job.

## 3. The conceptual question: is "AI-generated" even the right category here?

Detectors do not measure ideation; they measure token-level regularities of the final string (classifiers) or how probable the string is under a reference LM (zero-shot). Both are properties of *who chose the words*. Anthropic's own statement on the Claude text watermark (anthropic.com/news/claude-text-watermark, Aug 2026) is the clearest admission from the source: a translation "carries a watermark, because in this case every word is chosen by Claude"; proofreading leaves "very little (if anything) for the watermark to attach to"; a hit "can only determine that Claude was likely involved with the content at some point. It cannot distinguish 'Claude wrote this' from 'Claude heavily edited this.'" The watermark applies to models launched on or after 2 Aug 2026 (EU AI Act transparency obligation); per heise (25 Sep 2026) Fable 5.1, Mythos 5.1, Opus 5.5 and Opus 5 already carry it, and Fable 5, Sonnet 5 and Opus 4.8 from 30 Sep 2026, so **every English rendering this project has produced since autumn 2026 is watermarked as Claude-chosen text**. Detection is a private-preview API for EU-eligible organisations; no public tool exists (Nature news, Gibney, 13 Aug 2026; heise 25 Sep 2026).

So the honest answer: a positive on a Claude translation is a *correct detection of the surface* and a *false positive about authorship of the content*. The literature has started to say this in so many words. Geng & Poibeau (arXiv 2510.20810, Oct 2025): there is "no consistent or precise definition" of LLM-generated text and detector results "should be interpreted only as references rather than decisive indicators". Dycke, Sakharova, Daheim & Gurevych (arXiv 2606.04906, Jun–Oct 2026) define several notions of AI-generated text, including human ideas with AI composition, and find detectors "only perform well for specific notions but not as broad detectors". Saha & Feizi 2025 recommend "moving beyond binary classification". Floridi, *Philosophy & Technology* (Jul 2026): the question for publishing is not detection but answerability.

The one tool built for the other axis confirms the split. IdeaLens (Rajendhran, Russell, Karpinska, Iyyer et al., arXiv 2610.06778; Nature news 14 Oct 2026) detects *idea provenance* "regardless of who wrote its words", flags 91.1 % of AI-ideated documents at 0.6 % FPR, and in the "shared authorship" setting (human ideas, AI prose) its detection rate drops to 7 %: it reads MG's case as human. Its authors say it must not be used on individual cases, so it is an argument, not a defence.

Imprint's policy (PDF 2025-07-30, verified in S356) permits AI "to improve readability and language" and forbids using it "to create content based on an author's initial ideas or hypotheses". A translation of a finished German text is the first thing; a word-level detector cannot tell a reviewer which of the two it is looking at. That is the whole problem, and it is not fixable with a better detector.

## 4. What actually works for the real need: not being rejected

**Provenance (strongest, and MG has an unusual amount of it).** The ideas are in a printed 2015 German book; the German drafts pre-date every English rendering; dictation produces timestamps; the repo has git history of `.md` sources before translation. The Enago "Responsible AI Movement" resource hub (a commercial editing company's page, not a petition) gives the same advice: "keep drafting records", "disclosed use is harder to penalize", and "detection tool output alone cannot prove AI authorship". Writing-process trackers exist (Grammarly Authorship: free, Google Docs and Word, records typed/pasted/AI-assisted text and replays the process; GPTZero's writing-replay for Google Docs) but prove that a human *typed*, not that a human *wrote*, and Grammarly's "Humanize" laundered AI text into "typed by a human" until a Nov 2025 fix (Plagiarism Today, 6 Nov 2025). For MG they add little over the German originals. C2PA 2.3/2.4 (Dec 2025 / Apr 2026) can now embed manifests and an "AI Disclosure" assertion in text and Office files, but no journal reads them yet. The Claude watermark is provenance *against* MG in the current framing: a future detector run by a journal would confirm "Claude involved", which he already declares; it cannot confirm his authorship of the content.

**Journal and publisher policy (the lever that decides outcomes).** The COPE Forum summary (Zhou & Soulière, Scholarly Kitchen, 25 Aug 2025; the COPE page itself is behind Cloudflare and could not be fetched) states: "Avoid rejecting individual manuscripts based solely on the suspicion of undisclosed AI"; detector output is "hints, not verdicts"; "false positives are common" and non-native authors "worry they will be penalized". Neither COPE nor ICMJE endorses a detection product. Elsevier (policy page, fetched): AI may be used to "improve the language and readability of their work and with the appropriate disclosure", stated "immediately above the references". Nature portfolio: "AI assisted copy editing" need not be declared; other use goes in Methods. Publishers increasingly run their own screens: Elsevier's Check Integrity across ~2,000 journals (press release, Mar 2026; broader than AI text, routed to human integrity analysts), Springer Nature's Geppetto donated to the STM Integrity Hub (press release, Apr 2025). A flag therefore gets a human reader, and the human reader is who the provenance dossier is for.

**Editing strategies that are honest.** Rewording until a score drops is Goodharting (S356) and the Style-as-Confound data show edits move different detectors in opposite directions, so there is no reliable direction to edit in. Three honest options change what is true rather than what is measured: (1) MG writes or dictates the English himself and Claude only proofreads: Nature treats that as undeclared-eligible copy-editing and Anthropic says the watermark barely attaches; (2) Claude drafts the translation and MG rewrites every sentence in his own voice: Anthropic says a rewrite "where every word is replaced" removes the watermark and "it's arguable whether the text can any longer be described as AI-generated", but Karr 2026 shows classifiers may still flag the result, so this is the honest middle path without a guarantee; (3) a human translator, with the cost that implies. Option (1) is realistic: MG works in English daily and holds a Master's; what he loses is the German-first thinking flow.

**Who else has this problem.** Giray, *Serials Librarian* 2024 (10.1080/0361526X.2024.2433256): false positives "disproportionately affect non-native English speakers and scholars with distinctive writing styles"; Giray, Roe & Espiritu, *English Teaching: Practice & Critique*, Mar 2026: "ineffective, unreliable and harmful". Lepp & Smith, Stanford, FAccT 2025 ("You cannot sound like GPT"): 80,000 reviews show reviewers infer author origin from LLM-associated phrases such as "delve" and then apply national stereotypes, a double bind for non-native authors who polish. Wang et al. 2025 and Pratama et al. 2025 are journal-side measurements. Vanderbilt 2023 and the Mozilla 2024 evaluation are the institutional precedents for refusing detector verdicts. No organised petition of non-native researchers was found; the organised efforts are the COPE forum and publisher policy work.

## 5. Recommendation for the agent (ranked)

1. **Stop using any detector as the target. Build the provenance-and-disclosure package instead.** Per paper: German original with file timestamps and git log, dictation log, the 2015 book passages that carry the ideas, an AI-use statement naming the tool and the task ("German written by the author; English translation and language editing by Claude [model]; content, argument and conclusions unchanged"), and a cover-letter paragraph quoting the Imprint language-improvement clause and the COPE guidance against detector-only rejection. Reliability: this is what every policy document says decides the case. Cost: hours. Nothing leaves the machine until submission.
2. **Change the workflow so surface and content have the same author.** Default for new JCS-bound prose: MG dictates or writes English, Claude proofreads and critiques register only. Where the German-first flow matters, Claude produces a rough translation and MG rewrites it sentence by sentence; Claude is then used only as a register critic (the one LLM-judge use the literature supports). Expected effect: large on the truth, unmeasured on classifiers (Karr 2026).
3. **If a journal names its detector and MG wants a prediction, use the Pangram API once per final draft, on his explicit go.** Best independent record (2 % FPR native text; 0–0.1 % in three 2025–26 studies), tiered labels that name translation as "light assistance". Cost $0.05 per 100 words (knowledge hub, reviewed 24 Jul 2026), ≈ $4.50 for a 9,000-word paper. Text leaves the machine and standard plans **store** it ("stored securely so you can view your results"); zero retention is enterprise-only; Pangram states it never trains on submissions. Expected reliability on *our* material: unknown and probably "AI-assisted" or "AI-generated" (APT-Eval 42.56 % on minimal polish; a full translation is more). Use only on text MG is willing to have on a third-party server.
4. **GPTZero: disqualified as a measuring stick for this author, keep only as an exhibit.** A 100 %-confidence AI verdict on a 2015 printed book is a hard falsification and a ready-made appeal attachment. If ever needed again for prediction, use the API, not the web form: API inputs "are not stored by default"; web scans are retained and used "in aggregate" (GPTZero support), which means the German draft scanned in S357 now sits on their servers.
5. **LLM-as-judge (Opus/Fable): never as a verdict.** Documented ceiling ~90 % F1 at 3.6 % FPR on generic short texts (arXiv 2609.34691), near-total failure in 2023, no evidence on polished non-native academic prose, no access to Anthropic's watermark key. Use for phrase-level register critique only.
6. **Local detectors (Binoculars, Fast-DetectGPT, RADAR, RoBERTa, Ghostbuster): discontinue for this purpose.** Our AUROC 0.25/0.75 runs agree with every independent evaluation; RADAR and RoBERTa flag 88–93 % of non-native manuscripts (Park et al. 2026). Keep `scripts/ai_detector.py` only if a future human-vs-raw-render register study needs it; no further model downloads.
7. **Watch two things, no action yet:** Anthropic's watermark detection API (if it ever opens to educational or research users, the agent could verify what a journal would see, and the result will be "Claude involved", which is what MG declares) and IdeaLens or any idea-provenance tool reaching a journal workflow, which would be the first detector whose output matches MG's actual claim.

Plain words: there is no reliable detector for this case because the detectors are right about the words and wrong about the author, and the fix is paperwork and workflow, not software.
