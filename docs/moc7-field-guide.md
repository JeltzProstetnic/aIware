# MoC7 Field Guide for the Four-Model Theory

Copenhagen, 12–16 October 2026. Based on FMT v16 (full paper) and the MoC7 programme booklet. Your poster is **P56, Thursday 15:00–16:30, HCØ main corridor**.

## A note on "standard model workshops"

The booklet contains no session called "standard model". The closest equivalents are:

1. **The Transdisciplinary Consensus Project.** Seven discussion slots in classrooms A101–A112, ending with a plenary on Friday at 15:00. The goal is a shared roadmap of focus areas, milestones, deliverables and methods, which may become a collective paper. In the booklet's words, consensus means "agreeing on the map of possibilities, not on the final destination, theory or framework".
2. **"Comparing Theories of Consciousness", Thursday 16:30, Auditorium 3.** This session is about meta-theory and model selection.
3. **The Minimal Unifying Model (MUM) thread.** MUM is Wanja Wiese's term (Wiese, 2020). It is the field's closest phrase to a "standard model", and Dolega's Thursday talk argues explicitly against it. Wiese is an organiser.

This guide treats all three as the "standard model" track.

## Fix the poster before you fly (high priority)

The P56 abstract in the booklet is out of date compared with v16. Several of its claims are easy to attack at this conference, where the main theme is methodological rigour: Mudrik, Dolega and Yaron-style confirmation-bias critiques are all on the programme.

| Booklet abstract (P56) | FMT v16 | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Title: "...with Independent Empirical Confirmation" | Says "converging support", which is "shared with other criticality-based frameworks"; some of the evidence predates 2015 | The word "confirmation" will be picked apart in the first 30 seconds |
| "Five formal principles", including "substrate must operate at the edge of chaos" | Three principles; near-criticality is "the signature... rather than the requirement itself"; requirement = Class 4 capability | Looks inconsistent if someone has read the paper |
| "Five predictions derived prior to publication in 2015 have received independent corroboration" | Separates consistency with pre-2015 data (Casali 2013, Carhart-Harris 2014) from post-publication convergence (ConCrit, Hengen & Shew, Xu et al.) | This is exactly the kind of post-hoc framing Yaron et al. (2022) criticise |
| "Category-theoretic formalization in development" | Open question 2: no quantitative formalism yet | AMCS is a mathematics society; promise only what you can show |

Recommendation: keep the booklet title, but make the printed poster say "converging evidence". Lead with the three principles. Put Predictions 3, 4 and 5 (the distinctive ones) in the centre. Add one panel on the in-silico result: in a leaky echo-state network, the σ = 1 setpoint was not the edge of chaos, and memory and computation co-peaked in the ordered regime. That result is your best methodological contribution to this audience, because it is self-critical, quantitative and generalisable.

## Consensus project playbook

The facilitators want focus areas, milestones and deliverables, not theory advocacy. The most effective way to make FMT visible is to propose community methods for which FMT is the obvious worked example. Six contributions, roughly in order of how likely they are to make it into the collective paper:

1. **Reporting standard for dynamical markers (methods focus area).** Report σ only next to a rate-matched surrogate. Avalanche criticality and edge-of-chaos criticality (λ ≈ 0) are different instruments that need not co-occur (Kanders et al., 2017). Specify the level of description that carries the claimed mechanism. Deliverable: a checklist. FMT §8.9's ESN result is your evidence. This is your strongest move: it is useful, neutral and quantitative.
2. **Timestamped prediction registry (milestone).** Directly addresses post-hoc correlation (Yaron et al., 2022) and "methods predict outcome" (Overgaard et al., 2026). Each prediction gets an origin date, derivation chain and falsifier. FMT is a natural entry: the 2015 monograph, v16, and the shared vs distinctive split.
3. **Interaction predictions as a class of severe test (milestone).** Most theories can absorb a single-manipulation result. Fewer can absorb a predicted interaction (global manipulation × local deficit). FMT's Prediction 1 (psychedelics × anosognosia) is the template. This also connects to Mudrik's prediction maps, since interaction predictions should rank high in centrality.
4. **Structure-vs-absolute dissociation benchmark (deliverable).** Unsupervised Gromov–Wasserstein alignment of RDMs across individuals, together with cross-subject absolute decoding on the same data. It builds on Kawakita et al. (2025) and the structural turn (Fleming & Shea; Ellia & Tsuchiya). Every structuralist theory can register a prediction on it.
5. **In-silico component tests as a recognised middle ground (focus area).** Make efficiency claims rather than necessity claims; this avoids the unfolding argument, which FMT §8.9 concedes on general grounds. Deliverable: a shared model-system benchmark suite (ESNs, spiking pools, RL agents with self-models). Herlo, van der Vlag and Antonova would sign on.
6. **Two rubrics, not one (theory-comparison focus area).** Offer FMT's eight philosophical requirements as one rubric. Openly acknowledge it is theory-laden, as §7.1 already does, and pair it with an empirical-apparatus rubric. That positions you as a fair broker against Dolega's virtue framework instead of a partisan.

Tone: never say "my theory solves the hard problem" in a consensus room. Say "here is a test that would embarrass my own framework if it failed." Predictions 3 (DID switches) and 4 (lucid-dream onset as a phase transition with critical slowing) are distinctive to FMT, so offer them as falsifiers.
