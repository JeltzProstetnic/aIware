## 1b. What the source actually claims

### Q1 — Does it claim a *continuum*?

**No. Not in the sense the project holds.**

The word "continuum" occurs **exactly once in the entire paper**, and it is not about a
dreaming / mind-wandering / daydreaming continuum. It appears in a discussion of a single
fMRI study (Vanhaudenhuyse et al., 2011) that rated "internal awareness" on a 4-point scale:

> "Moreover, their definition of 'strong internal awareness' is not exactly what we mean by
> dreaming during waking – although there are of course similarities, with dreaming presumably
> representing the extreme pole of the continuum of 'internal awareness' during waking."
> — p. 350

That is a hedged remark ("presumably") about where dreaming would sit on *someone else's
rating scale*, immediately preceded by the authors distancing themselves from that scale's
definition. It is not a claim that the three states form a continuum.

**What the paper actually claims** is narrower and stronger in a different direction — an
*identity of substrate* plus an *intensification* relation:

> "This article argues that the default network, augmented by secondary visual and
> sensorimotor cortices, is the likely neural correlate of dreaming."
> — Abstract, p. 342

> "These findings lead to the more specific hypothesis that dreaming is an *intensified* form
> of mind-wandering (Fox et al., 2013, p. 10) as well as being a form of embodied simulation."
> — p. 347

Section 4 is titled "An enhanced default network: **Dreaming as intensified mind-wandering**".

Note also that **daydreaming is not treated as a third term on a scale**. Domhoff & Fox use
"mind-wandering" and "daydreaming" largely interchangeably as the waking phenomenon, and the
paper's own Fig. 1 caption reads "Brain areas critical to both daydreaming and dreaming."

**VERDICT: the "continuum" claim is UNVERIFIED and should be withdrawn.**
The defensible claim is "dreaming as intensified mind-wandering, on a shared and augmented
default-network substrate" — and even that is offered as a *hypothesis to be tested*, not a
finding. The paper's own subtitle is "a **counterintuitive research proposal**", and its
closing section is "A counterintuitive research agenda".

### Q2 — What exactly does it say about the DMN?

The DMN is claimed as the substrate **only in augmented form**, never alone. Three
qualifications are load-bearing and all three are usually dropped in secondary summaries:

1. **Augmentation is part of the claim, not a detail.** "the default network, *augmented by
   secondary visual and sensorimotor cortices*, is the likely neural correlate of dreaming"
   (p. 342). The augmenting regions — lingual gyrus, caudate nucleus, medial temporal lobe —
   are what the authors use to explain why dreams are more sensorily vivid than mind-wandering.

2. **A specific subsystem, not the whole DMN.** "If the neural network that supports dreaming
   is centered in the *dorsal medial subsystem* of the default network outlined by
   Andrews-Hanna, Reidler, Sepulcre, et al. (2010)…" (p. 349).

3. **The authors themselves concede the DMN is not sufficient for mind-wandering.** In a
   parenthesis on p. 346:

   > "(Note, however, that our recent meta-analytic work has shown that the neural basis of
   > mind-wandering involves more than just default network activity (Fox et al., 2015).)"

   This is Fox undercutting the simple "shared DMN" story **inside the very paper being cited
   for it**. Any project claim of the form "they share the DMN" is contradicted by the source.

**VERDICT on "DMN is the shared substrate": PARTIALLY VERIFIED, and materially overstated as
the project holds it.** Correct form: *the default network augmented by secondary visual and
sensorimotor cortices — specifically its dorsal medial subsystem — is proposed as the likely
neural correlate of dreaming; the authors note that mind-wandering itself recruits more than
the default network.*

**Statistical fragility, in the authors' own words** (p. 347):

> "A caveat to be kept in mind is that all three sets of data are based on comparatively small
> samples, resulting in reduced statistical power. Given how nascent this field of research is,
> the REM sleep meta-analysis was based on only 6 studies (Fox et al., 2013), and the
> mind-wandering meta-analysis on only 10 studies (Fox et al., 2015). Solms's (1997)
> neuropsychological lesion work was similarly based on very small group[s]…"

Six studies, ten studies, and lesion overlap maps built on 6–9 patients. If the paper is cited,
this caveat should be cited with it.

### Q3 — Where is the distinction between dreaming and mind-wandering drawn?

The source does answer this, and the answer is more useful to the project than the similarity
claim. Domhoff & Fox give **five necessary conditions for dreaming** (p. 344), of which three
are the differentiators from waking mind-wandering:

> "(1) an intact neural substrate that can support the process of dreaming…; (2) an adequate
> level of cortical activation…; (3) **an occlusion of external stimuli**; (4) a fully mature
> cognitive imagination system…; and (5) **the loss of conscious self-control**."

Plus a fourth, neural differentiator:

> "the greater activation of regions such as the lingual gyrus (in the medial occipital lobe)
> and the caudate nucleus may help explain **the enhanced sensorimotor qualities that are
> present in dreaming as compared to mind-wandering**." — p. 347

And the proposed mechanism of the difference:

> "More precisely, intensified mind-wandering, in the context of **low levels of activation in
> the regions that support executive, attentional, and primary sensorimotor networks**, may
> make it possible for embodied simulations to be elevated into the dramatic enactments we call
> dreams" — p. 347

> "…then its **decoupling from the dorsal attentional network** may provide the neural basis
> for the loss of conscious self-control that is the final necessary condition for dreaming"
> — p. 349

So the differentiator set is: **occlusion of external input + loss of volitional self-control
(neurally: DMN decoupling from the dorsal attention network) + elevated secondary visual and
subcortical motor activity.** Status: **VERIFIED**.

A further distinction the source draws, worth having: Domhoff & Fox explicitly reject
assimilating dreaming to hallucination or psychosis, against Hobson and Solms (p. 344), and
distinguish dreaming from daydreaming by the involuntariness of self-participation:

> "It is in this sense of being an involved participant in (or observer of) an event, along with
> the inclusion of characters, activities, and social interactions, that distinguishes dreaming
> from other forms of thinking, whether during sleep or waking" — p. 344

### Q4 — Known critiques and replication failures

**The most important one is recent, direct, and was published in the same journal.**

```
Kirberg, M., & Windt, J. M. (2026). The kaleidoscope of bizarreness: The analysis of
    first-person-reports shows the relationship between dreaming and mind wandering to be
    complex. Consciousness and Cognition, 137, 103965.
    https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2025.103965   (Epub 9 Dec 2025)
```

verbatim from PubMed (PMID 41370982); full text paywalled at Elsevier, not read.

This paper names the target claim and reports against it. From the abstract, verbatim:

> "An influential proposal posits that dreaming and mind wandering exist on a continuum of
> spontaneous thought, in which dreaming is intensified compared to mind wandering."

> "Our study is the first to apply an in-depth bizarreness analysis to dreaming and mind
> wandering reports from the same group of participants within a naturalistic setting. Our
> results, based on 379 spontaneous reports, suggest that both dreaming and waking mind
> wandering, have unique bizarreness profiles, involving similarities as well as distinctive
> differences."

> "This means that **dreams cannot, straightforwardly, be described as intensified compared to
> waking mind wandering** on measures of bizarreness, and a more nuanced account, alongside
> more specific measures, is needed."

## 2b. Q1 — What the theory actually claims, in Revonsuo's own terms

Core thesis, from the abstract (p. 877), verbatim:

> "On the basis of this evidence, I put forward the hypothesis that the biological function of
> dreaming is to simulate threatening events, and to rehearse threat perception and threat
> avoidance."

And, stated as the hypothesis proper (§3.2, p. 882):

> "The hypothesis I am putting forward states that dream consciousness is essentially a
> mechanism for simulating threat perception and rehearsing threat-avoidance responses and
> behaviors."

The theory is formalised as **six empirically testable propositions** (p. 878), verbatim:

> "1. Dream consciousness is an organized and selective simulation of the perceptual world.
> 2. Dream consciousness is specialized in the simulation of threatening events.
> 3. Nothing but exposure to real threatening events fully activates the threat-simulation
> system.
> 4. The threat simulations produced by the fully activated system are perceptually and
> behaviorally realistic rehearsals of threatening events.
> 5. The realistic rehearsal of these skills can lead to enhanced performance regardless of
> whether or not the training episodes are explicitly remembered.
> 6. The ancestral environment in which the human brain evolved included frequent dangerous
> events that constituted extreme threats to human reproductive success. They thus presented
> serious selection pressures to ancestral human populations and fully activated the
> threat-simulation mechanisms."

**On the "ancestral-function-of-dreaming" move — the project's attribution is correct, with one
important qualification.** The move is Revonsuo's in the sense that he made it the organising
frame, and he says so explicitly:

> "Previous theories of dream function have not put dreaming into the evolutionary context in
> which it belongs: the ancestral human environment." — Conclusion, p. 898

> "…none of them has considered the human ancestral environment as the proper context for
> evaluating the possible biological functions of dreaming." — §7.1, p. 897

But he credits **priority for an evolutionary theory of dreaming** to others, and the project
should not claim he invented the genre: he names **Snyder (1966)**, "Toward an Evolutionary
Theory of Dreaming", with its "sentinel"/vigilance function, and **Ullman (1959)**, as earlier
evolutionary hypotheses (§7.1, p. 896), and discusses Jouvet (1980) and Winson (1990, 1993).
**VERIFIED.**

An important self-limitation, easy to miss and easy to misattribute against him (§1.1, p. 878):

> "I emphasize that to claim threat simulation as the biological function of dreaming is not to
> claim that every single dream of every single individual should realize this function. It is
> only to claim that in certain adaptively important situations with certain ecologically valid
> cues, the system does become fully activated…"

He also draws an explicit **dreaming vs. daydreaming** distinction (§7.2, p. 897) that bears
directly on Item 1 and cuts against a continuum reading:

> "However, there are also important differences between daydreams and dreams: daydreams very
> often contain interior verbal monologue and they are typically more pleasant than dreams."

> "Furthermore, unlike in daydreams, in dreams we invariably lose our self-reflectiveness: dream
> events happen to us without our control (Domhoff 1996). … Daydreaming is at least partly
> controlled voluntarily. By contrast, dreaming is a fully developed involuntary simulation of
> the perceptual world…"

> "Both daydreams and night dreams consist of mental imagery; but the former tend to chart the
> **goals** we would like to achieve **in the future** …, whereas night dreams tend to simulate
> the **dangers** we (or our ancestors) encountered **in the past**…"

**VERIFIED.** This is a useful cross-link: Revonsuo, in 2000, already argued the two differ in
volitional control, valence, and temporal orientation.

## 2c. Q2 — Evidence base offered, and the strongest published objections

### Evidence base he offers (all VERIFIED from the target article)

Revonsuo offers **no new data**. It is a synthesis of existing dream-content literature:

1. **Emotion norms** — Hall & Van de Castle (1966): ~80% of the ~700 emotions in 1,000 dream
   reports were negative; Snyder (1970): >2/3 of emotions in 635 REM reports negative (§3.4.1.1).
2. **Misfortunes** — 411 misfortunes vs 58 good fortunes in 1,000 reports; ~70% happen to the
   dream-self (§3.4.1.2).
3. **Aggression** — ~45% of normative dreams contain ≥1 aggressive interaction; dreamers
   involved in ~80% of them, more often victim than aggressor (§3.4.1.3).
4. **"Enemies"** — animals and male strangers, at ancestrally-plausible rates (§3.4.2.1).
5. **Children's dreams** — animal characters 25–30% at ages 2–6 vs ~5% in adults; wild/frightening
   animals ~40% of animal characters in one study (§3.4.2.2).
6. **Recurrent dreams and nightmares** — chase/attack themes dominate (§3.4.2.3).
7. **Absence of reading, writing, typing, calculating** from dreams — the selectivity argument
   (§3.4.2.4).
8. **Post-traumatic nightmares** — Nader's figures: 100% of 23 kidnapped children, 80% of 10
   children witnessing their mothers being raped, etc. (§3.5.1).
9. **Hunter-gatherer dreams** — Gregor's (1981) Mehinaku corpus; his own re-coding of 276
   Mehinaku men's dreams found **56.2% objective threats, 7.6% subjective threats, 20.3%
   peaceful, 15.9% none** with 82.2% inter-rater agreement (endnote 13, p. 900).
10. **RBD and NREM parasomnias** as evidence of motor realism (§3.6.2).
11. **Implicit/procedural learning** as the mechanism by which unremembered rehearsal could pay
    (§3.7).

He is candid that the causal determinants are not established, quoting Vogel (1993) approvingly:

> "dream content is remarkably independent of external psychological and physical stimuli both
> before and during sleep… Therefore, the sources of dream content, that is, its themes and its
> specific elements, remain a mystery." — p. 887

And he states his own falsification conditions (§5, p. 894):

> "In order to disconfirm the threat-simulation theory (or some part of it), it must be shown
> empirically that the above predictions are false."

## 2d. Q3 — Has he modified or extended the position?

**Yes — via the Social Simulation Theory (SST), 2015. VERIFIED from the full chapter.**

Core thesis, verbatim from the chapter abstract:

> "This novel concept of dreaming has consequently led to the idea that social interactions in
> dreams, known to be a universal and abundant feature of human dream content, can best be
> characterized as a simulation of human social reality, simulating the social skills, bonds,
> interactions, and networks that we engage in during our waking lives. Yet this tempting idea
> has never before been formulated into a clear and empirically testable theory of dreaming.
> Here we show that a testable Social Simulation Theory (SST) of dreaming can be formulated,
> from which empirical predictions can be derived."

**The relationship to TST is more adversarial than a simple "extension", and the project should
get this right.** Revonsuo frames SST as a **competing theory to be tested against TST**, in the
same abstract:

> "We argue that the SST should be tested by **directly contrasting its predictions with the
> major competing theories** on the nature and function of dreaming, such as the Continuity
> Hypothesis (CH) and the Threat Simulation Theory (TST). **These three major theories of
> dreaming make differing predictions** as to the quality and the quantity of social simulations
> in dreams."

> "By following this research program it will be possible to find out whether dreaming is a
> relatively unselective and thus probably non-functional simulation of the waking world (CH),
> a simulation primarily specialized in the simulation of dangerous and threatening events…
> (TST), or whether it is a simulation primarily specialized in training the social skills and
> bonds most important for us humans as a social species (SST)."

In the body he keeps TST and SST formally distinct:

> "In this case the SST needs to be distinguished from two other theories: CH and the TST. The
> TST is a simulation theory that describes and explains the simulation of aggressive behaviours
> in dreams, by including them under the category of 'threatening events'. The function of
> dreaming, according to TST, is not to specialize in the simulation of social interactions per
> se, but in threatening events; thus, any social interactions are simulated in dreams not
> because they are social events but because they are threatening events."

…while leaving the door open to coexistence:

> "Furthermore, these two simulation theories may not be mutually exclusive but instead
> complement each other. Some specific types of simulations of negative social interactions are
> better accounted for by the TST while other, positively toned simulations can be explained by
> th[e SST]."

And he states the construction explicitly as parallel, not derivative:

> "SST can therefore be formulated in an analogous manner to TST, but in addition to the
> evolutionary background theory, also taking into consideration important social functions such
> as the need to belong, social bonding, social networking, and social support as essential
> ingredients."

**Net:** SST is a **sibling theory built on the same evolutionary-simulation scaffolding**, put
forward as a rival hypothesis to be discriminated against TST empirically, while allowed to
complement it. It is **not** a retraction of TST, and **not** merely an extension of it. No
concession that TST is false appears anywhere in the chapter. **VERIFIED.**

Other post-2000 items, all **UNVERIFIED**, listed only so the project knows what to chase:
Valli & Revonsuo (2009), *American Journal of Psychology*, 122(1), 17–38, review of TST evidence;
Valli et al. (2005) on traumatised Kurdish children, *Consciousness and Cognition*;
Revonsuo (2006) *Inner Presence* (MIT Press); Revonsuo (2010) *Consciousness: The Science of
Subjectivity*; and COVID-era TST applications (2023–2024).

## 2e. Q4 — Does anything in his taxonomy code for MODALITY MISATTRIBUTION?

**Answer: NO. This is a well-supported negative, and it is safe to put to Revonsuo directly.**

### Primary-source evidence (VERIFIED)

for every relevant term. Results:

Both `modalit*` hits are about modalities being *present*, never about being *confused*:

> "All sensory modalities are involved in perceptual dream experience, and approximately with a
> frequency comparable to that of everyday waking experience (e.g., Foulkes 1985; Strauch &
> Meier 1996; Zadra et al. 1998)." — §3.3, p. 882

> "It may also be that other than mammalian brains simply cannot support the sort of
> multimodality simulations that dreaming consists of." — endnote 14, p. 900

The framework's treatment of modality is **additive** — modalities are *involved*, dreams are
*multimodal*, imagery is "integrated multimodal sensory imagery" (p. 879, quoting Foulkes).
Modality is a property the simulation *has*, never a variable that can be *mis-assigned*.
This is exactly what TST's argument requires: TST needs dreams to be *perceptually realistic*
(Proposition 4), so systematic modality error would be a liability to his thesis, not a
category he would want. **VERIFIED — a confident NO for the 2000 article.**

### The 1995 taxonomy (PARTIALLY VERIFIED — and this is where the honest hedge belongs)

```
Revonsuo, A., & Salmivalli, C. (1995). A content analysis of bizarre elements in dreams.
    Dreaming, 5(3), 169–187. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0094433
```
Status: **PARTIALLY VERIFIED** — citation and DOI confirmed; **full text NOT accessed**
(paywalled, APA PsycNet/Ovid). Structure below is from converging secondary descriptions and
must be treated as such.

The scheme is two-dimensional:
- **14 content categories:** Self, Place, Time, Persons, Animals, Body Parts, Plants, Objects,
  Events, Actions, Language, Cognition, Emotions, **Sensory Experiences**.
- **3 bizarreness types:** **Incongruity** (subdivided: distorted / exotic / impossible),
  **Vagueness** (no clear, stable identity), **Discontinuity** (sudden temporal or spatial change).

**The critical nuance the project needs:** the scheme *does* have a "Sensory Experiences" content
category, so a coder can mark a sensory experience as incongruous, vague or discontinuous. But
that codes *"this sensory experience is odd"* — **not** *"this content arrived through the wrong
sense."* The two axes are content-type × bizarreness-type; there is no third axis for
sense-channel, and no cross-tabulation of "modality expected" against "modality experienced".
Modality misattribution is **not representable** in the scheme as described.

**Status of this specific negative: PARTIALLY VERIFIED.** To make it airtight, the 1995 full
text is needed — see §2f.

### The nearest neighbour in his work, and why it is still not the same thing

```
Revonsuo, A., & Tarkko, K. (2002). Binding in dreams: The bizarreness of dream images and the
    unity of consciousness. Journal of Consciousness Studies, 9(7), 3–24.
```
Status: **PARTIALLY VERIFIED** — citation confirmed via PhilPapers, Ingenta and Semantic
Scholar; **full text NOT accessed** (paywalled at Imprint Academic/Ingenta). Note the correct
subtitle is "**the unity of consciousness**", not "the taxonomy of bizarreness".

Per its abstract, this paper reconceives bizarreness as **binding failure** — "an unusual
combination of features in the binding of different information sources." That is the closest
Revonsuo ever gets to the project's construct, and the distinction is worth stating precisely:

- **Binding failure (Revonsuo & Tarkko):** features are combined into the *wrong bundle* — a
  cat with a dog's head. The features are correctly sensed; their *conjunction* is wrong.
- **Modality misattribution (the project's construct):** a feature is delivered through the
  *wrong channel* — a colour heard, a sound felt as texture, content "seen" that was in fact
  merely known. The conjunction may be fine; the *sense-channel assignment* is wrong.

Binding is about *which features go together*; modality misattribution is about *which sense
delivered a feature*. Revonsuo's framework has machinery for the first and, as far as every
source reached shows, none for the second. **PARTIALLY VERIFIED.**

### Field-wide check

No modality-misattribution category was found in any rival coding scheme either — Hall & Van de
Castle; Hobson, Hoffman, Helfand & Kostner (1987); Williams, Merritt, Rittenhouse & Hobson
(1992) — nor in the dream-sensory-modality literature (Zadra, Nielsen & Donderi, 1998), which
codes modality **prevalence** (vision ~100%, audition ~50%, olfactory/gustatory ~1%) but not
modality **error**. All of these are **UNVERIFIED** at full-text level.

**This suggests the gap is field-wide, not specific to Revonsuo** — which makes it a better
question to put to him, not a worse one: it is an unoccupied category, not an oversight.

**Suggested framing for the direct question to Revonsuo** — accurate to what was verified:
> In the 2000 BBS article, sensory modalities enter only as *involved* in the simulation
> ("All sensory modalities are involved in perceptual dream experience…"). In the 1995
> bizarreness scheme, "Sensory Experiences" is a content category that can be coded incongruous,
> vague or discontinuous, and in Revonsuo & Tarkko (2002) bizarreness is recast as a binding
> failure across information sources. None of these codes for an element being attributed to the
> *wrong sensory modality*. Is that a deliberate exclusion, an untested category, or something
> addressed in work I have missed?

That framing states only what the primaries support and leaves him room to correct it.
