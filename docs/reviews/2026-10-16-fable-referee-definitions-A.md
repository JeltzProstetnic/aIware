# Fable red-team referee report — Definitions A (S334, 2026-10-16)

REFEREE REPORT — "Pointing Is Not Defining: The Definition of Consciousness Is Not Settled" (Gruber), reviewed as for JCS/Synthese by a referee sympathetic to the phenomenal-concept tradition.
File reviewed: /home/deck/aIware/drafts/papers-moc7/definitions-not-settled.md (brief: /home/deck/aIware/docs/papers-moc7/definitions-brief.md). No files edited.

====================================================================
1. FIVE STRONGEST OBJECTIONS TO THE CENTRAL ARGUMENT
====================================================================

OBJ-1. The paper refutes a position nobody in its own evidence holds; the opponents already grant the conclusion.
Target: §1 "On this view the definition is the phenomenological one, and any functional or architectural formulation is a hypothesis about it." and Abstract "It is sometimes said that the definition of consciousness is settled ... That claim is false."
Why it lands: The only cited holders of the "settled" view are Kleiner & Ludwig (who make the concept an explicit parameter, i.e. they *agree* it is not defined) and a poster abstract (Smith). §4 then concedes "None of these authors claims to have defined consciousness in the sense of C1 to C5." So the thesis the paper attacks — "the pointer is a theoretical definition" — is a view with no named defender; the realist's actual thesis is "the explanandum is adequately fixed by the pointer, and theoretical definition (if any) is the *output* of science" — which is Searle's own line, quoted approvingly in §2.1. The brief's Q7 already flags this; the draft did not resolve it.
Fix: Reframe the target as a methodological thesis, and state it in the opponent's words. Replace the abstract's opening two sentences with: "It is widely held that the explanandum of consciousness science is adequately fixed by phenomenological characterisation — Nagel's 'something it is like' and its kin — so that no further definition is needed before theories are compared, and functional or architectural formulations are to be judged as hypotheses about that fixed target. I argue that this is true only for the uncontested cases. For the cases that motivate the science (unresponsive patients, infants, animals, machines) the characterisation does not fix a target at all, and the practice of carrying it to those cases silently imports a theory." Then cite a *published* "we all know what consciousness is" statement as foil (e.g. Tononi's "what abandons us every night when we fall into dreamless sleep" — verify wording/source; or Searle's own "we all know what consciousness is"), not a poster abstract.

OBJ-2. On the paper's own Kripke/Putnam framework, "the definition is not settled" is trivially true and the opponent can accept the whole paper.
Target: §2.1 "'The clear liquid in rivers and lakes' fixes reference. 'H₂O' defines." and §8 "Searle placed analytic definition at the end of inquiry. The field is not yet at that end."
Why it lands: In 1700 nobody said "the definition of water is not settled"; chemistry proceeded on the pointer and "H₂O" was a *discovery*, not a definition. By the paper's own analogy, "the definition of consciousness is not settled" = "the science is not finished", which Searle, Chalmers, Block and Kleiner all assert. The conclusion collapses into the view it opposes. Worse, the analogy is *disputed* for this very case by the paper's own authority: Kripke (1980, Lecture III) argues that for pain/sensations the reference-fixer is not a contingent description — "pain is picked out by its immediate phenomenological quality", i.e. by an essential property — so the heat/water model fails for phenomenal states by design. A phenomenal-concept referee will raise this on page 2; the paper never mentions it.
Fix: Add to §2.1 a paragraph: "Kripke himself denied that the water model carries over to sensations: the property by which we fix the reference of 'pain' is, he argued, an essential property of pain, not a contingent mode of presentation (Kripke 1980, Lecture III). If that is right, the pointer is the definition and there is nothing further to settle. I do not assume it is wrong. I note only that it is a substantive thesis — the phenomenal-concept strategy (Loar 1990; Papineau 2002) holds that the *concept* presents the property as essential while the property may be physical — and that a thesis cannot be assumed in order to declare the definition settled. The argument of this paper is conditional: if Kripke's disanalogy fails, the pointer is not a definition; if it holds, the pointer defines but is applicable only from the first-person case, and the problem of §6 returns in a different form." Then retitle or re-thesis: the defensible claim is "the reference of 'consciousness' is fixed only where it is not needed", not "the definition is not settled".

OBJ-3. C1–C3 are question-begging against phenomenal realists; the Table 1 verdicts restate the realist's position scored as failures.
Target: §2.2 "C1. Non-circularity. The definiens does not contain the definiendum, a synonym of it (experience, awareness, subjectivity, feeling, sentience)..." and "C3 ... without first adopting a theory of consciousness".
Why it lands: (a) Every conceptual scheme has primitives; the realist holds that phenomenal concepts are primitive (as "red" or "pain" are) and that this is no defect — Block says exactly that in the sentence after the one the paper quotes ("I don't consider this an embarrassment", because reductive definitions are not to be expected for anything). C1 presupposes that consciousness is not a primitive. (b) C3 demands third-person decidability without theory, i.e. presupposes that consciousness is a functional/structural kind, which Searle and Chalmers deny on principle. So "Circular: Yes / Operationalisable: No" in the nine phenomenal rows are not findings; they are the realist's view restated in the paper's vocabulary. (c) C3 is also internally inconsistent: the paper's paradigm of a successful definition, "H₂O", cannot be applied without adopting atomic theory. By C3's wording, H₂O fails C3.
Fix: Two changes. (i) Rewrite C3 as: "C3. Explicit operationalisation. The definition, together with a *stated* theory, yields a decision procedure for whether a given system at a given time falls under it, that does not rely solely on the system's own first-person report. A definition that can be applied only by the subject to itself fails C3 whatever theory is added." (ii) Add after C5 the justification that the paper currently leaves implicit: "A phenomenal realist may reply that consciousness is a primitive and that C1 is misplaced, as it would be for 'red'. The reply is correct for 'red', whose application any normal perceiver can check from outside. It is not available for consciousness, because the primitive can be applied, on the realist's own account, only by the subject to its own case (§6). C1–C3 are therefore not demands on *any* concept; they are the minimal conditions on a formulation that could decide the contested cases, and the realist who declines them declines the arbitration job, which is the point." Also demote C1 from "criterion" to "diagnostic" — it is doing rhetorical, not argumentative, work.

OBJ-4. §6 proves too much: the report-dependence argument applies equally to every theoretical definition, including the paper's own C7–C8 and §7.3; and it conflates epistemic access with reference-fixing.
Target: §6 "In practice each is applied through a report, and the report is produced by the system whose experience is in question." and §6 "The pointer cannot serve as a neutral arbiter for systems that do not report".
Why it lands: (a) C7 (agreement on uncontested cases) and C8 (explain the reports) test any theoretical definition against the same report-fixed extension, so the definition inherits the limitation; the gain is not neutrality but explicitness + projectibility, and the paper does not say so (the brief's W2 states this exactly). (b) Semantically, the realist says reference is fixed by acquaintance with one's own case and *extended* to others by inference (Nagel says so for the bat); report is evidence, not the reference-fixer. The paper's "applied through report" slides from "we learn the extension via report" to "the extension is drawn by report". (c) Empirically false as stated: in animal research the pointer is applied through behavioural/physiological analogy, not report. (d) Nisbett & Wilson concern reports of *causes* of judgements, not reports of the *occurrence* of experience; Schwitzgebel 2008 is the same author who in 2016 holds that the *presence* of experience is "folk-psychologically obvious" — he distinguishes unreliability about character from unreliability about existence, and the paper uses his 2008 against his 2016 without noting that he has an answer.
Fix: Replace the §6 opening with: "The phenomenal formulations are presented as observation-neutral. In practice the extension of each beyond the first-person case is fixed by inference from report and from similarity to reporters, and the report is produced by the system whose experience is in question." Add at the end of §6: "A theoretical definition does not escape this evidential base; C7 and C8 test it against the same report-fixed cases. What it adds is a stated condition that projects to non-reporting systems, so that the extrapolation is a hypothesis in the open rather than an analogy in the dark. The gain is explicitness, not neutrality." Add one sentence answering Schwitzgebel: "Schwitzgebel (2008, 2016) separates unreliability about the character of experience from its presence; the point here concerns neither, but the boundary of the extension, which is drawn by the same reports."

OBJ-5. The survey numbers measure disagreement about *theories*, not about the *definiendum*; the paper equivocates between accepting Block's distinction and accepting overflow.
Target: §5.1 "That is the distinction Block's pointer is meant to isolate, and four in ten respondents did not accept it."
Why it lands: One can share Block's pointer exactly and deny overflow — that is what the A/P debate *is*; both sides agree on what P-consciousness points at and disagree whether it exceeds access. The 60.6% is a vote on an empirical/theoretical thesis. "Requires higher-order states" (34.5%) is a vote on HOT theory. "Sharp transition" is an empirical question. None shows disagreement about the definiendum. The designers' *decision not to ask* for definitions is an expectation, not a datum, and the paper treats it as evidence ("The survey design is itself informative"). Only the quoted conclusion sentence supports the paper, and it is the authors' interpretation.
Fix: Replace the sentence with: "That question concerns the empirical thesis of overflow, not Block's distinction itself; a respondent can accept the distinction and deny overflow. What the number shows is that the field's most-used pointer does not, by itself, fix whether the states it points at extend beyond the states that can be accessed, which is the boundary a definition would have to draw." And rewrite the design paragraph: "The designers chose not to ask respondents to state a definition, expecting difficulty; that expectation is not itself evidence, but it is a judgement by seven researchers close to the field about what a definition request would yield." Drop the HOT and sharp-transition numbers from the definiendum argument or label them as theory-disagreement.

2. MISREPRESENTATIONS / DISPUTABLE PARAPHRASES OF CITED AUTHORS
====================================================================

BLOCK (1995). (a) Tone: Block concedes the fact of circularity and denies it is a defect ("I don't consider this an embarrassment" — because reductive definition is not to be expected of anything fundamental). The abstract's "Their authors say this in print" reads a concession of inadequacy into what is a methodological stance. (b) "asserts that they [P and A] come apart" (§3.2) — Block argues conceptual possibility and discusses hypothetical/possible dissociations (super-blindsight); he is cautious about actual ones. Say "argues that they can come apart". (c) Block's characterisation of P-consciousness is not only "equation with experience"; he lists P-conscious kinds (sensations, perceptions, thoughts with P-content, feelings), i.e. definition by example, like Schwitzgebel. The table row understates this.
SEARLE (1998). Quotes are right, but Searle's point in the same paper is that the common-sense identification is *sufficient* to begin the science and that demanding a prior definition is a mistake; and he holds consciousness has a first-person ontology irreducible to third-person criteria. Citing him for "the field has a reference-fixer and is still looking for the definition" is fair; using him alongside C3 is not — he rejects C3 in principle. Note this.
SCHWITZGEBEL (2016). (a) The examples are sorted by the *theorist* (Schwitzgebel), not by "the system"; the §3.6 sentence "does not meet C3 for any system that cannot sort its own states into the two lists" misattributes the sorting. (b) He would deny the examples are sorted "by reportability"; his criterion is folk-psychological obviousness, and he presents the boundary as deliberately left open for contested cases — the "weakness" §3.6 identifies is advertised by him as a feature ("innocence" = no metaphysical or boundary commitments). (c) Verify the claim that masked-stimulus responses are in his negative list and that he gives the reason the paper gives; from memory his negatives are hormone regulation, fingernail growth, dispositional/tacit knowledge, and subliminal processing, without a stated reportability rationale.
CHALMERS. (a) "Chalmers (2018)... classes the meta-problem as an easy problem" — he says it is an easy problem *in principle* (functional) but does not accept that solving it dissolves the hard problem; C8 uses it correctly but §6 leans harder than Chalmers allows. (b) §4: "Chalmers (1996) opens *The Conscious Mind* by citing Sutherland" — Chalmers quotes Sutherland's dictionary to illustrate the difficulty and then says consciousness can be *characterised* though not defined; §4's summary "One says that the concept resists definition" attributes Sutherland's claim to Chalmers. Write "Chalmers endorses Sutherland's point that...". (c) More importantly, Chalmers holds that indefinability is harmless because we know the referent by acquaintance; he is thus an instance of OBJ-1.
KLEINER & LUDWIG (2024). "treat the concept of conscious experience as a free parameter" (abstract) overstates. Their relativisation to "one's chosen concept" is a neutrality device across theories, not a concession that the concept is undefined; they take the standard phenomenal pointer as adequately fixing the explanandum. §1 cites them as a "published version" of the *settled* view, which they do not state. Also "recursively" circular (table): their unity gloss is a structural description, not a definition of experience; they would say the recursion is in the structure, not the definiens. Cite the wider structural programme (Fink, Lyre & Kob 2021; Lee 2021; Prentner; Tsuchiya & Saigo) or the "programme" label reads as one paper.
FRANCKEN ET AL. (2022). The quoted conclusion is accurate; the use of the three percentages is not (see OBJ-5). Also check whether the senior/junior difference is on the sharp-transition item specifically.
NAGEL (1974). §3.1 presents the "resemblance reading" as a live ambiguity. Nagel's own footnote forecloses it: the phrase "does not mean 'what (in our experience) it resembles' but rather 'how it is for the subject himself'". Present the resemblance reading as one Nagel explicitly rejected and that Stoljar re-examines, not as an open reading.
STOLJAR (2016). Verify the paraphrase. My recollection is that Stoljar defends an "affective" reading ("being in X affects S in some way") and argues *against* the view that the phrase entails awareness of being in the state (contra Kriegel/Rosenthal). The paper's "on the reading that does say something it implies that the subject is aware of being in the state" may be the view Stoljar criticises. The brief marks Stoljar [V] by bibliography only, not by content.
FRANKISH (2016). §3.10 "Frankish accepts this description [an account of why we say we are conscious, not of consciousness] and holds that nothing further exists to be defined" — misrepresents. Frankish insists illusionism *is* a theory of consciousness (the paper's own reference title), not a change of subject; he would say the realist's description of it is tendentious. Rewrite: "Frankish denies that this is a change of subject: on his view the illusion is all there is to explain."
METZINGER (2020). "fixes the concept by reports of 'pure awareness'" — MPE is fixed by a set of phenomenological constraints (wakefulness, low complexity, self-luminosity, introspective availability, epistemicity, transparent otherness — verify list) and treated as a theoretical construct; the MPE-92M questionnaire is one data source. "Fixed by reports" is a caricature he would reject; say "constrained by contemplative reports".
LAUREYS / CLINICAL. The "inversion" is lexical only. Clinical "awareness" is *operationalised* as command-following, i.e. as access — which is Chalmers' "awareness". So the operationalisations overlap while the words cross. §3.8/§5.3 should say so; otherwise a clinician referee will object that the two literatures agree more than the paper claims.
GRAZIANO & WEBB. "Undefined terms: none essential" — "attention" is as theory-laden as "awareness"; the table applies a laxer standard to the functional rows than to the phenomenal ones (see §3 below).
IIT. The paper cites 2015/2016 only; IIT 4.0 (Albantakis et al. 2023) restates the axioms (existence, intrinsicality, information, integration, exclusion, composition). Also IIT explicitly claims an *explanatory identity* (experience = cause-effect structure), which is the paper's C6; §3.5 treats this fairly, but §1 lists "the axioms of IIT" among formulations "usually offered as its definition" — IIT does not offer the axioms as a definition.

3. LOGICAL GAPS AND EQUIVOCATIONS
====================================================================

G1. "Definition" is used in at least four senses without marking: (i) lexical/dictionary meaning, (ii) reference-fixing description, (iii) theoretical identification (H₂O), (iv) operational definition (C3). The title and abstract use the unmarked term; §2 distinguishes (ii) from (iii); C3 then adds (iv); §5.3 ("A settled definition would remove the need for translation") uses (i). The headline thesis is true in sense (iii), false in sense (ii) for uncontested cases, and the opponent grants (iii). State in §2.1 which sense "settled" is denied, and keep it fixed. Carnap's *explication* is the correct frame for what the paper proposes in §7 and is not cited.
G2. "Settled" is never defined: consensus? correctness? adequacy for theory comparison? The intro's opposing view is a thesis about the *explanandum*, not about a definition; §5 then measures consensus. Pick one.
G3. Uneven standards in Table 1. "Circular" counts any synonym including "aware"; by that standard A-consciousness ("reasoning", "rational control") presupposes cognition and AST presupposes "attention". The two-group pattern (§3, "A settled definition would have to sit in both groups at once") is the paper's best argument, but it is produced partly by scoring the functional rows leniently. Either apply the synonym test symmetrically (and accept that no row is "non-circular" in the strong sense) or restate C1 as "does not contain the definiendum or a term whose application presupposes it".
G4. The dilemma in §3 lets the phenomenal realist's non-acceptance of functional definitions define "accepted as about consciousness", then uses that non-acceptance as evidence of non-settledness. Illusionists say the definition *is* settled (functionally) and realists are wrong. "Not settled" then means only "there is a dispute" — true, thin.
G5. §5.4 "applying it requires knowing whether there is something it is like to be in that state, and that is the question at issue" conflates the pointer's *content* with an *application procedure*. The pointer states exactly which further fact decides (C5's second disjunct is met by it); what it lacks is a third-person route to that fact. H₂O likewise gives no detection procedure on Europa without chemistry. The real demand is "third-person accessible in principle", which is C3 again — so C5's second disjunct cannot be used against the pointer without C3, and C3 is contested (OBJ-3).
G6. §6→§7: "the pointer cannot arbitrate non-reporting cases" → "a theoretical definition is needed". But a definition plus bridge premise decides contested cases by whichever hypothesis one accepts, i.e. by the theory. Neither arbitrates neutrally. The paper should say the gain is explicitness of the extrapolation, not an arbiter.
G7. §3.4 dilemma: "If that position is right, the definition cannot be settled in the sense that matters to an experimenter; if it is wrong, the definition has not yet been given." First horn equivocates: if Chalmers is right the definition is settled *as a primitive* and what the experimenter needs is psychophysical laws, not a definition. The horn supports OBJ-1.
G8. C9 reinvents a standard distinction. "Capacity vs occurrence" is Rosenthal's creature vs state consciousness (Rosenthal 1986; 2005), and the claim "The phenomenal pointer cannot distinguish 'capacity intact, occurrence paused' from 'capacity absent'" is false: the pointer plus a dispositional operator ("a being such that there *can* be something it is like for it") makes the distinction, and ordinary usage already does ("a conscious creature" vs "a conscious state"). Cite Rosenthal and Bayne 2007 ("Conscious states and conscious creatures"); rewrite C9 as a *bookkeeping* requirement, not a defect of the pointer.
G9. C6 says the bridge is "of the same kind as 'water is H₂O'", i.e. an identity. Property dualists (Chalmers) accept bridging *laws*, not identities. As stated C6 excludes the realist by fiat. Write "an identity or a nomological bridge".
G10. §7.2's seven dimensions overlap heavily with Bayne, Hohwy & Owen 2016 ("Are there levels of consciousness?", multidimensional model) and Birch, Schnell & Clayton 2020 ("Dimensions of animal consciousness"), neither cited; Van Gulick's SEP entry already lists the senses of "conscious". The "no comparable map of definitions exists" claim (§5.2) will be read as not having looked.
Are C1–C9 question-begging against phenomenal realists? C1, C2, C3 yes as stated (OBJ-3); C4, C5, C7, C8 no (a realist can accept them as desiderata, and C8 is Chalmers' own test); C6 yes via "identification" (G9); C9 no, but it is not the realist's problem (G8). Net: the criteria can be made realist-neutral with the OBJ-3 rewrite of C3, the "or presupposes" rewrite of C1, and the G9 rewrite of C6, plus the explicit "these are conditions on an arbitration device, which the realist may decline" sentence.

4. OVER-CLAIMS AND MINIMAL HEDGES
====================================================================

- Abstract "That claim is false." → "That claim conflates agreement on a reference-fixer with possession of a definition."
- Abstract/§8 "Every accepted formulation rests on terms it does not define" → "Each of the twelve formulations examined rests on...".
- Abstract "none can be applied without first-person report or a prior theory" → "...without first-person report, inference from reporting cases, or a prior theory".
- Abstract "Survey data show that researchers disagree about the definiendum itself" → "The one systematic survey concludes that the definition of what is to be explained is not shared among its respondents".
- §1 "Published versions of this position are common." → cite three published book/journal statements or write "This position is more often practised than stated; the practice is to open with Nagel's formula and proceed."
- §1 "the axioms of integrated information theory" as a formulation "usually offered as its definition" → "sometimes treated as".
- §3 "Formulations that meet the criteria for a definition are rejected as changes of subject." → "...are rejected by phenomenal realists as changes of subject."
- §4 "The strongest evidence ... comes from their authors." → "Four of the six authors say so in print" (Nagel says nothing of the kind; Chalmers quotes Sutherland).
- §4 "One says that the concept resists definition." → "One endorses Sutherland's remark that it resists definition."
- §5.1 "four in ten respondents did not accept it" → "four in ten did not endorse overflow".
- §5.3 "A settled definition would remove the need for translation." → delete, or "A shared vocabulary would remove the need for translation; a settled definition of *consciousness* would not by itself fix the word *awareness*."
- §6 "each is applied through a report" → "each is applied through report or through inference from systems that report".
- §6 "The IIT existence axiom is a first-person assertion, an act of self-attribution." → "...a first-person assertion; whether it is an act of self-attribution in the sense of attention-schema theory is itself a theoretical claim."
- §6 Nisbett & Wilson → add "about the causes of judgements, not about the occurrence of experience; the relevance here is that the reporting system interposes theory".
- §7.3 "Its terms ... can be defined without reference to experience, so it meets C1 formally" → "I claim elsewhere that its terms can be defined without reference to experience (the derivation and the reply to the charge that 'model' and 'relate to itself' smuggle the definiendum are given separately); here it serves only as an example of the form C6–C9 require." Stronger recommendation: move §7.3 to a footnote or cut it. A survey whose criteria are met by the author's self-published definition will be discounted by referees (brief W9), and it is the one place the paper looks like advocacy.
- §5.2 "No comparable map of definitions exists" → "No map of *definientia and their dependencies* exists that I am aware of; Bayne et al. (2016) and Birch et al. (2020) map dimensions of consciousness rather than definitions."
- MoC7 abstracts (Smith, Katyal, Anthes, Peters, Reichert) are used as load-bearing evidence of the field's positions in §1, §5.2, §5.5 and §7.1. Conference abstracts are weak evidence at JCS/Synthese; demote to footnotes ("as one recent conference abstract puts it") and carry each point on a published source where possible.
- §3.3 / §5.4 "Searle and the clinical tradition fix the boundary of consciousness at dreamless sleep" → "anchor the boundary at" — neither claims it is a definitional boundary.

====================================================================
5. OVERALL VERDICT
====================================================================

As it stands the paper would not survive review at Synthese and would get a major-revision at best at JCS. The scholarship is careful, the Table 1 dilemma (§3) and the report-dependence point (§6) are genuinely useful, and the prose is clean. But the headline thesis is one its named opponents already hold (Searle, Block, Chalmers and Kleiner all say the pointer is not a theoretical definition and that this is fine), the Kripke authority cuts against the water analogy for precisely this case and is not addressed, and the criteria that generate the Table 1 verdicts presuppose the realist is wrong — so a phenomenal-concept referee will say "nothing here is news, and what is new is question-begging," and the self-published definition in §7.3 will confirm the suspicion that the criteria were built to fit it. The single change that most improves the odds: replace "the definition is not settled" with the sharper, non-trivial thesis the paper's own §5.4 and §6 already support — that the pointer fixes reference only for cases that report or resemble reporters, that every extrapolation to patients, infants, animals and machines therefore imports a theory the extrapolator does not state, and that the field's obligation is to state it (Francken's recommendation, made rigorous). Build C1–C9 as conditions on that extrapolation device rather than as requirements a "definition" must meet, concede explicitly that the realist may decline the device, address Kripke's pain disanalogy in §2.1, cite Rosenthal (creature/state), Bayne-Hohwy-Owen 2016 and Birch et al. 2020 for the map, and cut or footnote §7.3. With those changes it is a publishable JCS paper and a plausible PhiMiSci one.
