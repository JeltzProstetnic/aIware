# EN BOOK (Chapter 13)

## PIECE A — "Two grips, neither free"

**INSERT-AT:** after the NDE/salvia avalanche + life-review paragraph (~line 1426; downstream of the Libet block at 1370–1386), before "What you can do with this knowledge" (1442).

> So sit with what this actually means, because it's easy to read it and not let it land. For roughly ninety-nine percent of your waking life, you are not the one deciding. You are the one being informed. The substrate commits, and a half-second later "you" receive the news, neatly narrated, in the right order, with your name on it. You are a delayed observer who has been told, your whole life, that he is the author. Keep that in your hand for the next part, because everything that follows breaks if you put it down.
>
> Now. Consciousness does reach out and touch the world — that much is real. It does it in two completely different ways, and they feel nothing alike. The first is the one we usually mean. The virtual self shapes the substrate, the substrate shapes behavior, behavior shapes the world — and that loop turns slowly. Months. Years. You decide to become a different person and, if you're lucky and persistent, two years later the people around you notice. It is the most indirect causal grip imaginable, so indirect that you can spend a decade doubting it works at all.
>
> The second grip is immediate, and it is the one nobody warns you about. You cannot change the room you're sitting in this second. You *can* change what you are experiencing this second. Right now, without moving, you can pull your attention inward, build a scene that isn't here, run a memory, sink into a fantasy, detach from the noise around you and live somewhere the world didn't put you. No two-year delay. No behavior, no substrate-shaping detour through the outside world. You simply retreat into a world of your own making, and you do it *now*. That feels like the one place you are finally in charge — the one act that is unmistakably, instantly *yours*.
>
> It isn't. This is the part that stings, and it stings precisely because we just walked through Libet. The decision to retreat inward was made by the substrate before "you" knew you'd made it — exactly like the decision to lift your hand in the lab. "I chose to daydream" is the same delayed-observer report as "I chose to move my arm," dressed in nicer clothes. The substrate fired first. You were told afterward, and the telling arrived fast enough to feel like authorship. The immediacy you're so impressed by is the *speed of the chain*, not freedom of choice. Nothing here is free. The inner retreat that felt like your private sovereign act was decided for you, like everything else.
>
> So what does consciousness actually contribute, if not the choosing? It contributes *necessity*. The substrate may have fired the starting gun, but without the running simulation, the chain reaction goes nowhere — there is no detachment to feel, no inner world to retreat into, no fantasy to inhabit, because the simulation is the only place those things can happen. Strike consciousness out and the whole downstream cascade simply doesn't propagate. That is the real second causal role: not a seat of will, but a load-bearing link in the chain. Consciousness has no independent causal power — and it is also not a useless passenger, not the epiphenomenon the philosophers worried about. It is the part of the mechanism without which the rest cannot happen. A necessary link, not a free agent. Which is, I'll admit, less thrilling than it first sounds. You go looking for the one place you're finally free, and you find a place where you're indispensable but still not in control. The fast grip turned out to be fast, not free.
>
> And here I have to be honest about a limit, because the temptation is to overshoot. That the *timing* of the inward turn isn't yours — Libet covers that much. How much you steer what happens *inside*, once you're there, is a different question, and the honest answer is that we don't know. And the question is sharper than it first looks. What's really at stake isn't how much freedom your self-model has in general — it's how much grip the self-model has on the *world*-model: how far the avatar can reach in and reshape the simulated world it finds itself inside. That is a superpower in its own right, and not a basic one. It takes a certain depth of self-model even to attempt it, which is exactly why it isn't evenly distributed and why it can be *trained*, at least partway — the same way you can learn to climb your own visual cortex on purpose, from raw phosphenes up through patterns to full faces and scenes (Chapter 6), instead of just waiting to see what surfaces. How many degrees of freedom the machinery underneath handed your self-model for that job, nobody has counted. Maybe you genuinely steer what you explore in there, to a real and trainable degree. Maybe you mostly don't. Introspection can't settle it; it's the one instrument guaranteed to be blind here, and settling it for real would take a full wiring map of a human brain and a great deal of computation. So take the deflation for exactly what it is and not one inch more: the *opening* of the inner act isn't yours to author. What you do once the curtain is up — how far you can bend the world behind it — stays an open question, and an honest one.
>
> And here is the counterweight, because I don't want you closing the book here thinking the inner world is a cage. That same capacity — the ability to decouple from what's in front of you and run your simulation on its own contents — is, used in the right measure, the single most powerful thing evolution ever built. Every act of cognition lives here. So does creativity. So does memory itself, and the trick the ancients called the memory palace — walking imagined rooms to store what matters. Imagination, planning, the entire machinery of "what if I did this instead," the ability to step out of the present and travel forward and backward in your own mind — none of it requires the world to cooperate, all of it requires this exact ability to pull inward and let the simulation run on internal content. The slow outward grip built civilizations over generations. This inward one builds an entire thought in a second. It is the engine of everything we mean by human thinking. Reach for it lightly, reversibly, in graded doses, and it is a superpower.
>
> Lean on it too hard, though, and the dial that lets you in can stick open. Run the inner retreat at full power and the thin channel that ties your simulation to the actual world gets overwhelmed — the same lock-in I described earlier, the same runaway that NDE and high-dose salvia force on you involuntarily, except here you walked in on your own feet. Push far enough and you stop being able to find your way back to the shared world at all. That's the salvia subject climbing out the window because the window is no longer real to him (Chapter 6). And the simulation, pushed that hard, doesn't always come back as one thing — it can fork, split, return as more than one of you, or as someone who isn't quite who left (the two minds sharing one brain, Chapter 9). Used in measure, it's the best tool you have. Overdriven, it is genuinely dangerous — and if you do it too much, who knows if you will come back and how many of you.

## PIECE B — extension of "What you can do with this knowledge"

> There's one more thing the theory hands you, and it's the part I almost didn't write down, because it edges close to self-help and I have a low tolerance for that genre. But it follows directly from everything above, so here it is.
>
> You did not choose to read this book. Something put it in your path — a recommendation, an algorithm, a bored afternoon — and your substrate, in a state you didn't author, decided to keep going. That's luck, not virtue. I told you in the last pages that nothing you do is freely chosen, and I meant it. So you might reasonably ask what the point of any of this is, if the gears were always going to turn the way they turn.
>
> Here's the point, and it's the hopeful turn the whole argument has been building toward: the gears are *shapeable*. Determinism doesn't mean you're stuck — it means you're trainable. Your substrate is not a fixed crystal; it's a landscape that exposure and practice physically rewrite. Reading this changed something, whether you wanted it to or not. And once a system has met an idea, the same unfree machinery that brought you here can now "decide" — substrate-made, yes, but real, and with real consequences — to spend its time differently. You won't have chosen it the way the libertarians dream of choosing. But the choice still lands in the world, still reshapes the network, still alters who you are next week. That's not nothing. That's the only kind of freedom that was ever on offer, and it turns out to be enough to build a life on.
>
> So if I've talked you into anything, let it be this: block out time for pure consciousness work, the way you'd block time for the gym, and treat it as non-negotiable. Meditation, first — not for the incense and the playlists, but because clearing the stage on purpose is the cleanest training your attention will ever get. It sharpens the background discipline you bring to everything else, and it lets the pathways you grind down all day actually regenerate. Block time to *think* — at large, or aimed at one problem you care about — and protect it from the phone. Make things. Play music; it folds into any of the rest. Take up any hobby, sport included, that mainly uses your consciousness in a way you genuinely enjoy — the criterion isn't productivity, it's whether the inner machine is doing something it likes. And reframe the dead time you already have: every traffic jam, every waiting room, every queue is not stolen from you, it's *gifted* to you — uninterrupted permission to retreat inward and run the simulation. Polish that retreat. Enjoy it. It's the one appointment that's never cancelled.
>
> Then there's the maintenance — call it mental hygiene, because that's exactly what it is. First, unlearn the negative inner monologue. It is not honesty and it is not realism; it's a worn groove in the substrate that you can wear smooth again with attention. Frame things in black and white, or better, just in white — leave the grey for the rare case where you genuinely can't avoid it. The grey zone is where rumination breeds, and rumination is the inward grip turned against its owner. **[CUT — LOCKED (MG, S232): the Russia/grey joke is out; substance kept. >
> While we're on the substrate turned against itself: anger. Being angry at a person or a thing is, almost always, punishing *yourself* for a problem that lives outside you. That feeling was never built to point inward. It evolved to drive action when we still had to chase down dinner — a burst of resource mobilization aimed at the world, not a corrosive you pour over your own circuits while the offending thing carries on, unbothered, somewhere else. When you notice the anger has nowhere to go but in, that's the signal to put it down.
>
> And if you've tried meditation and bounced off it — which is common, and no failure — there's a back door I can recommend from experience: martial arts. It sounds like the opposite of a calm mind, all impact and adrenaline, but the discipline of training the body to act while you get the conscious chatter out of the way is the same skill meditation teaches, arrived at from the other side. Honestly, of the two, meditation is the *harder* path, not the easier one — sitting still with your own substrate and asking it to be quiet is one of the most difficult things a person can attempt. If the cushion defeats you, take the long way around through the dojo. The destination is the same calm mind; you're just allowed to sweat to get there.

### 4.2.3 The Two Causal Roles of the Self-Model

> The Causal Role requirement (§2.7) is discharged in two distinct modes, and distinguishing them forecloses a persistent misreading of the theory's stance on volition. In the *outward* mode, the explicit self-model — a high-dimensional pattern realized in the critical substrate — participates in the causal chain that issues in behaviour; its influence on the world is mediated, distributed, and slow (self-model → substrate → behaviour → environment, over developmental timescales). In the *inward* mode, the running self-simulation is the necessary medium for reality-decoupled processing: attentional redirection, mental imagery, counterfactual simulation, and the simulation-forking and variable-permeability operations of §[forking/permeability]. The inward mode is phenomenologically immediate, and it is tempting to read that immediacy as a locus of libertarian control — the one place the self "freely" acts upon itself. The theory rejects this reading. The initiation of an inward act (the "decision" to disengage and simulate) is substrate-determined and precedes its conscious registration, exactly as for outward action (consistent with the delayed-observer account, §[Libet]); the felt authorship of self-directed retreat is the same retrospective construction as the felt authorship of a limb movement.
>
> What the self-simulation contributes is therefore *necessity*, not freedom. The downstream decoupled cascade cannot occur unless the explicit models are actively running — remove the simulation and there is no internal world to occupy — so the virtual self is a causally necessary link in the substrate's dynamics, not a causally inert by-product. This preserves the theory's position (§[epiphenomenalism/3.10]) that consciousness has no *independent* causal power, while denying that it is epiphenomenal: the running computation is efficacious precisely *as* part of the physical substrate on which it is realized (consistent with compatibilist treatments of mental causation; the FMT-native formulation is that the virtual pattern's causal grip is the substrate's own, expressed most rapidly in the inward decoupling mode). The same analysis dissolves the apparent tension between determinism and self-cultivation: because the explicit models reshape the implicit substrate through learning, exposure-driven change — including conceptual exposure — is causally real without requiring libertarian agency. In its graded, adaptive regime the inward decoupling capacity is, moreover, the computational basis of offline cognition (planning, prospection, imaginative recombination), linking the Causal Role requirement to the motivational-intelligence model (RIM; Appendix [B]).
>
> *Scope caveat.* The substrate-determined-initiation claim concerns the *onset* of inward engagement, not the degree of voluntary control over its *contents*. The capacity at issue is specifically the explicit self-model's manipulation of the explicit *world*-model (ESM-on-EWM) — the extent to which the self-model can reshape the simulated world it is embedded in. This is plausibly a higher-order capacity: it presupposes sufficient recursive depth (the extended/erweitert ladder, §3.5; presence-vs-access, §3.4.6) and is partially trainable (cf. deliberate navigation of the visual processing hierarchy, §6.x). How many degrees of freedom the implicit models grant the ESM for this is currently unquantified; whether — and how far — the system steers its own internal exploration is an open empirical question that would require connectome-scale structural data and large-scale simulation to resolve (cf. §[open questions]; AC-implementation programme). The theory asserts unfree *initiation*, not the absence of content-control.
