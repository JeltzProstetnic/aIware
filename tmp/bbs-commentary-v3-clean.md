## Abstract

Seth identifies the right problem: substrate-independence claims need principled specification. We propose the Four-Model Theory (FMT) as a candidate answer. FMT specifies consciousness as self-referential simulation at dynamical criticality — a functional requirement compatible with Seth's causal-powers framework. The architecture generates testable consequences that competing frameworks do not, addressing Seth's concern that current specifications remain too abstract.

**Self-Referential Simulation as a Candidate Causal Specification**

Seth (2025) frames the central question precisely: what causal architecture must a system instantiate for consciousness? His argument that current AI systems are not conscious is well-taken — not because they lack biological matter, but because they lack the right kind of causal organisation. He allows non-biological consciousness in principle while arguing that current approaches fail to specify the required causal powers. The Four-Model Theory (FMT; Gruber, 2015, 2026) proposes a candidate specification.

FMT identifies consciousness with a self-referential simulation generated by two kinds of models running in parallel — world-directed and self-directed — each with an implicit (learned, non-phenomenal) and an explicit (simulated, phenomenal) mode. The critical architectural property is self-referential closure: the system's model of the world includes a model of itself modelling the world, collapsing the inside/outside distinction that the Hard Problem presupposes. FMT addresses the Hard Problem by transforming it: self-referential closure is constitutive of phenomenal character — a foundational commitment analogous to those every theory of consciousness requires (Seth & Bayne, 2022) — and closure at criticality generates *temporal echoing*, recursive reverberations through implicit model structure that give the inside perspective its phenomenal texture. This narrows the gap from bare assertion to architectural mechanism. The question is whether this mechanism generates better predictions than the alternatives.

Architecture alone is insufficient. FMT imposes a dual threshold: the substrate must also operate at dynamical criticality — the boundary between ordered and disordered dynamics (Wolfram Class 4). This was derived theoretically in 2015 from computational universality requirements, before the empirical consolidation of criticality–consciousness links that followed. Recent work is consistent: Algom and Shriki (2026) review 140+ datasets linking criticality to consciousness; Tucker, Luu and Friston (2025) ground consciousness in excitatory–inhibitory balance at the critical transition; Xu et al. (2024) show sleep restores cortical criticality. These findings do not test FMT specifically, but confirm the dynamical regime FMT identifies as necessary is empirically load-bearing.

A specification earns its keep by generating testable consequences that competing frameworks do not. FMT's architecture does this. Because the boundary between implicit and explicit models has variable permeability — a structural property family governing information transfer from substrate to simulation — disruptions to that permeability produce specific, directional effects. For instance, the architecture predicts that global permeability increases (as occur under psychedelics) should partially compensate for local self-model blockages such as anosognosia — a consequence that follows from the inter-model information flow structure rather than from predictive processing per se. Similarly, because the explicit self-model requires continuous input to maintain coherent content, destabilising that input (via psychedelics, sensory deprivation, or meditation) should make identity content a function of whatever input dominates — ego dissolution becomes controllable rather than random. A fuller catalogue — including predictions for dissociative identity disorder and lucid dream onset — appears in Gruber (2026, Sections 8.2–8.5). The point is not the individual predictions but the architectural property they share: each follows from self-referential structure and the permeability mechanism, not from domain-specific stipulation.

This specification also clarifies the status of current AI systems — Seth's central concern. Large language models fail not because they lack biological matter but because they lack the architecture: no persistent implicit models, no self-referential closure (feedforward processing with no ongoing self-simulation), and no substrate dynamics near criticality. FMT can say precisely *what would need to change*: a system with persistent world- and self-models, self-referential closure between them, and a substrate operating at the Class 4 boundary. This is not "any system computing the right function" — it is a named architecture with named dynamical constraints.

The active inference tradition itself trends in this direction. Laukkonen, Friston and Chandaria's Beautiful Loop (2025) derives self-modelling requirements from active inference — a structurally compatible result, though grounded in uncertainty reduction rather than simulation dynamics. That Friston's own framework generates self-modelling constraints suggests the explanatory work is done by causal structure, not substrate chemistry. Seth's causal-powers framework and FMT's architectural specification may be closer than they initially appear: if "biological causal powers" ultimately reduce to "the causal powers of a self-referential architecture at criticality," the positions converge. Seth might contest this — but the predictions that would distinguish the two readings are the kind of specification both frameworks call for.

Seth is right that the specification problem is the bottleneck. The disagreement is not whether specification matters but what form it takes. FMT offers one candidate — substrate-neutral, architecturally specific, empirically constrained by four untested predictions. Whether it is the right candidate is an empirical question, and the predictions are on the table.
