The move from shared properties to structural identity requires philosophical justification. Leibniz's Identity of Indiscernibles (Leibniz, 1686) provides it: entities that share all discernible properties are not merely similar but identical. The interior of every singularity in the inventory is defined, not merely observed, to be informationally inaccessible. No signal, no measurement, and no physical process can extract a distinguishing property from the far side of an information-impermeable boundary. This is not an observational limitation that future instruments might overcome; it is a constitutive feature of what makes a boundary a singularity in the first place. If no distinguishing property can in principle be observed, then by Leibniz's principle, the interiors are identical until someone demonstrates a property that differentiates them. The burden of proof falls on the critic: anyone who insists that a black hole interior, a Planck-scale interior, and the region beyond the cosmological horizon are different kinds of things must specify what information distinguishes them, and must explain how that information crosses a boundary defined by the impossibility of such crossing.

A natural objection is that singularities plainly differ: black holes have mass, charge, and angular momentum; the Big Bang has cosmological parameters; elementary particles have quantum numbers. But these are properties of the boundary surface as observed from within the computational domain, not properties of the singularity interior. General relativity makes this point with particular clarity at the event horizon. An observer falling through a black hole's event horizon encounters no local physical discontinuity; there is no membrane, no wall, no detectable marker at the boundary. The "surface" is a coordinate artifact, a construct of the exterior observer's description. What we measure as distinct about different singularities, their mass or their cosmological parameters or their quantum numbers, pertains entirely to how the boundary appears from our side. It characterizes the computational domain's interface with the boundary, not what lies beyond it. Once this distinction is drawn, the apparent diversity of singularities dissolves: they differ in their exterior presentation to the domain, not in their interior structure, which by definition admits no characterization at all. The unification claim, then, is not an analogy stretched across disparate phenomena but an application of a standard principle of identity to entities whose interiors are, by construction, indiscernible.
