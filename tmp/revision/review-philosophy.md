# Philosophical Review: The Four-Model Theory of Consciousness

**Reviewer expertise:** Analytic philosophy of mind; the Hard Problem; qualia; theories of consciousness  
**Journal:** Journal of Consciousness Studies  
**Recommendation:** Major revision

---

## Summary

Gruber presents an ambitious unified theory of consciousness built on five principles: (1) a criticality requirement derived from Wolfram's cellular automaton classification, (2) a two-level ontology distinguishing "real" (substrate/implicit) from "virtual" (computational/explicit) properties, (3) four nested models along axes of scope (world/self) and mode (implicit/explicit), (4) variable permeability between implicit and explicit levels, and (5) self-referential closure as the constitutive condition for phenomenality. The theory claims to address all eight of what the author identifies as core requirements for a complete theory of consciousness, including the Hard Problem, which it reframes as a category error -- a level confusion that seeks phenomenal properties at the substrate level where they categorically do not exist. Four novel predictions are offered, alongside convergence evidence from the criticality literature.

The paper is impressively systematic in scope, philosophically engaged beyond what most neuroscientific theories of consciousness attempt, and refreshingly honest about its limitations. However, the philosophical arguments contain several gaps that range from potentially fixable to structurally significant. The central claims about "dissolving" the Hard Problem and the constitutive role of self-referential closure require substantially more rigorous defense than currently provided.

---

## Major Philosophical Concerns

**1. The Hard Problem is relocated, not dissolved.**

The paper's central philosophical claim is that the Hard Problem rests on a "category error" -- seeking phenomenal properties at the substrate level when they exist only at the computational level. This is presented as a dissolution: once we see the level confusion, the problem vanishes.

But this maneuver does not dissolve the Hard Problem; it relocates it. Grant that qualia are "constitutive properties of the computational level." The Hard Problem reformulates immediately: *why does this particular computational-level process -- self-simulation at criticality -- have phenomenal character, when other computational-level processes (weather simulations, chess engines, spreadsheets) do not?* The paper recognizes this objection and offers self-referential closure as the answer. But this simply pushes the question back one step: *why does self-referential closure generate phenomenality?*

The paper is commendably frank that "the commitment is not deducible from physics alone" and that "every theory of consciousness bottoms out in an analogous commitment" (Section 3.4.3). But this concession is in tension with the claim to have *dissolved* the Hard Problem. A dissolved problem is one shown to be ill-formed -- it should not require a brute-fact commitment to answer. What the paper actually offers is a *reframing* of the Hard Problem at a different level of description, combined with a foundational posit (self-referential closure constitutes phenomenality). This is a legitimate theoretical move -- arguably more illuminating than IIT's axiom-based identification or GNW's silence -- but calling it a "dissolution" overstates the achievement. A dissolution would show that there is no residual question; here, there clearly is one.

The analogy to spreadsheets, while pedagogically effective, conceals the gap. "Cell A1 contains the sum of column B" is incoherent at the transistor level, true. But *we understand perfectly well* why the computational level has this property -- we wrote the program, we defined "sum," the mapping is fully transparent. For qualia, no analogous transparent mapping exists. The level distinction obtains, but the *explanatory transparency* that makes the spreadsheet case unproblematic is precisely what is missing in the consciousness case. The paper needs to address this disanalogy directly.

**2. Self-referential closure as constitutive: the argument has a hidden gap.**

The three-stage argument from weather simulation through monitored simulation to self-referentially closed system (Section 3.4.3) is the philosophical heart of the paper. It is well-constructed but contains a non sequitur at the critical juncture.

Stages 1 and 2 convincingly establish that simple computation and self-monitoring are insufficient for phenomenality. Stage 3 then argues that when the system's model includes a model of itself modeling, something qualitatively new emerges: an "inside perspective" that "can only be instantiated, never merely described." The claim is that the inside/outside distinction collapses, creating an asymmetry between internal and external characterization that is "constitutive rather than merely epistemic."

The gap is this: the paper asserts that self-referential closure creates a perspective that "exists only from within the closed loop," but it never demonstrates *why* recursive self-modeling should generate a perspective at all, as opposed to merely generating a very complex data structure. A thermostat that models its own temperature-sensing process, and models *that* modeling, and so on to arbitrary depth -- would this constitute phenomenal experience? The paper would presumably say no (lacking criticality and the four-model architecture), but this reveals that self-referential closure alone is not doing the work the argument claims. The conjunction of closure *plus* criticality *plus* the four-model architecture is what is asserted to be sufficient -- but the philosophical argument focuses almost entirely on closure, leaving the conjunction underargued.

Furthermore, the analogy to a musical score ("reading the code is not having the experience, any more than reading a musical score is hearing the music") is apt for illustrating that descriptions are not instances. But the score/music distinction does not establish that the music has an *inside perspective*. A symphony has no first-person viewpoint. The analogy proves less than the paper needs.

**3. The zombie argument response does not engage Chalmers at the right level.**

The paper argues that a zombie duplicate of a self-referentially closed system is inconceivable because "to conceive of a zombie is to conceive of a self-referential loop that is not self-referential -- a contradiction." This is too quick.

Chalmers' zombie is physically identical *and* functionally identical. It runs the same self-referential computation. The zombie argument asks: could all of this obtain *without there being something it is like*? The paper's response amounts to: "No, because the inside perspective IS the computation." But this is precisely what Chalmers denies is established by any functional/physical argument. The response works only if one already accepts that self-referential closure constitutively generates phenomenality -- which is the very claim at issue.

To put it formally: Chalmers grants that zombies are functionally identical. The paper argues that phenomenality is a functional/computational property (constituted by self-referential closure). Therefore zombies are impossible. But Chalmers' entire point is that phenomenality might not be a functional property. The paper has not shown that it is; it has *posited* that it is. The response to the zombie argument thus begs the question -- it assumes the identity of phenomenality with a computational property in order to argue that phenomenality cannot be absent from that computational property.

A more promising strategy, which the paper gestures toward but does not develop, would be to argue that the conceivability of zombies is an artefact of the level confusion the paper identifies -- that we can *seem* to conceive of zombies only because we mistakenly look for phenomenality at the substrate level and, not finding it there, imagine it could be absent. But this would require a much more detailed engagement with the two-dimensional semantics that undergirds Chalmers' conceivability argument.

**4. The two-level ontology: genuinely novel or relabeled property dualism?**

The paper claims the two-level ontology is a "third position" distinct from physicalism, dualism, and illusionism. But the claim that "both levels are physical" while they have "different ontological properties" is structurally isomorphic to property dualism: one physical substance, two kinds of properties (physical-substrate and computational-experiential), with the latter not reducible to the former.

The paper attempts to distinguish its position by insisting on full supervenience and no strong emergence. But standard property dualism (e.g., Chalmers 1996) also accepts supervenience (at least nomological supervenience) and need not invoke strong emergence in any objectionable sense. The key question is whether the "level-specific" properties are ontologically fundamental or derived. If derived (fully determined by and explicable in terms of the substrate), then we have standard physicalism with a useful level-of-description distinction. If not fully derived -- if there is something about computational-level properties that outruns substrate-level determination -- then we have property dualism, however labeled.

The paper's insistence that "some properties of physical processes are constitutively processual -- they exist in the doing, not in the blueprint" is intriguing but underdeveloped. This sounds like it could be a genuine ontological innovation, but it needs to be distinguished more carefully from (a) Russellian physicalism (physical properties have an intrinsic nature that grounds phenomenality), (b) neutral monism (experience and physical properties are both aspects of a more fundamental reality), and (c) emergentism (higher-level properties are real, novel, and not reducible). The paper mentions none of these positions.

**5. The "constitutive rather than merely correlates" claim for self-referential closure lacks a criterion of adequacy.**

The paper repeatedly asserts that self-referential closure *constitutes* phenomenal experience rather than merely correlating with it. But it provides no criterion by which constitution could be distinguished from correlation. What would it look like for self-referential closure to *merely correlate* with experience? If no observational or conceptual difference between "constitutes" and "correlates" can be specified, the claim is empirically empty -- a philosophical preference, not a theoretical result.

The paper acknowledges that the constitutive claim is "falsifiable" -- if a system achieving self-referential closure at criticality shows no behavioral or functional signatures of experience, the claim is wrong. But behavioral and functional signatures are signatures of *access consciousness*, not necessarily of *phenomenal consciousness*. The paper itself argues (Section 3.4.5) that access and phenomenal consciousness are "two descriptions of the same running process." If this identity is assumed, then the falsification criterion is well-defined. But the identity of access and phenomenal consciousness is itself one of the most contested claims in the philosophy of mind (Block 1995, 2007), and the paper cannot assume it without argument.

**6. Scope claims exceed what the arguments deliver.**

The abstract claims the theory "addresses" all eight requirements. Section 7 gives the theory a full score on every requirement. This self-assessment is overconfident. The treatment of the Hard Problem, as argued above, is a reframing with a brute-fact posit, not a dissolution. The treatment of the Explanatory Gap relies entirely on the success of the level-distinction argument, which has the gaps noted above. The treatment of Combination and Emergence claims "weak emergence, no combination problem" -- but the theory *does* face an analogue of the combination problem: how do non-conscious substrate-level components combine to produce a conscious computational-level process? Saying "they just do, and it's not strong emergence" does not resolve the question; it dismisses it by definitional fiat.

The self-scoring matrix (Table 5) is acknowledged as the author's own judgment but will nevertheless be read as the paper's official position. Giving oneself a perfect score while rating all competitors lower -- even with caveats about the rubric being "theory-laden" -- undermines the paper's credibility. The caveat is welcome but insufficient to counterbalance the visual impression of the table.

## Minor Points

**1.** The operational definition of "consciousness" (Table 1) uses PCI > 0.31 as the observable, then immediately disclaims it as merely a "validated empirical marker, not a constitutive measure." This is methodologically appropriate but creates a practical gap: the theory's constitutive account (self-referential closure at criticality) has no established measurement protocol, as the paper itself acknowledges. The theory thus cannot, at present, empirically distinguish its constitutive account from the correlational measures it criticizes in competitors.

**2.** The Wolfram/Class 4 derivation of the criticality requirement is presented as an independent theoretical achievement. But Wolfram's classification is a taxonomy of dynamical behavior, not a deductive framework. Saying "consciousness requires Class 4 dynamics because self-simulation requires universal computation" is an assertion about what self-simulation computationally requires, not a derivation from Wolfram's framework. The word "derived" is too strong; "motivated" or "inspired" would be more accurate.

**3.** The paper's treatment of the Mary case is philosophically competent but somewhat routine. The claim that Mary gains a "new constitutive state of her self-simulation" is a variant of the Loar/phenomenal concepts strategy combined with the ability hypothesis. The paper would benefit from engaging with the extensive literature on this response -- particularly objections that the phenomenal concepts strategy is either trivially true (of course the concept is new) or question-begging (whether the concept tracks a genuinely new property is the very issue).

**4.** The distinction between FMT and Frankish's weak illusionism (Section 3.4.4 and 4.2) is well-drawn and philosophically important. The paper correctly identifies that the practical/ethical consequences diverge even if the empirical predictions do not. However, the paper concedes that "whether this disagreement is substantively different from weak illusionism or merely a relabeling is a live philosophical question that cannot be settled by empirical observation from outside the system." This concession is potentially fatal for the claim to have moved beyond illusionism -- if the disagreement cannot be settled empirically, it is a metaphysical preference, not a theoretical advance.

**5.** The dual evaluation architecture (Section 4.2.1) -- where the substrate "deploys" the simulation as an evaluation mechanism and the simulation feeds back to reshape the implicit models -- is an interesting account of the causal role. But the clock analogy ("the hands and numerals of a clock have no direct mechanical relation to the gear train, yet the clock cannot function as a clock without them") actually undermines the paper's anti-epiphenomenalist stance. Clock hands genuinely *are* epiphenomenal with respect to the gears -- they display but do not cause. If this is the model for qualia's causal role, then qualia are epiphenomenal in exactly the sense the paper wishes to deny. The paper needs a different analogy or a clearer account of how the feedback loop gives qualia genuine causal relevance rather than display-only status.

**6.** The five-system hierarchy (Section 3.7.1) asserts that "each level is fully physical and fully determined by the level below it; there is no strong emergence." If this is so, then the claim that Level 5 properties are "incoherent at the substrate level" must mean *descriptively* incoherent, not *ontologically* incoherent. But the paper elsewhere uses "ontologically level-specific" (Section 3.4.1), which sounds like ontological incoherence. The paper oscillates between epistemological and ontological readings of the level distinction without clearly committing to one.

**7.** The cognitive-learning argument (Section 4.2.3) -- that consciousness enables learning from observed deaths -- is a plausible just-so story about the adaptive value of consciousness, but it does not establish that consciousness (phenomenal experience) is *necessary* for this capacity. A sophisticated information-processing system could, in principle, extract the relevant information from observing another agent's demise without experiencing anything. The argument conflates the functional architecture (which enables the learning) with the phenomenal properties (which the paper claims are constitutive of that architecture). But "constitutive" is the claim at issue, not an established premise.

**8.** Section 3.2 ("The Four Models as a principled minimum") correctly acknowledges that the brain does not implement four discrete models. But the paper then needs to explain why the *theory* is called "Four-Model Theory" if the four models are merely "extremal points in a continuous space." If the models are not discrete, what work does the number four do? Is the claim that there are exactly two axes (scope and mode), or that there are at least two? Could there be a third axis (e.g., temporal: present/predictive)?

**9.** The paper's engagement with predictive processing (PP) is somewhat superficial. It claims PP is "explicitly silent on the Hard Problem" (citing Seth 2021), but Seth's position is more nuanced -- he argues for dissolving the Hard Problem by addressing the "real problem" of consciousness, which bears structural similarity to FMT's own dissolution strategy. A more detailed engagement with Seth's "controlled hallucination" framework, which shares significant architectural territory with FMT (self-models, prediction error, hierarchy), would strengthen the comparative analysis.

## Constructive Suggestions

**1.** Reframe the Hard Problem claim. Instead of claiming to "dissolve" it, claim to have *identified the architectural conditions under which the Hard Problem does not arise as a separate question*. This is more defensible and still philosophically significant. The paper can argue that the level distinction *transforms* the Hard Problem from a deep metaphysical mystery into a specific question about the relationship between computational levels -- a question that is difficult but not intractable. This framing would also be more honest about the residual brute-fact commitment regarding self-referential closure.

**2.** Engage directly with the conceivability argument's two-dimensional semantics. The zombie response needs to grapple with Chalmers' distinction between primary and secondary conceivability and between conceivability and metaphysical possibility. Simply asserting that zombies are "contradictions" will not convince anyone who has read *The Conscious Mind*.

**3.** Explicitly locate the theory in relation to Russellian physicalism, neutral monism, and property dualism. The two-level ontology occupies a distinctive position in logical space, but the paper does not map that space. An explicit positioning statement -- "FMT is not property dualism because X, not Russellian physicalism because Y, not neutral monism because Z" -- would clarify the theory's philosophical commitments and forestall obvious objections.

**4.** Develop the "constitutively processual" notion more rigorously. This is potentially the paper's most original philosophical contribution -- the idea that some physical properties exist only in instantiation, not in description, without this constituting strong emergence. But it needs to be distinguished from (a) token identity theory (every token experience is identical to a token physical process), (b) realization theory (computational properties are realized by but not identical to physical properties), and (c) dispositionalism (properties are powers). The concept currently floats free of the existing metaphysical literature.

**5.** Replace the clock analogy with one that better serves the anti-epiphenomenalist aim. Consider instead a feedback-control system where the display is also the sensor: e.g., a thermostat whose display *is read by the system itself* to calibrate further action. This would better capture the self-referential closure that the paper argues distinguishes consciousness from mere display.

**6.** Moderate the scoring matrix. Either add a column for limitations/caveats alongside each rating, or change the FMT column to include honest "partial" ratings where the treatment is genuinely weaker than "addresses" (Hard Problem, Explanatory Gap, Combination/Emergence). This would significantly improve the paper's credibility with philosophically trained reviewers.

**7.** Address the "is this just functionalism with a relabeled Hard-Problem response?" objection head-on. The paper claims to go beyond functionalism by adding "ontologically level-specific" properties. But if these properties fully supervene on the physical substrate and involve no strong emergence, a functionalist might reasonably reply: "That is just what I meant all along -- functional properties are real, level-specific, and not usefully described in substrate vocabulary. You have added terminology, not ontology." The paper needs a clear argument for why its position is ontologically distinct from sophisticated functionalism (e.g., Dennett's "real patterns" or Lycan's homuncular functionalism).
