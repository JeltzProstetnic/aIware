### Zombies

**Replace lines 416 (the existing zombie paragraph) with:**

**Zombies** (Chalmers, 1996). Chalmers argues that a physical duplicate of a conscious being — identical in every physical respect — is *conceivable* without phenomenal experience, and that this conceivability entails the metaphysical possibility of consciousness being non-physical. FMT's response targets the conceivability premise directly. For any system that achieves self-referential closure (Section 3.4.3), phenomenal character is not an accompaniment of the computation but *constitutive* of it — it is what the self-referential loop IS from the only perspective available once closure obtains. A zombie duplicate of such a system would have to be computationally identical — running the same four-model architecture at criticality, with the same self-referential closure — yet lack an inside perspective. But the inside perspective is not a further fact about the system; it is the self-referential computation itself, characterized from within. To conceive of a zombie is therefore to conceive of a self-referential loop that is not self-referential — a contradiction, not a genuine possibility. Chalmers' conceivability argument gains its intuitive force from treating consciousness as something *added to* physical processing — an extra ingredient that could, in principle, be subtracted while leaving the processing intact. Once consciousness is understood as constitutive of a specific kind of processing (self-simulation at closure), there is no ingredient to subtract. The system either runs the self-referential computation or it does not; if it does, the inside perspective is constituted; if it does not, it is not a physical duplicate. This does not beg the question against dualism — it identifies the specific architectural feature (self-referential closure) that makes the conceivability intuition inapplicable. The argument is falsifiable: if a system demonstrably achieving self-referential closure at criticality shows no behavioral or functional signatures of experience, the constitutive claim is wrong.

### Mary's Room

**Replace lines 418 (the existing knowledge argument paragraph) with:**

**The knowledge argument** (Jackson, 1982). Mary, confined to a black-and-white room, knows every physical fact about color vision but has never experienced red. Upon seeing red for the first time, does she learn something new? Under FMT, yes — but what she learns is not a non-physical fact. She gains new *virtual-level* representations: her explicit self-model (ESM) acquires entries for "what red looks like to me" that could not exist without the actual sensory input driving the self-simulation. Mary's pre-release knowledge was complete at the substrate level — she knew every physical fact about wavelength processing, cone activation, and neural coding. What she lacked was the processual property (Section 3.4.4): the ESM state that constitutes experiencing red, which exists only when the self-referential computation runs with chromatic input. The knowledge gap is real but not metaphysically mysterious — it is the difference between a self-model that includes the experience and one that does not, which is in turn the difference between a computation that has run and one that has been described but not instantiated. This position is closer to the "ability hypothesis" (Lewis, 1988; Nemirow, 1990) — Mary gains a new ability (to recognize, imagine, and remember red from the first-person perspective) — but grounded architecturally rather than dispositionally. She does not merely gain know-how; she gains a new constitutive state of her self-simulation. The distinction matters: the ability hypothesis is often criticized for reducing phenomenal knowledge to mere skill, but FMT's version says that the new ability is underwritten by a new *ontological state* at the virtual level — a genuine property of the running computation that did not exist before, even though no new physics was added. No independent causal power is required; no non-physical fact is discovered. The physical picture was always complete — but completeness of description is not completeness of instantiation.

### Frankish Illusionism

**Add as a new paragraph after the Mary's Room paragraph in §4.2.4:**

**Illusionism** (Frankish, 2016). The proximity between FMT and Frankish's weak illusionism deserves explicit treatment, because the two positions share significant territory while diverging at a critical juncture. Both deny that phenomenal properties exist at the substrate level — on this, they fully agree. Frankish's weak illusionism grants that the functional states driving introspective reports are real computational events, but holds that these states *systematically misrepresent themselves* as having phenomenal character; phenomenality itself is the illusion, and "quasi-phenomenal" properties are the end of the story. FMT makes the opposite ontological move. It holds that phenomenal properties *genuinely exist* at the computational level, constituted by the self-referential simulation (Section 3.4.3) rather than illusorily attributed by introspective misrepresentation. The disagreement is not verbal but has concrete consequences. On Frankish's account, there is ultimately nothing it is like to be a system — the seeming exhausts the reality. On FMT's account, there genuinely IS something it is like, but it exists at the virtual level where substrate-focused inquiry cannot find it. The theories agree on the negative claim (no substrate-level qualia) and disagree on the positive claim (whether computational-level phenomenality is real or merely represented as real). This places FMT in a specific position in the logical space: closer to illusionism than to the strong qualia realism of Chalmers or IIT's intrinsic causal power, but affirming the reality of phenomenal properties as level-relative — real at their level of description, absent at lower levels. The practical consequence is non-trivial: if FMT is correct, a system built to its architectural specification would be a genuine moral patient. Weak illusionism generates no such obligation, because on its account there is no fact of the matter about whether the system "really" experiences. The same physics, the same behavior, but different ethics — a divergence that will cease to be academic as artificial systems approach the architectural complexity the theory specifies.

This objection conflates intellectual ancestry with theoretical identity. The novel contribution is not any single ingredient but the specific integration: a two-level ontology (real/virtual) that addresses the Hard Problem without panpsychism or strong emergence; a criticality requirement derived from computational first principles a decade before its empirical confirmation; variable implicit-explicit permeability as a single-parameter mechanism unifying altered states; and a graduated consciousness hierarchy via recursive self-modeling depth. ## C. §9 OQ4 — ESM/EWM Double Dissociation

**Replace OQ4 (currently lines 719) with the following expanded text:**

**4. ESM/EWM double dissociation: the sharpest architectural test.** The four-model architecture makes a structural prediction that distinguishes it from its closest competitor: the explicit self-model (ESM) and explicit world model (EWM) should be *independently disruptable*. FMT treats these as architecturally distinct model kinds — one organized around self-referential content (body ownership, autobiographical narrative, agency), the other around world-representational content (scene construction, object permanence, spatial navigation). If this is correct, it should be possible to selectively degrade the ESM while leaving the EWM largely intact, and vice versa.

This prediction directly contrasts with predictive processing (PP), which treats the self-model as one component of a unified hierarchical generative model. Under PP, disrupting self-model precision should propagate through the hierarchy and degrade world-model performance commensurately — the two should *covary*. FMT predicts the opposite: because the ESM and EWM draw on partially overlapping but architecturally separable substrate resources, their disruption should be *orthogonal*. Selective ESM disruption should produce depersonalization (intact world, absent self); selective EWM disruption should produce something closer to dream-like or hallucinatory states (fragmented world, intact self-awareness). Existing clinical evidence is suggestive — depersonalization-derealization disorder shows partial dissociation — but the clean double dissociation has not been experimentally demonstrated.

Candidate approaches include: (i) targeted transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) to medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and temporoparietal junction (TPJ), which are implicated in self-referential processing, versus posterior parietal and occipitotemporal cortex, which subserve scene construction and spatial representation; (ii) pharmacological agents with differential self/world effects — ketamine at sub-anesthetic doses disrupts self-referential processing disproportionately, while certain anticholinergic agents (e.g., scopolamine) preferentially degrade world-model coherence; (iii) comparison of neurological patients with selective lesions (anosognosia as partial ESM failure vs. visual form agnosia as partial EWM failure). The prediction is formulated in terms of functional dissociation rather than anatomical localization, to avoid the naive-modularism pitfall of assigning named brain networks to theoretical constructs.

This double dissociation is not promoted to a formal prediction because no specific experimental paradigm has been designed and validated. However, it represents FMT's most distinctive architectural commitment: if ESM and EWM disruption proves to be consistently correlated rather than orthogonal across multiple manipulation methods, the four-model architecture's central structural claim would be undermined. The question is therefore highlighted as a priority for future experimental investigation.

## E. §3.7 Criticality Neural Signature

**Current text (lines 336)** already commits to three convergent signatures. The relevant paragraph begins: "**Concrete neural signatures.** The criticality requirement translates to three convergent measurable signatures..."

The current commitment is actually stronger than the task requires — it already identifies three measures (branching ratio sigma near 1, DFA exponent alpha 0.6-0.9, power-law avalanche exponent ~-3/2) and explicitly commits: "The theory commits to these as the operational signatures of its criticality requirement."

**What is missing:** (1) An explicit statement of which *single* signature is primary, since the task asks to "commit to ONE concrete neural signature." (2) An explicit distinction between PCI and criticality.

**Proposed insertion after the existing "Concrete neural signatures" paragraph (after line 336):**

**Primary commitment and the PCI distinction.** Of the three convergent signatures, the theory designates the **DFA exponent alpha** as its primary operational measure. The rationale is methodological: alpha is computable from standard EEG/MEG recordings without requiring spike-level resolution (unlike branching ratio sigma, which in its strictest form requires single-neuron recordings) and without the distributional ambiguities that plague power-law fitting in finite neural datasets (Clauset, Shalizi, & Newman, 2009). DFA alpha in the range 0.6-0.9 indexes long-range temporal correlations — the signature of a system whose current state carries information about its distant past, which is precisely the computational property required for coherent self-simulation across time. A system at alpha = 0.5 (uncorrelated noise) cannot sustain a stable self-model; a system at alpha > 1.0 (non-stationary drift) cannot flexibly update one. The waking cortex operates in the intermediate regime that balances stability with flexibility — the computational sweet spot for ongoing self-referential modeling.

A clarification on the Perturbational Complexity Index (PCI; Casali et al., 2013) is warranted, as PCI is sometimes conflated with criticality measures. PCI quantifies the algorithmic complexity of the cortical response to a TMS perturbation — it measures how much information the brain generates in response to a standardized kick. PCI reliably discriminates conscious from unconscious states (threshold ~0.31) and is the best-validated clinical consciousness measure available. However, PCI is not a criticality measure. A system can have high PCI without being at criticality (if it generates complex but non-critical responses), and a system at criticality could in principle have low PCI (if the perturbation does not propagate through the critical network effectively). PCI *correlates* with criticality because critical systems tend to generate complex, far-propagating responses to perturbation, but the correlation is empirical, not definitional. The theory's criticality requirement is satisfied by the convergence of sigma, alpha, and avalanche scaling — not by PCI. PCI serves as a *clinical proxy* for consciousness level, which the theory predicts tracks criticality, but the proxy should not be confused with the thing it proxies. This distinction matters for artificial substrates: an artificial system operating at criticality would satisfy the theory's computational threshold regardless of how it responds to TMS-like perturbation.


## F. §3.7.2 Phosphenes

**Current text (lines 356):**

> This framing yields a concrete observational claim: the cortical automaton's activity is, in principle, directly observable. In a dark, quiet environment with eyes closed, after retinal afterimages have faded, the faint flickering patterns visible against the dark field are not retinal noise but V1-level manifestations of the cortical automaton's ongoing activity — the lowest layer of the implicit-to-explicit permeability becoming momentarily accessible. The progression from these simple phosphenes to the geometric patterns and eventually hypnagogic imagery observed during sleep onset represents progressively higher cortical areas being recruited into the observable dynamics, consistent with the hierarchical permeability account developed in Section 6.1.

**Recommendation: Heavily qualify, not delete.** The paragraph serves a useful purpose — it connects the abstract cortical automaton concept to something experientially accessible. But the unqualified claim "are not retinal noise but V1-level manifestations of the cortical automaton" will provoke immediate objection from any vision scientist.

**Proposed replacement:**

This framing suggests a concrete observational hypothesis. In a dark, quiet environment with eyes closed, after retinal afterimages have faded, faint flickering patterns are visible against the dark field. These "dark noise" phosphenes have multiple well-characterized sources: spontaneous retinal ganglion cell firing (Baylor, Lamb, & Yau, 1979), intrinsic retinal noise from thermal isomerization of rhodopsin, and spontaneous cortical activity in V1 and higher visual areas (Heitmann & Bhatt, 2022). The relative contribution of retinal versus cortical sources remains an active research question. The theory's hypothesis is that the cortical component of these phosphenes — to the extent it can be isolated from retinal contributions — represents the lowest layer of implicit-to-explicit permeability: spontaneous dynamics of the cortical automaton leaking through to the virtual level. This is consistent with the observation that the patterns increase in complexity during sleep onset, progressing from simple flickering to geometric forms and eventually hypnagogic imagery, a progression that parallels the hierarchical permeability account developed in Section 6.1 and that is difficult to explain by retinal noise alone. However, this remains a hypothesis rather than a confirmed prediction, and the cortical contribution to closed-eye phosphenes has not been cleanly separated from retinal sources in the relevant experimental paradigms. The claim is offered as an illustration of how the cortical automaton concept connects to accessible phenomenology, not as established fact.
