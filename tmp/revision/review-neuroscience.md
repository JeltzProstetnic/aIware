# Peer Review: "The Four-Model Theory of Consciousness"

**Reviewer recommendation: Reject with encouragement to revise and resubmit to a more appropriate venue**

---

## Summary

The manuscript presents the "Four-Model Theory" (FMT) of consciousness, proposing that consciousness is constituted by ongoing self-simulation across four nested models (Implicit/Explicit x World/Self) operating at criticality (Wolfram Class 4 dynamics). The paper claims to address all eight "core requirements" a theory of consciousness must meet, derives four novel predictions, and reports converging empirical support for several of its 2015-era commitments. The ambition is considerable, and the paper is clearly written. However, the manuscript suffers from fundamental problems in empirical grounding, theoretical precision, and engagement with the neuroscience literature that preclude publication in its current form.

---

## Major Concerns

**1. The eight-requirement rubric is designed to favor the author's theory.**

The entire comparative analysis (Section 7, Table 5) rests on a rubric of eight philosophical requirements chosen by the author. As the manuscript itself acknowledges (line ~871), "the selection of these eight rather than others is inevitably theory-laden: a rubric that included mathematical formalization, quantitative neural predictions, or specific mechanism identification would rank IIT, Predictive Processing, and GNW considerably higher." This is a disqualifying methodological concern for a paper whose central claim is that FMT outperforms all competitors. The paper essentially constructs the exam, takes it, and declares victory. A neutral comparison would require a rubric established by independent consensus or, at minimum, would include dimensions on which FMT is weak (formalization, empirical track record, quantitative precision). The footnote acknowledging this does not remedy the structural bias; it merely concedes it while proceeding as though it doesn't matter.

**2. The "dissolution" of the Hard Problem is a relabeling, not a solution.**

The paper's central philosophical claim (Section 3.4) is that qualia are "constitutive properties of the computational level" and that the Hard Problem rests on a "category error." This is presented as a novel third position between illusionism and qualia realism. However, the argument amounts to: (a) define two levels (substrate and computation), (b) assert that qualia exist at the computational level, (c) declare that asking why the substrate has qualia is a category error. This does not explain why computational-level properties of self-referential systems are phenomenal while computational-level properties of weather simulations are not. The paper attempts to bridge this gap with "self-referential closure" (Section 3.4.3), but the argument there is ultimately an assertion: "self-referential closure at criticality is constitutive of phenomenality" (line ~423). The paper explicitly acknowledges this is "not deducible from physics alone" and that "every theory of consciousness bottoms out in an analogous commitment." But if the foundational commitment is simply that self-referential closure produces phenomenality, we have not dissolved the Hard Problem; we have relocated it. Why does self-referential closure produce an "inside perspective"? The paper offers an architectural argument (the system cannot be fully described from outside without entering the loop), but this is an epistemic claim about describability, not an ontological explanation of phenomenality. The gap between "external description cannot capture the perspective" and "therefore there IS a perspective" remains unbridged.

**3. The criticality requirement is underdetermined and shared with multiple frameworks.**

The paper repeatedly emphasizes that the criticality requirement was "derived from Wolfram's computational framework in 2015, independently of the empirical criticality program." However:

(a) The derivation itself is underspecified. Saying consciousness requires "Class 4 dynamics" because self-simulation requires complex computation is not a derivation --- it is a plausibility argument. Any theorist could have said "consciousness probably needs complex dynamics" without citing Wolfram. The paper does not demonstrate that Class 4 dynamics (as opposed to, say, Class 3 with appropriate structure) are specifically necessary for self-simulation.

(b) Wolfram's classification was defined for 1D elementary cellular automata. The paper acknowledges this (line ~530) and argues the classification "generalizes" to the brain. But the generalization is hand-waved. The paper asserts neurons are "discrete processing units" in a "topological lattice" with "effectively synchronous" updates --- none of which accurately describes cortical dynamics. Neurons have continuous membrane potentials, graded synaptic transmission, heterogeneous time constants spanning milliseconds to hours, and asynchronous firing. The cortex is not a cellular automaton in any formal sense. The behavioral analogy (the cortex *behaves like* a Class 4 CA) is defensible but much weaker than the formal claim the paper appears to make.

(c) The convergence evidence is shared with REBUS, ConCrit, the Entropic Brain Hypothesis, and multiple other criticality-based approaches. The paper acknowledges this repeatedly (to its credit), but this acknowledgment undermines the evidential weight of Section 8.1. If every criticality-based framework is equally supported by the same evidence, the evidence does not discriminate between them.

**4. The four-model taxonomy lacks empirical grounding and is not clearly distinguishable from existing frameworks.**

The 2x2 taxonomy (Implicit/Explicit x World/Self) is presented as the "minimum sufficient set" for consciousness (line ~303). However:

(a) No empirical evidence is presented for *why* this particular decomposition (and not, say, a 3-way or 5-way decomposition, or a continuous space without discrete model kinds) is the correct one. The claim that "four is the floor" is asserted, not derived. Why couldn't a system with an Implicit World Model, an Explicit World Model, and an Explicit Self Model (but no Implicit Self Model) be conscious? The paper provides no principled argument for why all four quadrants must be populated.

(b) The paper itself acknowledges that "real neural models do not respect the world/self boundary --- they blend across it" (line ~303) and that the four models are "extremal points in a continuous space." If the biological reality is a continuous blended space, what explanatory work does the discrete 2x2 taxonomy actually do? It appears to be a pedagogical convenience rather than a falsifiable architectural claim.

(c) The distinction between FMT's four models and the existing decompositions in Damasio (proto-self, core self, autobiographical self), Metzinger (world-model, self-model, transparent phenomenal model), or predictive processing (generative model with hierarchical priors including self-priors) is never clearly articulated. What does the 2x2 decomposition predict that these frameworks cannot?

**5. The predictions are either untestable, shared with competitors, or rest on questionable assumptions.**

*Prediction 1 (psychedelics alleviate anosognosia):* This is the most interesting prediction but rests on the assumption that "permeability" is a single global parameter that interacts additively with local blocks. There is no evidence that serotonergic psychedelics (which act primarily on 5-HT2A receptors in cortical layer V) would interact with the mechanisms underlying anosognosia (which involve right-hemisphere lesions disrupting interoceptive/proprioceptive integration). The pharmacology does not obviously support the proposed mechanism. Psychedelics could easily increase neural entropy globally without compensating for lesion-specific processing failures. The prediction is testable in principle, but the proposed mechanism is not well grounded in neuropharmacology.

*Prediction 2 (ego dissolution content tracks sensory input):* This is presented as the theory's "most distinctive empirical prediction." However, any embodied/enactive account of consciousness would make a similar prediction: if the self-model dissolves, what remains is sensory coupling with the environment, so of course experience will track environmental input. The predictive processing framework (REBUS) would predict exactly the same thing: when self-priors are relaxed, bottom-up sensory signals dominate. The claim that "no competing theory generates this prediction" (line ~1004) is false. Furthermore, the primary evidence cited is salvia divinorum phenomenology, which is anecdotal and based on self-report under extreme dissociation --- hardly the gold standard.

*Prediction 3 (DID alters show self-referential network specificity):* This prediction assumes that DID is a genuine neurological condition with distinct neural substrates per alter, which remains controversial. The sociocognitive model of DID (Lilienfeld et al., 1999; Lynn et al., 2012) holds that alters are role enactments, not distinct neural configurations. The paper cites Reinders et al. (2003, 2008) and Schlumpf et al. (2014) but does not engage with the sociocognitive critique. If DID alters are partly or fully socially constructed, the prediction tests DID phenomenology, not FMT architecture.

*Prediction 4 (lucid dream onset as criticality threshold):* The paper itself acknowledges the prediction "depends on methodological advances in criticality measurement during sleep that are underway but not yet mature" (line ~1026). Existing evidence from Voss et al. (2014) shows gradual gamma-power increases, not phase-transition dynamics. The prediction is unfalsifiable with current methods and is therefore not currently a scientific prediction.

**6. Overclaiming of independent derivation and temporal priority.**

The paper repeatedly emphasizes that the criticality requirement was "derived theoretically, from the computational properties needed for ongoing self-modeling, not from empirical neuroscience" (line ~502) and that this occurred "in 2015, a decade before the empirical confirmation." Several problems:

(a) Beggs & Plenz (2003) established neural criticality a full 12 years before the 2015 publication. The Entropic Brain Hypothesis (Carhart-Harris, 2014) predated it by a year. The idea that consciousness tracks criticality was already in the air. "Independent derivation via Wolfram" is a stronger claim than the evidence supports --- Wolfram (2002) was widely read, and the jump from "Class 4 dynamics are computationally interesting" to "consciousness needs Class 4 dynamics" is small enough that it could have been influenced by the existing criticality literature even if not cited.

(b) The "2015" publication is a self-published German-language monograph (Gruber, 2015), not a peer-reviewed journal article. Claiming temporal priority over peer-reviewed empirical work based on a self-published book raises significant concerns about the evidentiary standard being applied.

**7. The "virtual qualia" account faces the same problems as standard functionalism, despite claims to the contrary.**

Section 3.4.4 claims FMT departs from standard functionalism by adding "ontologically level-specific" properties. The paper draws a distinction between functionalism (where computational-level properties are "nothing more than" what the substrate does under a functional description) and FMT (where computational-level properties are "constitutively processual" and exist "in the running computation, not in its description"). However, the paper also insists on "full supervenience" and that "nothing is missing from the physical picture" (line ~437). If nothing is missing from the physical picture and the system fully supervenes on its substrate, then the "constitutively processual" claim reduces to: you have to run the computation to have the computation. This is trivially true of every computation. The paper's attempt to distinguish this from functionalism rests on the analogy to musical scores (reading a score is not hearing the music), but this analogy undermines the position: a recording of music is also not a performance, yet we don't attribute "ontologically level-specific" properties to live musical performances that recordings lack. The distinction between FMT and sophisticated functionalism appears verbal rather than substantive.

**8. Missing engagement with critical recent literature (2020-2026).**

The paper cites extensively but has significant gaps:

- **No engagement with the Adversarial IIT results beyond COGITATE.** The COGITATE results are described, but the specific findings of Ferrante et al. (2023, Nature Human Behaviour) and the ongoing IIT-GNW adversarial replications are not discussed.

- **No engagement with Doerig et al. (2019)'s "unfolding argument"** as applied to the FMT's own framework. The unfolding argument targets any theory that identifies consciousness with computational properties that can be "unfolded" into feedforward equivalents. Does FMT escape this argument? The paper mentions Doerig (2019) once in passing (line ~908) but never addresses whether the argument applies to FMT itself.

- **No engagement with recent no-report paradigm findings** (Pitts et al., 2014; Frässle et al., 2014; Tsuchiya et al., 2015 is cited but only in passing). These paradigms are directly relevant to whether the NCC identified in the paper track consciousness or merely report.

- **No engagement with the dendritic integration theory** beyond a single citation (Aru, Suzuki, & Larkum 2020). The apical dendrite hypothesis of consciousness has developed significantly since 2020 (Suzuki & Larkum 2023; Phillips 2024) and provides a specific neural mechanism for the "Level 4 to 5 transition" that FMT leaves unspecified.

- **No engagement with Safron's (2020) "Integrated World Modeling Theory"** (IWMT), which combines active inference, IIT, and GNW with a self-modeling framework in ways that overlap substantially with FMT's architecture. IWMT also proposes a world-model/self-model decomposition grounded in predictive processing. The failure to cite or distinguish from IWMT is a significant omission.

- **No engagement with Northoff's (2018, 2020) "Temporo-Spatial Theory of Consciousness"** (TTC), which specifically links consciousness to spatiotemporal dynamics and their relation to self-referential processing --- directly relevant to FMT's claims about criticality and self-modeling.

- **No engagement with Solms' (2021) "The Hidden Spring"** and the brainstem/subcortical consciousness literature. FMT's cortical focus (cortical automaton, cortical columns) is at odds with growing evidence that consciousness has significant subcortical contributions. How does FMT account for the extensive evidence that decorticate animals and anencephalic infants show signs of consciousness?

**9. The "dual evaluation architecture" (Section 4.2) is not clearly distinguished from epiphenomenalism.**

The clock analogy (line ~611) --- "nowhere does the virtual interaction between pointer and numeral cause anything mechanical, yet without that interaction the clock cannot be said to function" --- is exactly what an epiphenomenalist would say. The paper attempts to distinguish this from epiphenomenalism by invoking "feedback" between explicit and implicit models, but this feedback operates via the substrate (learning, synaptic plasticity), not via qualia themselves. The paper concedes that "qualia lack independent causal power over the substrate" (line ~1121). If qualia are causally inert and their effects are fully explained by the substrate-level processes that constitute them, the position is functionally indistinguishable from epiphenomenalism regardless of the "constitutive" framing. The Byczynski et al. (2025) citation (line ~615) does not help: it demonstrates that mental imagery primes perception, but this is a substrate-level causal chain (imagery-generating neural activity causes perceptual priming), not evidence for qualia having causal power.

**10. The permeability construct is too flexible and risks unfalsifiability.**

"Permeability" is invoked to explain:
- Psychedelic phenomenology (global increase)
- Anosognosia (local decrease)
- Pre-sleep imagery (gradual increase)
- Meditation (trained modulation)
- Dream states (state-dependent variation)
- Closed-eye phosphenes (minimal baseline leak)
- The Meta-Problem (near-opacity with occasional leaks)

When a single construct can be adjusted (locally/globally, increased/decreased, gradual/sudden) to explain virtually any phenomenological observation, it has the hallmarks of an unfalsifiable framework. The paper acknowledges that "a formal model... does not yet exist" for mapping permeability to measurable neural quantities (line ~483). Without formal specification of what permeability IS at the neural level, how it is measured, and what its boundary conditions are, it functions as a free parameter that can be tuned post hoc to accommodate any observation.

## Minor Concerns

**1.** The abstract is 398 words. Most neuroscience journals require 150-250. At its current length, it reads as a mini-paper rather than an abstract.

**2.** The claim that FMT "addresses all eight requirements" (abstract, Section 11) is not warranted even on the paper's own terms. The "Combination and Emergence" requirement is "addressed" by saying FMT avoids the problem (it doesn't have micro-experiences to combine). Avoidance is not the same as addressing. IIT could equally claim to "address" substrate independence by rejecting it.

**3.** Section 3.5 (graduated consciousness levels) introduces basic, simply extended, doubly extended, and triply extended consciousness. No empirical criteria are provided for distinguishing these levels in any organism. How would one determine whether a corvid has "simply extended" vs. "doubly extended" consciousness? Without operational criteria, this is taxonomy without content.

**4.** The 40 Hz / 20 Hz temporal dynamics claim (line ~625) is presented as empirical fact but is a substantial oversimplification. Cortical oscillations span a continuous spectrum; the "40 Hz unconscious / 20 Hz conscious" dichotomy is not supported by the cited references (Llinas 1993; Engel 2001), which describe gamma oscillations as a correlate of conscious binding, not a substrate-level processing rate.

**5.** The Libet reinterpretation (line ~625) --- that the ESM "simply runs on a slower clock that receives all its inputs with the same latency" --- contradicts known facts about sensory processing latencies, which vary dramatically across modalities (auditory ~10ms, visual ~50-100ms, tactile ~20-40ms). The "uniform lag" claim is empirically false.

**6.** The evolutionary argument for consciousness via "cognitive learning" (Section 4.2.3) conflates consciousness with the capacity for observational learning. Observational learning exists in many species and can be modeled without phenomenal consciousness (e.g., model-based reinforcement learning in AI). The claim that "unconscious processing --- however sophisticated --- cannot replicate" cognitive learning (line ~633) is an assertion without supporting argument.

**7.** Table 1 (Operational Definitions) lists PCI > 0.31 as the observable for consciousness. PCI was developed by Casali et al. (2013) as a *clinical tool*, not as a definition of consciousness. Using it as the operational definition of your theoretical construct creates a circular dependency: PCI was validated against behavioral consciousness assessments, so defining consciousness by PCI effectively reduces FMT's definition to behavioral responsiveness.

**8.** The paper length (~16,000+ words of body text, excluding references) far exceeds the standard for most neuroscience journals (typically 5,000-8,000 words for theoretical papers).

**9.** The zombie argument response (line ~639) claims that a zombie of a self-referentially closed system is "a self-referential loop that is not self-referential --- a contradiction." But this is question-begging: it assumes that self-referential computation IS phenomenal consciousness. The zombie thought experiment challenges precisely this identification. Asserting the identification and then declaring the zombie inconceivable is circular.

**10.** The "five-system hierarchy" (Section 3.7.1) is presented without justification for why exactly five levels, and not four or six. Why is the proteomic system a separate level from the electrochemical system? These are intertwined at every timescale. The hierarchy appears arbitrary.

**11.** The paper attributes the "cortical automaton" concept to the biological brain but never addresses seizures. If the cortex is a cellular automaton at criticality, and epileptic seizures represent supercritical cascades, the theory should predict that seizures produce *altered* consciousness (hypercritical dynamics), not absence. Yet many seizure types produce unconsciousness. This is not discussed.

**12.** The holographic storage analogy (Section 5.2) --- "cutting a hologram in half produces two complete but lower-resolution images" --- is physically incorrect as applied to neural networks. Neural networks do not have the interference-pattern redundancy of optical holograms. The analogy is potentially misleading.

## Questions for Authors

1. You claim the Hard Problem is "dissolved" by revealing a category error. But the category error argument works only if we accept that computational-level properties of self-referentially closed systems are phenomenal. What evidence could possibly distinguish a self-referentially closed system that has genuine phenomenality from one that merely processes information about itself without any "inside perspective"? If no such evidence exists, is the claim empirically meaningful?

2. How does FMT account for subcortical contributions to consciousness? The cortical automaton framework appears to exclude thalamic, brainstem, and cerebellar contributions that are increasingly recognized as important (Solms, 2021; Parvizi & Damasio, 2001). Can the theory accommodate the evidence that decorticate animals show behavioral signs of consciousness?

3. The paper claims consciousness requires both criticality AND the four-model architecture (Section 3.7.3). What would constitute evidence that criticality alone (without four-model architecture) is sufficient? And what would constitute evidence that the four-model architecture (without criticality) is sufficient? Without being able to test these independently, the conjunctive claim is untestable.

4. You acknowledge that "mathematical formalization" is absent and is a "priority for future work." Given that the theory was first published in 2015, why has no formalization been developed in the intervening 11 years? The absence of mathematical formalization is not a "research frontier"; it is a fundamental gap that prevents the theory from making quantitative predictions.

5. How does the theory account for the variability in psychedelic experiences across individuals? If "permeability" is a single parameter tracking serotonergic activation, why do two subjects given the same dose of psilocybin in the same environment report radically different experiences? What determines which implicit content crosses the barrier?

6. The theory identifies the ESM with self-referential processing and invokes medial prefrontal cortex and temporoparietal junction as candidate substrates (line ~1072). But mPFC lesions do not abolish consciousness or self-awareness (they impair mentalizing and social cognition). How does the theory reconcile its architectural claims with the lesion evidence?

7. Can you provide a single concrete case where the four-model decomposition generates a prediction that a three-model decomposition (e.g., without the ISM as a distinct model) would not? What empirical observation would falsify the claim that four models, specifically, are the minimum?
