Dear Nautilus editors,

The COGITATE adversarial collaboration was supposed to settle the consciousness debate. After a decade of work and several million dollars, it tested Integrated Information Theory against Global Neuronal Workspace Theory and confirmed neither. The field treated this as a frustrating draw. I'd like to argue, in a 3,000-word feature, that it was actually diagnostic — both theories were asking the wrong question, and the result tells us why.

The problem is architectural. IIT, GWT, and most of their rivals ask "which neural process is conscious?" — hunting for a magic ingredient. But the brain doesn't have one. It maintains four distinct kinds of model along two dimensions: world versus self, learned versus actively simulated. The learned models live in synaptic structure and are invisible to consciousness. The simulated pair is generated in real time — that is your experience. The hard problem looks intractable only if you keep searching for the plot of a movie inside the projector's circuits.

This isn't a metaphor or a manifesto. The framework — the Four-Model Theory — is a computational taxonomy that makes specific, falsifiable predictions. The cleanest one: every general anesthetic that erases consciousness should converge on the same dynamical signature, no matter which receptor it targets. Propofol works on GABA, ketamine blocks NMDA, opioids hit their own system; if FMT is right, all of them push the cortex below the same critical threshold, and existing complexity measures (PCI, Lempel-Ziv) should detect it. That is a prediction IIT and GWT do not make and cannot easily accommodate.

The essay would walk through what COGITATE actually showed, sketch the four-model architecture in plain terms, develop the anesthesia prediction (with two further predictions on psychedelics and ego-dissolution flagged), and close on the engineering provocation: if the architecture is right, conscious machines stop being a metaphysical question and become a specification. Your readers already know the COGITATE landscape from your earlier coverage; this gives them a way out of the stalemate that isn't just a third combatant.

About me: independent researcher, master's in bioinformatics, R&D AI lead at a medical-devices company. The Four-Model Theory was first published in German in 2015, a decade before COGITATE's null result. The full paper is on Zenodo (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.18861613); the popular version, *The Simulation You Call "I"*, came out via KDP in February 2026. I'll be at the "Conscious Mind at 30" conference in Bochum in June (Chalmers, Seth, Melloni). I can deliver a polished draft within two weeks of acceptance and am open to scope or angle changes.
